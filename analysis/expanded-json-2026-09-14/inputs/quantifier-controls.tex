\section{Scope and comparison}

This supplement records the independent quantificational uses discussed in §2.3 of \textit{Determinatives as nouns in English}. It preserves the comparison table, retained attestations, and source-verification limits. The searches establish selected occurrences, without estimating their frequency or establishing an ordering of the constructions.

\textit{The Cambridge grammar of the English language} (\textit{CGEL}; \citealp{huddleston2002}) includes \mention{several} among determinatives and treats quantificational \mention{certain} and \mention{various} as marginal members. The \mention{of}-phrase with independent \mention{certain} and \mention{various} is hardly omissible. The partitive with \mention{certain} is relatively formal; that with \mention{various} is accepted by some speakers, primarily in American English \citep[392--393, 411--413]{huddleston2002}.

Table~\ref{tab:quant-controls} separates NP dependents from the surrounding construction. The argument frames are \mention{X of them}, \mention{X who arrived left}, and anaphoric \mention{X left}. The existential frames are \mention{There are X of them}, \mention{There are X who can help}, and anaphoric \mention{Are there X?} Interpret the constructed comparisons with people under discussion. Argument use also includes complementation of prepositions. The common-noun controls retain \mention{a} in \mention{a lot}.

The G and R cells retain the qualifications just stated; the common-noun partitives follow \textit{CGEL} \citep[349--350]{huddleston2002}. The relative use of \mention{many} follows \textit{CGEL}'s discussion of the human interpretation \citep[414]{huddleston2002}. The attestations below combine targeted searches in NOW and COCA with separately located examples. Expanded contexts exclude overt following nouns, predicative inversions, and mathematical \mention{multiple}. These searches establish selected occurrences; they supply no usage rates.\footnote{Searches conducted on 12--13 September 2026 in \href{https://www.english-corpora.org/now/}{NOW} and \href{https://www.english-corpora.org/coca/}{COCA}, using surface strings without part-of-speech filters. Queries, retained excerpts, and verification limits are recorded in \nolinkurl{notes/2026-09-12-quantifier-controls-corpus.json}; separate source checks are in \nolinkurl{notes/2026-09-12-quantifier-controls-sources.json}. The fourteen-cell follow-up is recorded in \nolinkurl{notes/2026-09-13-quantifier-cells.json}.}

The examples in (\ref{ex:quant-arguments}) show partitive and independent subject uses of further quantity words. The \mention{countless} partitive comes from academic prose; the \mention{multiple} partitive from a blog discussion. The final two examples have independent quantifiers as subjects, with people recoverable from context. Their occurrence requires a description even where speakers differ over their acceptability.

Existentials show a further range in (\ref{ex:quant-existentials}). In (\ref{ex:multiple-bare}), \mention{multiple} ends the question, with wedding dresses under discussion. Source locations identify the records; they don't establish each speaker's or writer's variety.

The additional existential examples in (\ref{ex:quant-more-existentials}) supply partitives with \mention{certain}, \mention{various}, and \mention{numerous}. The final two have no NP dependent: \mention{numerous} refers to benefits of a college education and \mention{countless} to investment options. These contexts establish anaphoric interpretations without an overt following nominal.

No qualifying attestation was retained for four cells: argument \mention{certain} with a relative, bare existential \mention{certain}, and existential \mention{various} with a relative or without a dependent. The bounded searches don't establish exclusion. These cells retain a different evidential status from the positive examples and from \textit{CGEL}'s stated restrictions.
