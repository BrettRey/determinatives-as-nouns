## What the sets show

**Bare *much* stays non-affirmative after *seem* and *become*, as after *be*.** Two usable lines: *it doesn't seem much* (seem-much 1) and *I didn't think it would become much* (become-much 1). In the first, *it* probably points back to a number ("equates to feet on the ground"), so it's an amount subject and bears on polarity only, not on the subject condition. The second's *it* can't be resolved from the window.

**With *too* or *a little*, *much* takes subjects denoting no amount, affirmatively, after all three verbs.** That is the frame Solt (76) stars for bare *much* (*\*John's patience is much*).

- *a little much* (69 lines): every line is affirmative. Subjects: 44 pronouns (*it*, *this*, *that*, *which*, *they* of things, one elided), 18 lexical or clausal subjects denoting no amount (5, 7, 11, 14, 15, 17, 20, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 37, 39, 57, 59, 60, 68), 4 persons (29, 31, 51, 58), and 3 amounts (16 *shipping*, i.e. its cost; 55 *a week*; 70 *$5 million for one semester*).
- *become too much* (74 lines): 33 pronoun subjects (*it*, *it all*, *everything*) and 41 lexical or clausal subjects (*the load*, *the abuse*, *the tension*, *the burden*, *holding down two jobs*). Four of the 41 are amount- or measure-like (25 *the time to run it*, 36 *the cost*, 48 *the temperature*, 50 *the workload*). No person subjects.
- *seem too/so much* (16 lines): 14 predicative, 13 of them affirmative (*Deletion in general seems too much*), one under negation (15 *may not seem too much*).
- *be too/so much* (498 lines): 475 *too*, 23 *so*. 23 are existential (*there's so much*, *there's too much*). About 30 are the person idiom *you're too much* (17, 37, 53, 68, 102, 134, 142, 158, 246, 252, 255, 257, 294, 295, 330, 336, 347, 351, 356, 362, 390, 415, 418, 419, 434, 435, 436, 455, 461, 475, 491; list from a screen plus reading, possibly incomplete). Line 119 is adverbial (*who you are so much*). Amount subjects occur (*5% was too much*, *$7.99 for an ebook is too much*) but are a minority; I didn't count them. Most subjects are entities, events, or situations: *the stress*, *the pain*, *the makeup*, *the dog bone*, *the draft regulation*. Of the eight non-existential predicative *so much* lines, six are affirmative, including *A PHONE CAN BE SO MUCH* (64), and two are negated (208, 271).

## Reading

The split matches the one *CGEL* draws for *much* in general. Bare *much* is "certainly an NPI" (p. 827, contrasting *Kim isn't much of a dancer* with *\*Kim is much of a dancer*), with a literary affirmative register (p. 826, [11i], e.g. *This means much to the American tradition*), while "much with an adverbial modifier like very, so, or too as in [iia–c] has a far wider distribution than much on its own … None of these illustrate NPIs" (p. 827). The predicative sets follow the same pattern, so the restriction on predicative *much* is the general polarity restriction on bare *much*, not a condition on the subject. Solt (76) sets polarity aside by also starring *isn't much* (the 19 September sets bear on that), and her overt-dimension licensing condition on Meas (stated after her (78)) doesn't distinguish *too much*, which makes no dimension overt and is fine with *the pain*.

*A little much* grades plain *much* the way *a little* grades plain adjectives (*CGEL* p. 549, [37viii] *I'm feeling a little disappointed*). That falls under the grading §2.2 already grants the four Q-words rather than adding a new property, but it's on the adjectival side and should be stated rather than left for a reviewer.

Untested: the *little* half. The `[be] little .` set (same date) can't be filtered because adjectival *little* swamps it. A frame that excludes the size reading is *too little, too late*.
