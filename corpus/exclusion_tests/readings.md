## Number transparency: *each of them* + plural verb (manuscript: singular *each of the people* doesn't take its number from *people*)

`each of them was/were/is/are/has/have`, plural rows saved (75 lines): 71 accepted, 4 escalated, none rejected. Plural agreement after *each of them* is attested across every register: SPOK 19, BLOG 18, WEB 17, MAG 7, NEWS 5, and ACAD. Edited cases include *Each of them have had a career that is the stuff of dreams* (USA Today, 2018), *each of them have taken deer off their Sangerville property* (Bangor Daily News, 2012), *each of them have not received injury which differed in kind* (Environmental Affairs, 1990), *each of them are very different from one another* (PBS Newshour, 1993), and a sitting president: *Each of them have very strong opinions* (ABC, 2009).

`each of the [nn2*] was/were`, the *were* row (46 lines): 37 escalated, none accepted, and the screen's hesitation was right, because in about half the academic lines the verb agrees with another head (*the factor loadings for each of the items were constrained*). The genuine cases, where *each of the Ns* is the subject, still number about sixteen: *Each of the murders were committed within sight of a body of water* (TV, 2018), *each of the crafts were Ababil models* (New Republic, 2014), *each of the participants were administered the survey packet* (School Counselor, 2007), *Each of the films were selected* (Roeper Review, 2002), *each of the smokers were well-aware* (NPR, 2000).

What it does to the claim: as a statement about usage the exclusion is false; plural agreement with a plural partitive complement is common and reaches edited and academic prose. As a statement about the grammar (*each* is singular; the plural is agreement with the nearer or notional plural) it is defensible, and that is how the manuscript should put it, with the rate. The rate, from the List counts in the same export: *each of them* + singular verb 389 (*is* 146, *has* 140, *was* 103) against plural 78 (*have* 46, *are* 23, *were* 9), so plural agreement in 17 percent of COCA tokens; with a plural partitive noun, *each of the Ns was* 151 against *were* 73 across 173 noun-specific strings, so 33%.

## Compounds with a pre-head adjective or an external determiner (manuscript: the compound construction excludes both)

`[at*] [j*] someone/somebody/something` (496 lines): 47 accepted, 359 escalated, 90 rejected. The determiner-plus-adjective shape is attested at volume: *a special someone* (282 in COCA by the List count), *a certain someone* (120), *a little something* (the bulk of the escalations, a lexicalized item the screen would not commit to). Beyond the fixed ones, the shape is productive: *an anonymous someone with very little solid evidence* (Mashable, 2017), *The responsible someone has to collect the rents* (Confrontation, 2014), *a furry something swinging indelicately from its mouth* (Analog, 2002), *a certain someone's very special birthday* (NEWS, 2019), *a big something* (MOV, 2019).

`[at*] someone/somebody/something` (494 lines): 6 accepted, 349 escalated, 139 rejected. Article plus bare compound exists in a philosophical and literary register: *a something infinite, such as God* (Style, 1997), *a something heavenly and ethereal* (newadvent.org), *a something in me was urging* (FIC, 2019), *a something blue* (FIC, 2017); much of the escalated mass is *the Something in the Water festival* and *the something else* in titles and set phrases.

What it does to the claim: the exclusion cannot stand as written. The manuscript's available move is the one CGEL makes for *a nobody* and *the little somethings*: these are conversions of the compound to a common noun, which is why they take an article, an adjective, and (as the plurals show) number. The corpus supplies the productive cases that make the conversion analysis necessary rather than a footnote, and the paper should state the diagnostics it relies on (article, prenominal adjective, plural, absence of the post-head restrictor) rather than assert the exclusion. The lines with a genitive (*a certain someone's*) are worth a sentence of their own.

Correction (Brett, 2026-09-17). A first reading of this session proposed a lexical restriction of the shift to *some-* and *no-* compounds as a diagnostic. It is wrong: *any-* compounds shift the same way. COCA: *Not anyone. And if I cld think of such an anyone, I would go out there and I would kill them myself* (TV, *Bones*, 2010); *anybody could have competed. I qualified as an anybody and was handed bib number 105* (*Skiing*, 1992). Web, supplied by Brett without URLs: *Casper pouf is part of an anywhere, everywhere furniture collection*; *If the firm is already an anywhere operation, then there may be no need for an alternative disaster recovery work site*; *an anywhere, anytime, user-centric experience*. The shift is productive across the compound construction, including the locative compounds in attributive use. The diagnostics that remain are the semantic ones: the article-taking use denotes a kind or an individual of the description the quantifier gives (*an anybody* is a person of no particular standing; *such an anyone* echoes the *anyone* of the previous sentence), quantificational force is gone, and plural inflection becomes available. The *Skiing* and *Bones* lines each show the quantifier and its nominal echo in adjacent sentences, which is the cleanest demonstration of the shift there is.

## Attributive degree-modified *numerous* (manuscript: #*so numerous mistakes*)

`so numerous [nn*]` (4 lines): none qualify. *So numerous women have already taken to the streets* and *So numerous investors can open* are discourse *so* before an NP; *said so numerous times* has *so* as the object of *said*. The exclusion holds for *so*.

`too numerous [nn*]` (4 lines): three marginal web attestations of attributive *too numerous N*: *There are too numerous inaccuracies in this article to list* (msn real estate comments, 2012), *too numerous people out there have been allowed* (blog, 2012), *this too numerous conspiracy* (a digitized older text). None in edited prose. The starred example concerns *so*; if the manuscript generalizes to degree modifiers, *too* needs a hedge.

## Degree adverbs on non-gradable determinatives (manuscript: #*very every*, #*very some*, #*very this*)

`very every/some/this` (7 lines): all rejected, correctly. *very every single day* is a disfluency, *some very some very unseemly* a stutter, *very This water layer* a transcription fault. Two are worth a footnote as jocular or converted uses rather than counterexamples: *THIS. very this.* (a web meme of emphatic agreement, 2012) and Bob Mackie's *it's very this year* (CNN, 1997), where *this year* is used as a predicative adjective-like phrase. `so every/some/this` was left at the List stage (so this 19,835, so some 1,732, so every 1,371), all discourse *so*. The exclusion holds.

## Not attested in COCA

`very a few`, `a quite few`, `no of the`: zero hits. `every [y*]` and `take every [y*]`: zero hits (the punctuation tag may not behave as the help implies; `every [v*]` covered the independent-*every* test, see `every-reading.md`). The tag-family strings `every [v*z*]`, `[v*d*]`, `[vm*]` also returned zero, which suggests the double wildcard inside a tag is not supported; that is a note for the sheet, not a finding. A zero on COCA is not an ungrammaticality judgment; the NOW fallback was not run.
