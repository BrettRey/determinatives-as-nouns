Two of the older texts (ll. 16, 43) also have *all and every of*. The Phys.Org line (l. 1) is filed as web text: it's a reader's comment in a source COCA classes as magazine. Four of the 25 partitive lines have *every* modified by *almost* or *just about* (ll. 27, 29, 55, 56), and so does one spun line (l. 37).

**Bearing.** In contemporary edited prose, partitive *every* alone is all but absent: one doubtful *Esquire* line. It occurs in unedited web text, much of it apparently non-native (l. 22 writes 5,00,000), and as slips in speech. Older texts had it freely. In usage it also follows *each and*, *any and*, and *all and*. Brett finds those unacceptable too, so they show a usage pattern, not a coordination the grammar admits. So the star on *every of the students* holds, by judgment and for edited English. Bare *every*, by contrast, has two edited attestations (`every-reading.md`). The partitive is therefore the safer starred form. Swapping in a bare starred example would put the star on the form that has the edited exceptions.

**Applied, 19 October.** §3 of the structural paper now stars CGEL's own example, \**Every of the apples was rotten* (p. 413), in place of the constructed \**every of the students*.
