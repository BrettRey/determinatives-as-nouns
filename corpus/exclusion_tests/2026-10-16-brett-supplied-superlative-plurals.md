**Bearing.** *CGEL*'s fused-head superlatives don't inflect (*the tallest*, *the elder*; *his most obstructive*, p. 416). Where a superlative converts to a count noun, it takes plural *-s* (*my closests*, *its biggests and its bests*). This is the same mark that identifies converted determinatives (*nones and somes*, `2026-10-15-brett-supplied-somes.md`). That supports the fusion paper's §4 argument that conversion leaves recognizable marks and *the some who* shows none.

**Only several.** *CGEL*: "Only occurs readily with a few, but is hardly idiomatic with several" (p. 392). The Jones line attests it in published fiction. One line doesn't refute a hedged claim. The fusion paper's §8.1 currently groups *only* with *quite* and *not* as combining "readily with *a few*, in contrast to *several*", which is stronger than *CGEL*'s wording for *only*.

**Bearing (both batches).**
- **Conversion is productive and marked.** Converted superlatives take plural *-s*, count quantifiers (*a lot of oldests*, *some other youngests*) and any determiner (*the*, *its*, *my*, *their*).
- **Writers treat it as coinage.** They often flag it with quotation marks, or model it on established *firsts*.
- **Two readings:**
  - records: *its biggests*, *baseball's youngests*, *firsts and largests*;
  - persons: *the youngests*, *my closests*, *Eldests*.
- **Fused superlatives never take *-s*.** *The youngests peeked* against fused-head *the youngest* is the contrast in the wild.
- **Relevance to the fusion paper:** this strengthens §4's claim that conversion leaves recognizable marks and that *the some who* shows none.
