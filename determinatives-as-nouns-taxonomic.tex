\begin{abstract}
% Opening question kept (Brett, 2026-10-15, with "I argue that, yes,").
Is \mention{the} a noun? I argue that, yes, English determinatives (\mention{the}, \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{few}) are a fourth subcategory of Noun beside common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns, not the separate primary category of \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language} (\textit{CGEL}). \textit{CGEL} already keeps pronouns within Noun despite their differences, as auxiliaries are kept within Verb. Comparing whole profiles, I find links to nouns running through the class. Quantity nouns like \mention{lot} and \mention{number} share partitives, number transparency, and restricted dependents with \mention{some} and \mention{many}; \mention{this} and \mention{that} inflect for number; and \mention{everyone} takes the head genitive. The adjectival profile of grade and degree modification is confined to four quantifiers. Within Noun, one secondary-use schema covers \mention{an Ophelia}, \mention{a he}, \mention{a special someone}, and \mention{in threes} with no change of category, and corpus evidence bears out its prediction for pronouns (\mention{the real me}). A separate category matches this coverage only by restating rules over two categories or by adding a feature that coincides with Noun membership. The classification holds however a grammar analyses the internal structure of these phrases.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Determinatives belong within Noun. \textit{CGEL} keeps pronouns there despite their differences from common and proper nouns in inflection and dependents, and \textcite{pullumwilson1977} keep auxiliaries within Verb; both precedents compare whole profiles, not a checklist every member must meet. On that comparison the strongest positive connection comes from quantificational common nouns: their selected complements, number transparency, restricted dependents, and degree uses connect with parts of the determinative inventory. The adjectival profile of the four gradable quantifiers deserves weight on the same terms, but it stays confined to them, while the nominal connections recur across the inventory. That recurrence is also why I decline to split the inventory between Noun and Adjective, as \textcite{vaneynde2003determiner}'s criterion would if applied to English: the gradable four share partitives, restricted dependents, and degree uses with quantificational common nouns too.

Restricted members belong through the determinative system: the articles take part in its contrasts of definiteness, quantity, and count selection, and \mention{no}/\mention{none} keeps its form-selection conditions. Dependence alone doesn't exclude a word from Noun: dependent \mention{my} is a pronoun form through its paradigm with \mention{I}, \mention{me}, and \mention{mine}. What sets determinatives apart is the kind of specialization Noun already contains. Proper nouns specialize in names and pronouns in deixis and anaphora; determinatives specialize in definiteness and quantity, and in the Det function, which \enquote{need not be realised by a determinative} \citep[358]{huddleston2002} and is already filled by genitives of common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns.

The grouping is also the more parsimonious grammar. \textit{CGEL} states the head genitive by the head noun's inflection, listing \mention{everyone's} beside \mention{Edward's}; within Noun that statement holds as written, where a separate category has to extend it to determinatives.

One secondary-use schema covers proper nouns, personal pronouns, compound determinatives, and cardinals, each carrying its own description of the individuals it identifies or quantifies over, and none changing category. Stated by that property, the schema predicts the pronoun input, which the corpus confirms. The pronoun's split fits a secondary use, not a conversion: the verb treats the NP as third person, while reflexives keep the pronoun's own person.

A grammar that keeps determinatives separate can reach the same coverage, but only by restating each of these rules over two categories or by assigning a feature whose domain coincides with Noun membership. Either way it keeps a primary boundary that none of the shared rules uses, and the profile comparison shows that boundary cutting across the connections those rules state. The difference isn't a shorter description: the feature formulation can be stated compactly. It's that the boundary then has to earn its place with generalizations of its own, and what determinatives share among themselves (definiteness, quantity, the Det function) is the kind of specialization Noun already contains.

Within Noun, coordinate rank beside pronoun fits the comparisons better than nesting: determinatives connect with pronouns through deixis, personal and non-personal contrasts, and interrogative and relative forms, and with quantificational common nouns through quantity. The classification doesn't depend on how independent determinatives are headed: it holds under fusion and under ordinary Head.
