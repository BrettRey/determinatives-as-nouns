\begin{abstract}
English determinatives (\mention{the}, \mention{some}, \mention{every}, \mention{few}, \ldots) are a fourth subcategory of Noun beside common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns, not the separate primary category of \textit{The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language}. Comparing the whole determinative inventory with the established subcategories and with adjectives, I find nominal connections recurring across it. Quantificational common nouns share partitives, number transparency, and restricted dependents with determinative quantifiers; demonstratives inflect for number; compounds take head genitives. The adjectival profile of grade and degree modification is confined to four quantifiers, and the articles and \mention{every} belong through their integration into the determinative system. Within Noun, one secondary-use schema covers proper nouns (\mention{an Ophelia}), personal pronouns (\mention{a he}, \mention{the real me}), compound determinatives (\mention{a special someone}), and cardinals (\mention{in threes}) without a change of category. Attested pronoun uses take plural inflection and adjectives, and keep the pronoun's person for reflexives, as a secondary use would and a conversion wouldn't. The classification stands whatever a grammar says about the internal structure of independent determinatives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Determinatives belong within Noun. The strongest positive comparison comes from quantificational common nouns: their selected complements, number transparency, restricted dependents, and degree uses connect with parts of the determinative inventory. The adjectival profile of the four gradable quantifiers deserves weight on the same terms, but it stays confined to them, while the nominal connections recur across the inventory. Restricted members belong through the determinative system: the articles take part in its contrasts of definiteness, quantity, and count selection, and \mention{no}/\mention{none} keeps its form-selection conditions.

The grouping lets the grammar state once what it now states piecemeal. One secondary-use schema covers proper nouns, personal pronouns, compound determinatives, and cardinals. Each carries its own description of the individuals it identifies or quantifies over, and none changes category. Stated by that property, the schema predicts the pronoun input, which the corpus confirms. The pronoun's split fits a secondary use, not a conversion: the verb treats the NP as third person, while reflexives keep the pronoun's own person.

A grammar that keeps determinatives separate can share these generalizations through a feature that tracks Noun membership.
