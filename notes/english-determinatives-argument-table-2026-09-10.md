# Arguments about English determinatives across the literature

Working comparison, 10 September 2026. Prepared for Brett Reynolds.

The main matrix covers eighteen works, including the major classification arguments, the CGEL benchmark, and closely relevant work on pronouns, compounds, numerals, and NP structure. It is split into two panels so that the arguments remain readable. A separate panel records the semantic tradition and its access limitations. This is a comparison of arguments, not a vote on which classification wins.

**External syntax** means the constructions in which an expression occurs: determiner, subject/object, adjunct, predicative complement, and so on. **Internal syntax** means the dependents it licenses and their arrangement, including the disputed structure of the larger nominal expression. These levels must be specified: the external distribution of *some people* does not by itself classify the word *some*.

**Word formation** includes affixal derivation, compounding, and conversion, distinguished below. It excludes syntactic “derivations” such as movement. **—** means no independent argument under that heading in the passages checked; it does not mean that the work contains no discussion anywhere. Ordinary entries report the source's argument. **Inference** marks an assessment made for this comparison. **Partial** marks an attribution for which the available reading is limited.

| **R21 — Reynolds 2021** | Quantify the distinctness of CGEL pronoun and determinative profiles. | Fifty-four syntactic features contribute to the reported matrix. | **PM22 — Pullum & Miller, English paper associated with 2022 work** | Defend N as head of the full nominal phrase. N-based restrictions better explain the full phrase's distribution. | Noun type constrains determiner presence/choice; the authors contrast this with auxiliary selection of verb form. | **R26 — Reynolds 2026** | Separate lexical numerals from larger syntactic numeratives; distinguish cardinal D, cardinal common/proper nouns, ordinal adjectives, and fractional nouns. | Cardinals have different distributions as quantifiers, mathematical names/labels, and common nouns. Interpolation, coordination, and ellipsis distinguish lexical compounds from phrases. Factors occur across cardinal, ordinal, and fractional structures, challenging their classification as NP-bound determiners. **Locators:** §§3–5. |

## 3. The semantic literature: included, with the reading limits visible

Semantic *determiner* often means an expression denoting a relation between a nominal restriction and a scope predicate. Its extension need not equal CGEL's lexical category: it can include complex expressions. It therefore supplies an additional kind of classification, whose relation to a lexical taxonomy needs an argument.

| Work | Relevant argument | Relation to syntax and morphology | Reading status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Barwise & Cooper 1981**, *Generalized quantifiers and natural language* | Determiner denotations are constrained by the nominal restriction: the resulting quantifier “lives on” that set. | A proposal about semantic composition and possible meanings, not by itself a demonstration of one English lexical category or of D-headed syntax. | **Original not obtained for this pass.** This specific attribution is corroborated by von Fintel & Keenan's primary discussion, which cites B&C p. 170. Other arguments, including the weak/strong distinction and existential constructions, need direct collation. |
| **Keenan & Stavi 1986**, *A semantic characterization of natural language determiners* | Conservativity characterizes the proposed relation: evaluating D(A, B) depends only on the part of B inside A. | **Inference:** whether this supports a category depends on the expressions admitted as semantic determiners and on the mapping from semantic type to lexical class. | **Original not obtained for this pass.** The conservativity attribution is checked through von Fintel & Keenan; the original paper's fuller English argument and construction inventory remain to be collated. |
| **von Fintel & Keenan 2018**, *Determiners, conservativity, witnesses* | The proposed Witness Set Constraint is equivalent to conservativity, so it cannot accommodate the nonconservative readings it was designed to admit. *Only* and certain proportional *many* readings are relevant difficulties. | *Only*'s status as a determiner is itself conditional. This makes the class's boundary part of the semantic argument; there is no inflectional or derivational diagnosis in the checked material. | **Abstract and indexed primary-text passages checked**, not a complete reading of the proof. ## 4. What the derivational column adds

**Correction following Brett's comment:** the initial comparison underrepresented derivations **from a potential D base into another category**. It concentrated on the formation of determinatives, compounds containing them, and numeral morphology. Outward derivation is already attested and includes nonnumeral bases. Treating *-ness* only as a future search target was an omission: CGEL itself discusses *nothingness* and *oneness*.

The relevant evidence concerns the **derivational rule and its input restrictions**, as well as the output category. A derived noun can supply evidence about whether its base patterns with adjectives, nouns, pronouns, or some combination. Output nounhood alone is insufficient, but that qualification must not replace examining the derivation. Existing derivatives, their synchronic analysability, and the productivity of the pattern are separate questions; established forms remain relevant even when a process no longer freely creates new words.

The following supplement distinguishes newly checked lexical evidence from arguments explicitly made in the papers. The D labels are potential analyses under the inventories being compared; dictionary labels have not been silently equated with CGEL's categories.

| Potential direction | Formation / evidence | What remains to be decided |
|---|---|---|
| **D → N** | *nothing → nothingness*. CGEL ch. 19, §5.7.2(i), explicitly uses *nothingness* as an example of *-ness* attaching to a non-adjectival base. This connects its morphological discussion with its classification of compound determinatives. | Whether the relevant sense of the base is directly D or an independently available noun. The base must be analysed in the relevant meaning, not inferred from spelling alone. |
| **D → N** | *this → thisness*. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/thisness) records the noun as a philosophical term and a translation of *haecceitas*. The segmentation as *this + -ness* is transparent. | A technical nominalization of demonstrative content, not a numeral formation. Its existence is established; the productivity and input restrictions of the pattern need comparison. |
| **D → N** | *few → fewness*; *much → muchness*. The nouns and their morphological histories are documented by [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/fewness) and [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/muchness). | How these relate synchronically to quantitative D uses, alternative adjectival analyses, and inherited formations. *Muchness* has restricted uses, which should be recorded rather than treated as absence. |
| **D → Adv** | *most → mostly*. [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/mostly) identifies *most + -ly* and an adverbial output. This is outward suffixation involving material classified with the degree determinatives in CGEL. | Which sense/category of *most* supplies the base; whether the relation favours an adjectival analysis or extends a rule beyond adjectives. It is not D-internal numeral formation. |
| **Potential D → Adj, historical** | *one → only*. [Dictionary.com](https://www.dictionary.com/browse/only) records adjectival *only* and the historical relation to *one* and *-ly*. | This is historical evidence. A modern direct D → Adj rule cannot be inferred without establishing synchronic segmentation and the category of the relevant *one*. Keep the adjective-forming and adverb-forming *-ly* patterns distinct. |
| **D-containing formation → Adj** | *every day → everyday*, with the adjective recorded by [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/everyday). CGEL ch. 19, §4.3.3 also discusses *all-time* and *no-win* among possible compound adjectives. | These establish outward **compound/dephrasal** routes, rather than direct suffixation of D. A phrasal input and an individual D base impose different requirements on an analysis. |
| **Verb-output control** | *half → halve*, documented by [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/halve). | CGEL ch. 5, §12(b) already treats fractional *half* as a noun, including predeterminer uses. Thus this is not evidence for D → V within CGEL's inventory; it matters when comparing broader determiner inventories. No unambiguous direct D → V case has been established here. |

These findings change the earlier assessment. The morphological comparison must cover **the categories determinatives can feed**, including affinities with adjective-based *-ness* and *-ly* patterns. Neither suffix is exclusively adjective-selecting: CGEL explicitly discusses non-adjectival bases for both (ch. 19, §5.7.2(i); ch. 6, §5.2(a)). The comparative question is whether the distribution of these processes supports primary D, an adjectival analysis of some members, a nominal supercategory, or a redistribution. A claim that morphology only illuminates numerals and D compounds is too narrow.

The original distinctions among the other word-formation arguments remain useful:

| Process / example | What is actually established in the checked source | What it could test for this paper |
|---|---|---|
| **Affixation to self:** *selfish, selfless* | Postal uses this to support *self* as a noun. | Keep the target straight: it cannot serve as direct affixal evidence about *the, some,* or *every*. |
| **Approximation suffix:** *thirty-odd* | CGEL explicitly calls this affixation (p. 431, n. 47). | Check the bases admitted by the process, its productivity, and whether the numerical use already establishes their category. A process confined to numerals may support only that subgroup. |
| **Numeral derivatives:** *thirteen, thirty* | CGEL distinguishes *-teen* and *-ty* derivatives from simple bases and decade–unit compounds (pp. 1716–1717). | Derivation occurs within the material classified as cardinal D. This challenges a blanket “determinatives lack derivation” claim, without showing that all determinatives share the process. |
| **Ordinal/fractional formation** | CGEL pp. 1718–1719 and Reynolds 2026 use the relation to cardinals; Reynolds makes the right-edge pattern evidence about constituency. | Separate evidence for the base category, the output category, and a phrasal boundary. These are three possible conclusions, not one. |
| **D + nominal compounds:** *someone, anything* | CGEL and PHP07 connect the D base's modifier distribution with the compound's distribution. | Compare this with ordinary noun compounds: does the special base-to-whole continuity predict something beyond the independently observed syntactic profile? |
| **Conversion:** *a nobody, a little something* | CGEL explicitly treats these as nouns converted from compound determinatives (p. 423, n. 43). | A rival noun-supercategory account can still recognize conversion between subcategories. The forms do not by themselves choose the higher category. |
| **Numerical compounds and conversion:** *thirty-something → thirty-somethings* | CGEL p. 1716, n. 55 distinguishes the compound from its converted person-denoting noun. | Useful for distinguishing input structure, output meaning, and subsequent plural inflection. |
| **Proposed reanalysis:** *once/twice/thrice* | PHP07 proposes a bound nominal *-ce*, with a historical inflection reanalysed as a lexical formant (§2.1, n. 30). | A serious comparative analysis to evaluate, not an uncontested productive suffix paradigm. |

Further **tests to investigate**, beyond the established outward derivatives above: whether *-ish* approximation extends across numerical and nonnumerical quantifiers; how widely *-ness*, *-hood*, and other suffixes accept D bases; and which cases involve direct derivation, prior conversion, or quoted/metalinguistic bases. Novel acceptable examples alone would not distinguish those possibilities.

## 5. Where the arguments directly meet

| Issue | Actual disagreement or shared ground |
|---|---|
| **Pronouns and articles** | Postal and Sommerstein argue in opposite reduction directions. Both belong in the history of rejecting a fundamental separation, even though neither passage straightforwardly supplies the present surface hierarchy. |
| **Independent use** | Palmer, Hudson, Spinillo, CGEL, PHP10, and Reynolds all give some weight to category continuity across uses. They disagree about the category preserved and the internal analysis. Continuity is shared ground, not a unique argument for this paper. |
| **Noun versus pronoun subtype** | Hudson's restricted inventory and valency account must be compared with the proposed coordinate subcategories, not described merely as an undifferentiated merger. His explicit Noun hierarchy is documented in Hudson 2010, pp. 253–254. |
| **Modifiers** | CGEL/PHP use characteristic D premodification to support a category distinction. PHP10 itself shows that the larger prohibition on adverbs modifying nouns is false. The live issue is the precise position and construction in which modification occurs. |
| **Headship versus lexical taxonomy** | Abney, Hudson, Maekawa, and Pullum–Miller disagree over structural relations. An N-headed full phrase is compatible with several lexical taxonomies; their headship arguments need an additional premise before bearing on the proposed superordinate Noun. |
| **Quantitative separation** | Reynolds 2021 supports distinguishable profiles and expressly does not settle primary category versus subcategory. Derivational reanalysis would need to add evidence, not erase that separation. |
| **English versus cross-linguistic evidence** | Van Eynde's strongest morphological redistribution argument concerns other languages. Déchaine–Wiltschko and Hudson also combine English and other-language evidence. English claims must use the English parts of those arguments. |
