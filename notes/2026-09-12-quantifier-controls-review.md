# Quantificational adjectives and the existential test

The control comparison is useful, but the proposed binary contrasts need revision. Existentials add a construction in which to test independent nominal use. They don't establish lexical nounhood by themselves, and several proposed negative controls have attested nominal uses.

## What the existential establishes

The contrast with generic *the rich* is useful when the discourse conditions are held constant. But definiteness isn't a categorical exclusion, and CGEL discusses several acceptable definite existential types beyond lists (pp. 1397–1401). Using *There's John* to support the nominal comparison requires allowing comparable discourse accommodation for the adjective examples.

Fusion also remains available in principle. CGEL allows an indefinite NP containing an ordinal adjective, *a second* (p. 416); the constructed *There was a second* is therefore an immediate comparison. This doesn't erase the difference in article-free use. It shows why the conclusion must concern the range of independent constructions rather than a general elimination of adjective fusion. All four implementations in the manuscript already give independent determinatives NP status.

Agreement strengthens the comparison with *plenty* and *a lot*, but it belongs initially to the phrase. CGEL's account has *there* inherit person–number from the displaced NP, with informal invariant *there's* as a complication (p. 242). Its *the rich* is a plural NP although *rich* lacks plural inflection (p. 418). Agreement therefore doesn't by itself identify the lexical head as a noun. Tests of number transparency should fix count and non-count antecedents explicitly.

CGEL's actual D–Adjective criteria are article incompatibility, licensing count singular NPs, and partitives (pp. 539–540). It also gives contrasts in the position of degree-modified expressions. These don't all reduce to independent noun headedness. Nor must a primary category have one exclusive diagnostic: the paper itself argues from combinations of properties. The stronger case is that a nominal subcategory captures the combination more naturally, not that separate D must treat every overlap as coincidence.

## Is there a constructional cline?

A plausible cline concerns decreasing dependence on overt material that supplies the nominal restriction. It distinguishes constructions containing an ordinary noun, independent expressions with an explicit partitive domain or restrictive relative, and independent expressions whose interpretation is recovered from context alone. Partitives and relatives belong on separate branches until we know whether one is systematically more permissive. Calling them consecutive stages would get ahead of the evidence.

| Configuration | Example or frame | What must be distinguished |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Overt noun | *numerous people* | Quantity meaning and prenominal position alone don't decide whether the expression is an adjective or determinative. |
| Explicit partitive domain | *certain of them* | The domain is supplied inside the NP; omission of the PP is a separate test. |
| Restrictive relative, no overt noun | *countless who …* | Descriptive content is supplied inside the NP. Its effect on acceptability must be tested against the same expression without it. |
| No post-head dependent | *Are there multiple?* | The quantifier stands alone, with an antecedent recoverable from discourse. |
| Conventional special interpretation | *Few would disagree* | A human interpretation may be available without a specific antecedent. This is a further lexical/constructional specialization, not simply fewer words. |

CGEL pp. 411–414 already separates simple, explicit and implicit partitive, and special-interpretation uses. Its p. 413 restrictions on *certain* and *various* give the proposed cline a specific starting point. The corpus findings show why relative-modified uses deserve their own column: *certain* has a recorded use in that frame, and *numerous* reaches it too. The comparison between these frames and the unmodified existential remains open.

A second dimension concerns the external construction: canonical subject, object, complement of a preposition, and existential displaced subject. These should be tested separately under matched discourse conditions. The existence of one nominal use doesn't establish unrestricted participation in the others. Agreement is then recorded for those NPs, rather than treated as another stage of the same cline.

The cline is therefore a hypothesis about the conditions on independent use, not an established implication scale or a historical sequence. It must also remain local to the relevant quantificational uses. Genitives and demonstratives already show that direct partitive licensing and independent headedness aren't universally nested: CGEL p. 413 excludes genitive partitives and restricts demonstrative *those* to modified ones. Requiring an article, as *a lot* does, likewise doesn't make an established common noun less nominal.

## Manuscript deployment

The strongest deployment gives the controls a substantive role in the profile argument. A single generic Adjective column would conceal the very variation that the new evidence reveals. I recommend a dedicated subsection, **Quantificational adjectives and nominal independence**, after the quantificational-common-noun comparison and before the connected adjectival profile. Its length should follow the evidence and explanation needed.

The subsection should introduce the semantic control, state the proposed ordering of independent constructions, and show a word-by-construction matrix. Rows should distinguish *many*, *several*, *various*, *certain*, *numerous*, *multiple*, and *countless*, with *plenty* and *a lot* as established common-noun comparators. Columns should distinguish explicit partitives, relative-modified argument uses, unmodified anaphoric argument uses, and existential uses with and without dependents. Attributive and predicative uses supply the baseline. Number and degree properties remain separate comparisons.

The matrix needs informative cells: attested example, CGEL description with its qualifications, or unresolved. A blank search result must not become a minus, and an isolated attestation must not become a claim of unrestricted acceptability. Illustrate the important cells in numbered examples and keep the full query record outside the running argument. Separate different senses and record varieties/registers where they matter.

The existing *so many mistakes* versus \**so numerous mistakes* contrast belongs at the start of this control discussion. It shows that the same broad quantity meaning permits different syntax. The following corpus examples then establish the more interesting result: the comparison isn't exhausted by a binary D–Adjective split. Some quantity words have documented independent uses whose range and supporting conditions need individual description.

The section should finish by saying exactly how this bears on the proposed Noun grouping. The question is how well that grouping captures the recurrent combination of nominal positions, dependents, and number behaviour, while allowing constructional restrictions at its edges. Relabelling each counterexample a D-noun on the strength of the tested property alone would make the control circular. The full profile must do the classificatory work.

Carry that conclusion into **Weighing the profiles**. Retain the existing four-subcategory table for its broader taxonomic comparison; the new construction matrix answers the different question of how quantity words enter nominal constructions. Add the existential comparison to **Functions and constructional range** with a cross-reference, and consolidate the overlapping qualifications about fusion and phrase-level agreement instead of scattering them through the new subsection.

This supersedes the earlier two-paragraph minimum. The proposed subsection and matrix make the evidence assessable and give the control a role in the central argument. A strict cline needn't be established for the section to be useful: discovering where participation is ordered and where it cross-cuts is itself the relevant comparison.

Non-occurrence in a retrieved sample remains distinct from an ungrammaticality judgment.
