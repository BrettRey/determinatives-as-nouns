**Keywords:** determinatives; nouns; lexical categories; noun phrases;
English

# The question and the alternatives {#sec:intro}

What's the categorial relationship among words such as *some*, *me*,
*apple*, and *Brett*? I argue that all four are nouns: determinatives
form a coordinate subcategory alongside common nouns, proper nouns, and
pronouns. I write the superordinate category as Noun.

I adopt the general framework of *The Cambridge grammar of the English
language* (*CGEL*; [@huddleston2002]). Unlike *CGEL*, though, I include
determinatives within Noun. The claims concern synchronic English
lexical categories; examples without a source are constructed, and
attested examples name theirs.[^2]

I use [determinative]{.smallcaps} for the category containing articles,
demonstratives, and quantifiers such as *the*, *this*, *some*, *every*,
and *many*.[^3] I reserve [determiner]{.smallcaps} for the syntactic
function within the noun phrase (NP) characteristically performed by
phrases headed by these words. This distinction separates what kind of
word *some* is from what syntactic relationships its phrase participates
in.

Compare *take some apples* with *take some*. The word *some* remains
determinative in both. Before *apples*, its phrase functions as
determiner; without *apples*, the whole expression functions as object.

In these NP comparisons, [dependent]{.smallcaps} describes a
determinative's use as determiner or internal modifier with a nominal
target; [independent]{.smallcaps} describes use without that target. An
independent expression can still have dependents of its own, as in *some
of the wine*, and rely on context for its interpretation.

Two choices are involved. The first is whether determinatives belong
within Noun. The second concerns the Head relations within independent
NPs. In *CGEL*, the determinative phrase (DP) headed by independent
*some* jointly fills determiner and Head, a [fusion of
functions]{.smallcaps} [@huddleston2002 410--412]. The alternative has
*some* head a nominal (Nom) directly, with Nom heading NP: the
[ordinary-Head analysis]{.smallcaps}.

I call the inclusion of determinatives within Noun the [D-noun
categorization]{.smallcaps}, D-noun for short. The [D-noun
analysis]{.smallcaps} proposed here combines it with ordinary
headedness. Noun membership makes ordinary NP structure a natural
treatment; that structural regularity strengthens the case for the
grouping.

Neither commitment, though, requires the other: a noun's projection, the
phrase it heads, can fill a fused function, and a grammar can keep
determinative (D) separate while assigning ordinary Head.

Both Head analyses give the whole independent expression NP status.
Their difference concerns its internal structure. In the separate-D
ordinary-Head implementation, words of category D project NP
(§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:det-uniform"}).

Lexical categories and syntactic functions cut across one another. An NP
can function as determiner, as in *[Kim's]{.underline} book*, and a
determinative-headed phrase can function as modifier, as in *the
[many]{.underline} people* [@payne2010; @pullummiller2022nps].

The DP hypothesis of @abney1987 concerns a different Head relation.
Under that hypothesis, D heads expressions such as *some apples*. I
retain noun-headed NPs and reject the DP analysis for English, following
the arguments of @pullummiller2022nps and @bruening2020nominal. Here DP
means [determinative phrase]{.smallcaps}, as in *CGEL*.

# Grounds for a broader Noun category {#sec:proposal}

The existing Noun category contains common nouns, proper nouns, and
pronouns with markedly different meanings, inflection, and dependents.
Ordinary count nouns alone are an inadequate comparison group. The
question is how determinatives connect with that broader range, and how
those connections compare with their affinities to adjectives.

Like many modern treatments of English, *CGEL* includes pronouns within
Noun on the basis of their phrases' functions, despite differences from
common and proper nouns in inflection and dependents [@huddleston2002
327--328]. The inclusion of auxiliaries within Verb supplies a further
precedent for preserving distinctive properties within a broader lexical
category [@pullumwilson1977]. These precedents motivate comparing the
whole grammatical [profile]{.smallcaps}, the functions, dependents,
forms, and meanings a category's members show, rather than requiring
every member to display each property of nouns.

Shared external functions alone don't determine lexical category. The
comparison weighs three considerations: how broadly a property occurs,
how it recurs across constructions, and how specifically it connects the
groups. These considerations apply equally to connections with nouns and
with adjectives. A pattern concentrated in a few words can be
substantial evidence; its contribution depends on its grammatical
relationships as well as its reach.

Four kinds of evidence recur in this comparison, and they establish
different things. A *CGEL* description records an established
possibility; a starred form records a judgment; a positive attestation
records an occurrence, in a register and a context; and a bounded search
with no result records absence where opportunity was frequent, which
supports an exclusion in proportion to the opportunity. The category
comparison rests on the constructions established in the first two ways
and on their recurrence across the inventory; attestations and searches
inform, or motivate a restatement of, individual exclusions.

## The connection from quantificational common nouns {#sec:quant-nouns}

Quantificational common nouns make the connection more specific than
shared quantity meanings. I use [quantifier]{.smallcaps} across lexical
categories for expressions specifying quantity. In *a lot of the
delegates* and *many of the delegates*, both heads quantify over a
[partitive domain]{.smallcaps}: the whole from which a quantity is
drawn, here the delegates. But *lot* also permits *a lot of delegates*,
whereas \**many of delegates* is excluded [@huddleston2002 349].

Agreement provides another connection. Compare *A lot of the people were
waiting* and *Some of the people were waiting*, with plural agreement,
against *A lot of the work was finished* and *Some of the work was
finished*, with singular agreement. The whole subject NP's number
depends on the complement of *of*. *CGEL* calls quantificational *lot*
[number-transparent]{.smallcaps} [@huddleston2002 349--350, 501--503].

*Each of the people* takes singular agreement by default, and ordinary
*a photograph of the people* takes it categorically, so neither is
number-transparent in *CGEL*'s sense. The contrast isn't absolute,
though. Plural agreement after *each of them* is well attested; plural
verb forms account for about a sixth of the relevant COCA query hits.
Plural agreement also occurs after *each of the* with a plural noun,
including in edited prose.[^5] That variation is agreement with the
nearer or notional plural, the pattern familiar from *a number of the
people were*, and it leaves *each* singular; with *some of the people*,
by contrast, plural agreement is the only option. Number transparency
specifically connects quantificational *lot* with number-neutral *some*.

Restricted dependents also occur on the common-noun side. *CGEL*
categorizes quantificational *plenty* as a common noun whose use resists
determination and modification; *lot* requires *a* and permits only
limited modification [@huddleston2002 349--350]. Established common
nouns thus approach the determinative profile as determinatives approach
theirs.

The connection also extends beyond argument NPs. Quantificational nouns
occur in degree modifiers such as *a great deal smaller* and *plenty big
enough* [@huddleston2002 549--550].
Section [5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:enough"}
compares these with determinative degree modifiers, including their
attributive distribution. Meaning, complementation, agreement, and
restricted dependents give the proposed grouping a specific basis within
established Noun.

Common nouns also permit a wider range of PP and clausal complements,
whereas pronouns and primary naming uses of proper nouns have much more
restricted dependents [@huddleston2002 429--430, 439--443, 517--521].
The shared partitive in *a lot/some of the wine* connects these
different complement systems.

## The connected adjectival profile {#sec:adjectival-profile}

The strongest adjectival connection also concerns a restricted group:
*few*, *many*, *much*, and *little*. They distinguish plain,
comparative, and superlative forms: *few*/*fewer*/*fewest*,
*many*/*more*/*most*, and corresponding paradigms for *little* and
*much* [@huddleston2002 393--395]. *CGEL* excepts *more* and *less*
before adjectives and adverbs, which it classes as adverbs
[@huddleston2002 395, 1123]; @reynolds2024why shows the contrasts with
*much* and *little* that the exception denies, so the paradigm holds
across their uses. Grade combines with degree-modifier selection and
comparative complementation, as in *more than ten*. The three properties
form a connected profile.

Degree *very*, *so*, and *too* select gradable heads: *very tall* and
*very few*, but \**so you*, \**so the book*, and \**too my life*. These
degree modifiers also exclude non-gradable determinatives: \**very
every*, \**very some*, and \**very this*. The associated series includes
*so few*, *too few*, *how few*, and *as few as*. These degree patterns
select *few*, *many*, *much*, and *little*, the four determinatives that
inflect for grade [@huddleston2002 393--395, 431--432].[^6]

With *a few*, modifier position distinguishes two attachment sites.
*CGEL* contrasts internal *very* in *a very few mistakes* with
peripheral *quite* in *quite a few mistakes* [@huddleston2002 p. 392,
(61)]; the reversed orders \**very a few* and \**a quite few* are
excluded. On the analysis of
§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}, *few* heads the phrase in Det
and *very* is an internal modifier within it. Both *a* and *quite* are
peripheral modifiers of the larger NP, with *quite* preceding *a*.

Approximative and focusing modifiers have a broader distribution.
Compare *almost every*, *nearly all*, *hardly any*, *practically no*,
*almost everyone*, and *almost ten*. They have counterparts in
established NP modification: *almost the whole class*, *hardly a day*,
and *only the best*. In *almost every student*, *almost* approximates
the quantity; it doesn't intensify a gradable property of *every*.

The broader approximative pattern supplies NP parallels distinct from
the four quantifiers' adjectival profile.
Section [4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}
develops an attachment analysis of that contrast, available under either
ordinary-Head taxonomy.

Overlap with adjectives likewise needs phrase levels kept distinct. Both
*the people* and *the rich* are NPs in *CGEL*, though only the former
has a noun as lexical head. In *a soccer/round ball*, a noun-headed
expression and an AdjP share modifier function; in *Jones became
president/ill*, NP and AdjP share predicative complement function.
External functions alone therefore don't establish nounhood.

*She is a beauty* ascribes a property, whereas *That is Kim* identifies
a person. *CGEL* excludes specifying *be* clauses from its
noun--adjective diagnostic because phrases from other categories occur
in them too [@huddleston2002 536].

Demonstratives also occur as predicative complements of *become*:
*religious leaders have suddenly become those who are regularly invited
to public meetings* and *the details become those that would add to his
culpability*.[^7] These NPs combine predicative use with relative
postmodification. *Become* doesn't itself distinguish ascription from
specification, and the examples are compatible with ordinary Head and
fusion.

NP grammar also distinguishes predicative and argument uses. A bare-role
NP such as *president* is licensed in *I'd like to be president*, but
requires determination in the corresponding object use *I'd like to meet
the president* [@huddleston2002 328]. Determinatives' asymmetries fit
this broader variation within Noun.

@solt2015qadjectives treats *few*, *many*, *much*, and *little* as
adjectives of quantity and gives them one semantics, as gradable
predicates of sets of degrees, across the quantificational, predicative,
attributive, differential, and adverbial uses the class shows.

*Much* and *little* take the predicative use only with an
amount-denoting subject, her *Ten gallons is not much* against \**John's
patience is much*, and that's the case usage supplies at volume: COCA's
sentence-final *be much* is mostly *it won't be much*, *pay won't be
much*, *the hurt can not be much*, beside existential *there hasn't been
much*, and NOW has existential *there is little*, all of them
independent NP uses.

With a degree modifier the subject condition lapses: *the pain and
immobility became too much to bear*, *sympathy seemed too much to hope
for*, *it seemed so little to give*, the flexibility of modified *much*
that @reynolds2024why notes and the pattern of a quantity NP in
predicative function, as in *a first-year festival seems a lot to ask of
the public* and *CGEL*'s *Kim isn't much of an actor* [@huddleston2002
395].[^8]

That unity is the semantic side of this adjectival profile, and it sits
as well with Noun as with Adjective: the same four show the properties
of nouns set out in §[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-nouns"}.

The same relation holds between distribution and category.
@reynolds2024why largely explains why *much* and *more* differ in what
they modify from their semantics and the scale structure of the heads,
without a category difference between them, following @payne2010 [61] on
why complementary distribution alone doesn't assign category.

Bare colour expressions and evaluative comparative subjects complicate a
simple distributional boundary. *CGEL*'s *Henrietta likes red shirts,
and I like blue* permits reduction licensed by coordination. The
constructed *Blue is good* raises a separate ambiguity between noun and
adjective, while the attested *Bluer is better* establishes a bare
comparative subject without settling its phrase category.[^9]

## Number, genitive marking, and reference {#sec:inflection}

Inflection supplies several connections with established nouns, though
no one contrast runs through the whole category. Common nouns typically
distinguish singular and plural; proper nouns permit number inflection
in restricted uses such as *the Smiths* [@huddleston2002 521].
Demonstratives likewise distinguish *this*/*these* and *that*/*those*
[@huddleston2002 373]. These are contrasts between forms of a lexeme,
unlike the fixed number restrictions of *each* and *several*.

Number needn't be inflectional wherever it matters. *CGEL* treats
singular and plural *you* as distinct lexemes, differentiated overtly
only by the reflexive forms *yourself* and *yourselves* [@huddleston2002
486]. Number-neutral *some* has no corresponding singular--plural
alternation. The demonstratives supply positive morphological evidence;
the absence of that alternation elsewhere doesn't distinguish
determinatives from pronouns as whole categories.

The demonstrative number contrasts remain visible in independent uses.
Generic human *the rich*, by contrast, is a plural NP whose adjective
lacks number inflection and still takes adverb modifiers: *the very
rich* [@huddleston2002 418]. The difference concerns overt paradigms,
rather than the mere possession of a number value by the whole NP.

Case gives a further partial connection. Genitives occur across the
proposed subcategories: *dog's*, *Kim's*, *my*, and *someone's*.
Personal pronouns have fuller case paradigms. Most determinatives lack
case inflection. The compounds' genitives belong to the NP: *CGEL* lists
*everyone's* beside *Edward's* among head genitives, marked by "the
inflection of the head noun", and *everyone else's* and *somebody
local's* beside *the King of England's* among phrasal genitives, with
the suffix after the postmodifier [@huddleston2002 479]. On *CGEL*'s
categorization the head of *everyone's* is a determinative, so that
definition is stretched at this point; within Noun it isn't. This
supports the compounds' affinity with nouns more directly than the
category's membership as a whole.

Simple forms such as \**many's* and \**some's* resist genitive marking.
*CGEL* suggests that fusion supplies a reason: the form has to meet the
case requirements of both Det and Head [@huddleston2002 420]. Its
analogy with case matching in fused relatives [@huddleston2002
1073--1074] motivates that suggestion, but the restriction still depends
on the case conditions assigned to the determinative use. Genitive NPs
themselves freely fill Det.

The ordinary-Head account states the restriction on these forms
lexically. Noun membership doesn't guarantee a genitive paradigm:
reflexive pronouns likewise lack genitive forms [@huddleston2002 426].
That precedent establishes compatibility with Noun, without explaining
the determinatives' particular restriction.

Meaning and reference vary within Noun. *Apple* supplies descriptive
content; *Kim*, in its primary naming use, identifies through a name;
*she* depends on context [@huddleston2002 425--428, 516--521]. Many
determinatives also have pro-form uses. Determinative meanings range
from deictic *this* to quantitative *many* and universal *every*
[@huddleston2002 358--360, 370--405]. Deixis connects demonstratives
with pronouns, while quantity connects determinatives with common nouns
such as *number* and *majority*.

Semantic affinities contribute to the comparison without settling its
boundaries: *every* quantifies over a nominal restriction, while *Kim*
identifies an individual.

Pro-form gender [@reynolds2025proformgender] connects these ways of
referring. Descriptions such as *the woman* and names such as *Kim*
identify referents whose construal bears on pro-form choice. Pronouns
and determinatives express contrasts between personal *she*/*somebody*
and non-personal *it*/*something*. Relative *who*/*which* supplies
another contrast.[^10]

Interrogative and relative properties are distinct from functions such
as object. An interrogative object may be headed by a pronoun (*who*) or
a common noun (*which book*); the latter obtains its interrogative
property from a dependent.

Inflection and reference provide partial connections, with different
reach. Demonstrative number directly compares forms of determinative
lexemes with the number paradigms of nouns; compound genitives follow
the head and phrasal pattern of noun-headed NPs. The *no*/*none*
alternation concerns form selection, discussed with restricted
membership in §[3.3](#sec:form-selection){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:form-selection"}.

## Functions and constructional range {#sec:independent}

NPs serve as subjects, objects, and complements of prepositions;
independent determinative expressions share these functions.

Independent use is widespread within the determinative inventory. *CGEL*
discusses such uses for demonstratives and quantifiers including *some*,
*all*, *both*, *many*, *few*, *several*, *each*, *either*, *neither*,
*much*, and *enough*, while recording lexical restrictions and the
separate forms *no*/*none* [@huddleston2002 371--372, 410--424]. The
generalization concerns the availability of independent constructions
across a lexical category, not unrestricted acceptability in every
sentence frame.[^11]

With Kim and a group of people under discussion, compare the bracketed
NPs in ([\[ex:external\]](#ex:external){reference-type="ref"
reference="ex:external"}).

NPs containing fused-head adjective phrases (AdjPs) fill the same
positions. *The rich* and *the poor* are NP arguments, and comparative
and superlative adjectives head expressions without a generic human
interpretation: *CGEL*'s *the most important of her criticisms* is an NP
containing a partitive *of*-phrase [@huddleston2002 332--333, 416--423].

Independent *some* can form a one-word NP, whereas an NP with *rich* as
fused Head requires the definite article *the* on the generic human
reading [@huddleston2002 417--418]. Singular count common nouns also
need determination in argument NPs.

With possible solutions under discussion, *There are several* places the
independent quantifier in displaced-subject position; dummy *there* is
the subject [@huddleston2002 1391--1393]. Attested *there is little* and
*there hasn't been much* place *little* and *much* there too
(§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}).
Section [2.5](#sec:quant-controls){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-controls"} tests the contrast with quantificational
adjectives.

Determiner function also cuts across the existing noun subcategories. In
*my preferences*, *Kim's preferences*, and *people's preferences*, the
determiner is an NP ultimately headed by a pronoun, a proper noun, and a
common noun respectively [@huddleston2002 354--355, 470--471].[^12]

The compound determinative *someone*, following the categorization of
@Payne2007 [§1.3], participates in *someone's preferences*. Plain-case
NPs also fill Det: *what size* in *what size shoes*, *that size* in
*that size shoes*, and *Sunday* in *Sunday morning* [@huddleston2002
355, 357].

Determiner remains the characteristic function of determinative phrases.
This specialization could support a separate primary category. Its tasks
are themselves closely connected with how nouns refer: Det marks
definiteness and often contributes quantification [@huddleston2002
354--359]. The existing NP determiners supply a comparison with nouns.
In *Kim's preferences*, the embedded NP supplies an identifying anchor
through its own referent; *Sunday* and *what size* specify a day or a
dimension. Determinatives' deictic and quantitative specifications fit
that pattern. I take that fit to support treating their specialization
in Det as a distinction within Noun.

Preposition phrases (PPs) remain a restricted alternative, as in *up to
twenty minutes* and *between fifty and sixty tanks* [@huddleston2002
357, 433]. Number, countability, and other selectional conditions
distinguish the determining expressions.

Compare the modifiers in *dog houses*, *Canada Day*, *the manager
herself*, and *the few people*. Emphatic *herself* also functions as a
clause adjunct in *The manager detected the error herself*
[@huddleston2002 1496--1497]. Temporal NPs such as *that day* and
*Sunday* supply adjuncts, as does degree *enough* in *I hadn't prepared
enough* (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:enough"}).

Common nouns freely take integrated relatives, as in *people who came*;
personal pronouns permit a restricted range, including *we who have read
the report* [@huddleston2002 430]. Determinative examples include *few
who come ever leave*, *those who came*, *that which remains*, and
*something that you need to know*.

Compounds also permit *anyone who asks* and *everything that matters*.
With books under discussion, compare *some that I saw* and *two that I
have seen*. *CGEL* describes relative postmodification with
demonstratives and compounds [@huddleston2002 414, 422--423].

Compound determinatives also take post-head adjectives, as in *I need
something reliable and good looking*.[^13] The position and
interpretation of these modifiers have specialized conditions
(§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:compounds"}).

## Quantificational adjectives and independent use {#sec:quant-controls}

*Numerous people* and *many people* both quantify, but their syntax
differs. Degree modification exposes a contrast: *so many mistakes* is
possible, while \**so numerous mistakes* is excluded [@huddleston2002
539--540]. Quantity meaning and prenominal position provide a semantic
control for the comparison with nouns. Independent use tests whether
their further distributions sustain the distinction.

*CGEL*'s boundary cuts through this semantic range. It includes
*several* among determinatives and treats quantificational *certain* and
*various* as marginal members, partly on the evidence of partitives.
Their *of*-phrases are hardly omissible, with speaker and register
restrictions on the partitive uses [@huddleston2002 392--393, 411--413].
Predicative *Its advantages are several* and *Their enemies were many*
provide further overlap, though the latter is uncommon and formal
[@huddleston2002 392, 395--396].

Independent uses extend to *numerous*, *multiple*, and *countless*.
Attested examples include subject *numerous were injured*, anaphoric
*are there multiple?*, and *paving the way for countless who followed
him*, where a relative postmodifies the complement of *for*.[^14] These
selected occurrences establish overlap, without establishing equal
acceptability or frequency. Assigning every independent quantity word to
determinative would make independent use a circular category diagnostic.

Existentials admit fused-head AdjPs too. Adjective fusion permits
indefinite ordinal *a second* [@huddleston2002 416], as in *There was a
second*. Plural agreement likewise occurs with adjective-containing *the
rich*, whose adjective lacks number inflection [@huddleston2002 418].
The number transparency of *some* and *a lot* in
§[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-nouns"} supplies a more specific comparison, with
explicit count and non-count domains.

The comparisons with nouns and with adjectives each involve connected
properties. On the noun side, quantity combines with selected
complements, number transparency, and restricted dependents. On the
adjective side, grade combines with degree modifiers and comparative
complements. The four gradable quantifiers' limited number isn't itself
a reason to discount their adjectival pattern.

@vaneynde2003determiner argues from Italian and Dutch that determiners
form no category of their own: those that inflect and agree like
prenominal adjectives are adjectives, and genitives and non-agreeing
pronouns are nouns. The criterion is inflection and agreement shared
with prenominal adjectives, and English has no agreement to share, since
its adjectives don't agree.

The inflection English does have points two ways, demonstrative number
toward nouns and grade toward adjectives
(§§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}--[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:inflection"}), and his criterion, applied to English by
extrapolation (he applies it to Italian and Dutch determiners; his
English examples don't test it on determiners), would make the gradable
four adjectives and the rest nouns. That's the split I decline here,
because the four also share partitive patterns, restricted dependents,
and degree uses with quantificational common nouns.

His own treatment keeps determiner status apart from lexical category,
as a marking value a functor contributes to the phrase it forms with the
nominal it selects, so that an adjective and a pronoun can both be
determiners without sharing a category; the question here is the
different one of what category a word has when it heads the NP itself.
Appendix [8](#sec:historical){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:historical"} lists the alternative beside the others.

The connections with nouns recur across more of the determinative
system, in more constructions and for more lexeme groups, as the claim
register records lexeme by lexeme. Partitives link *many* with *some*;
relative postmodification links *few* with demonstratives and compounds.
Determination and reference connect determinatives with genitive NPs and
pronouns, while demonstrative number supplies a morphological parallel.
These links recur across constructions whose conditions were specified
separately. The gradable quantifiers participate in this wider range
alongside their adjectival pattern.

I favour Noun membership because it brings the words with these
recurring properties of nouns into one category. Grade and degree
selection still characterize a narrower subgroup. Separate D can
describe the same distributions, but places a primary boundary across
those connections. Determinative retains its specialization in
determination and its distinctive dependent patterns within Noun, just
as pronouns retain their differences from common nouns.

## Four separable questions {#sec:four-questions}

Four questions about these words are separable, and the comparison above
answers one of them. The lexical category is the question of this
section: Noun. The Head analysis, ordinary Head or fusion, is the
question of §[4](#sec:evidence){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:evidence"}, and a grammar can answer it either way for
either category (Table [1](#tab:accounts){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:accounts"}).

The semantic type is a third. The unified semantics of *few*, *many*,
*much*, and *little* is compatible with either Noun or Adjective
(§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}). The construction is a fourth:
*such* and *what* lexically select an *a*-NP, outside the Big Mess
construction (*how long a bridge*;
§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

The broader Noun grouping still needs an account of restricted members.
Their inclusion depends on how they participate in the determinative
system.

# Restricted members and the scope of the grouping {#sec:articles}

The category-level argument draws on constructions that many
determinatives enter. Articles lack ordinary independent argument uses,
so their inclusion needs a further argument: they belong to the
determinative system whose broader profile supports Noun membership. The
*no*/*none* paradigm raises a related question about restrictions on
forms.

## Articles within the determinative system {#sec:article-membership}

Why categorize the articles *the* and *a* as nouns if they can't form
independent argument NPs? *Every* is restricted too, while *no* has the
distinct independent form *none* [@huddleston2002 371--372, 379,
410--412].

*CGEL*'s treatment of *my* supplies a precedent for a restricted noun
form [@huddleston2002 470--471].[^15] Dependent *my* and independent
*mine* belong to one pronoun paradigm. *My* remains a noun even though,
in the ordinary NP construction, it requires a following nominal. The
articles lack an independent argument form; their positive evidence is
integration into determinative, and their nounhood depends on the
category-level argument. The inference has two stages. The first places
the articles within the determinative category: they fill the Det
position the unrestricted members fill, take part in its contrasts of
definiteness, quantity, and count selection, and sit inside the
determinative cluster of the 2021 matrix [@reynolds2021 9--10]. The
second places that category within Noun by the profile of
§[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"}. The
criterion for a restricted member is the first stage; nothing in it asks
the articles for a use they lack.

The articles participate in the determinative system of definiteness,
quantity, and count restrictions. In *the/a/this/every book*, they
occupy the same Det position and contribute to the interpretation of the
NP. *The* permits singular, plural, and non-count targets; *a* selects a
singular count target and contributes individuation. Their connections
extend beyond shared position, although neither has the full
determinative profile [@huddleston2002 368--373].

*The* also functions as a degree modifier. In *the bigger the better*,
it modifies comparative AdjPs, as other determinatives do in *much
bigger* or *no better* [@huddleston2002 1131--1132, 1135--1136]. This
supplies a connection outside ordinary Det function while preserving its
exclusion from independent argument use.

*A* also has a restricted peripheral modifier use. In *a few mistakes*,
*few* heads the NP in Det, while *a* modifies the larger NP headed by
*mistakes*. In independent *a few*, *a* modifies the NP headed by *few*.
The singular-count target restriction belongs to *a*'s Det use; its
peripheral use selects a quantificational construction.
Section [3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"} develops this analysis and
contrasts it with predeterminer modification in *many a*.

@spinillo2004reconceptualising [153--158] (earlier
[@spinillo2000determiners]) instead retains *the*, *a*, and *every* as
an expanded article category and redistributes other determinatives
among adjectives and pronouns [@spinillo2004reconceptualising 194--195].
Her grounds include dependence on a following nominal, lack of
predicative and partitive uses, and limited descriptive content. She
also recognizes differences within the article trio.

Category membership doesn't remove restrictions on use. *Every apple* is
grammatical, and *every* normally requires a following nominal, unlike
independent *each* or *some*. But the saved NOW lines contain anaphoric
*every* without a following nominal in published prose, including two
cases confirmed against their publication pages.[^16] These occurrences
defeat a claim of complete absence, while leaving their productivity and
status in standard usage unsettled. I treat dependence as the ordinary
pattern and limit the fragment to that pattern.

*Every* connects with independent *each* through universal
quantification and singular count selection. It also permits
*almost*/*nearly* and occurs after genitives in *her every move*.
@spinillo2004reconceptualising [156--158] recognizes these differences
from *the* and *a*, as well as the articles' greater phonological
dependence.

The ordinary dependence of the articles and *every*, and the exclusions
of predicative and partitive uses, concern occurrence without a
following nominal; they aren't three separate reasons for a primary
category. Little descriptive content extends well beyond the trio.
Meanwhile, *a*'s quantificational constructions and *every*'s modifier
conditions cross the proposed article boundary. I retain the restricted
members within determinative because these connections preserve a
broader system without granting unrestricted use to any member.

@spinillo2004reconceptualising [140--144] draws an analogy with verbs
and prepositions whose complements can be omitted. For demonstratives
and quantifiers such as *some* and *all*, occurrence before a nominal
(*some teachers*) alternates with occurrence without one (*some left*).
The analogy supports category continuity, but leaves the category to be
established: *CGEL*, Hudson, and the D-noun analysis can all preserve
it.

The argument is synchronic: the development of definite articles from
demonstratives and of articles expressing cardinality from numerals
[@lyons1999 331--336] bears on content and use, not on the
categorization.

## Reanalysing complex determinatives {#sec:complex-determinatives}

*CGEL* lists *a few*, *a little*, and *a great many* as complex
determinatives while recording that *a few* "admits *very*, *good*, and
*fair*" and calling the internal structure "sui generis"
[@huddleston2002 392, 394]. Its case against a complex quantifier *a
number of* appeals to the potential for replacement and expansion
[@huddleston2002 351--352]. Expansion is available here too: *a good
few*, *a fair few*, and *a select few* beside *a very few*.[^17]

*A little* and *a great many* have the same peripheral *a*. Before
another nominal, *little* and *many* head the determining phrases;
independently, they head the NPs that *a* modifies. The modifier in *a
great many* is obligatory [@huddleston2002 394]. Definite *the few
mistakes* retains Det *the* and internal modifier *few* [@huddleston2002
392]. The functions differ from those in *a few mistakes*.

The combination also has its own polarity: bare *few* and *little* are
negative, whereas *a few* and *a little* are positive [@huddleston2002
393--394]. Peripheral *a* licenses a positive quantificational
interpretation in these constructions. Polarity depends on the
combination, not simply on the position occupied by *few* or *little*.
This interpretation remains a convention of the construction under the
proposed syntactic decomposition.

Conventional polarity behaviour doesn't force lexical unity: the
syntactically decomposable NP *a bit* has a minimizer interpretation in
*They didn't enjoy it a bit* [@huddleston2002 549--550, 725]. Here *a
bit* is licensed by negation rather than itself negative
[@huddleston2002 822--823]; the parallel with *a few* is that a
conventional interpretation can belong to a phrase with internal
syntactic structure.

Independent *a few* takes plural agreement [@huddleston2002 392]. That
follows from plural *few*; peripheral *a* contributes no singular-count
requirement. Its distribution remains restricted: *several* can be a
modifier after a definite determiner (*its several advantages*)
[@huddleston2002 392] but excludes peripheral *a*. The permitted
internal modifiers likewise depend on the quantifier and the
construction.

*CGEL* records that *quite*, *not*, and *only* combine readily with *a
few*, in contrast to *several* [@huddleston2002 392]. I retain those
combinations as conditions on peripheral modification, including the
order *quite a few*. The lower bound of three for *a few*
[@huddleston2002 392] remains a convention of the combination; *CGEL*
itself declines to let semantic motivation alone establish a complex
unit [@huddleston2002 621].

*Such* and exclamative *what* enter the same reanalysis from the
adjective side. *CGEL* classes both as adjectives, exclamative *what*
because it "fails all of the determinative criteria (a)--(c)": it
combines with *a*, licenses no count singular head without it, and has
no partitive use [@huddleston2002 539--540]. The first two criteria test
Det function, and in *such a mess* and *what a mess* the words aren't in
Det.

*CGEL*'s inventory of predeterminer modifiers includes *all*, *both*,
fractions, multipliers, "a small range of adjectives (e.g. *such* in
\[ic\])", and AdjPs such as *how large a piece*, and "a predeterminer
modifier normally precedes a determiner" [@huddleston2002 331]. In
*CGEL* itself, then, *such a mess* has *a* in Det and *such* as
predeterminer modifier, and only the category of *such* is at issue.

The same structure covers *what a mess* and *many a man*, whose *many a*
*CGEL* treats as a "syntactically inert" complex determinative
[@huddleston2002 394]. Each inertness fact follows from the function.
Nothing intervenes between the word and *a* (\**what long a speech*)
because a predeterminer sits against the determiner of its NP, as in
*all the books* and *half a cake*, and the count singular head and
singular agreement of *many a man has* come from *a* and *man*.

Neither *many a* nor *what a* has an independent use: predeterminer
*many* and *what* require a following *a*-NP. With peripheral *a*, *few*
and *many* retain their independent uses as well as their Det uses
before another nominal [@huddleston2002 392, 394]. That *few* can't
replace *many* (\**few a man*) is lexical, as admission to predeterminer
function is anyway (\**most the books*).

So *many a* stands to *many* as *such a* to *such* and *what a* to
*what*. Each word has Det function with plural or non-count heads (*many
men*, *such promise*, *what fools*) and predeterminer function with an
indefinite-article NP, where the modifier is an NP, as *CGEL*'s
fractions are [@huddleston2002 385 n. 25], or an AdjP (*how large a
piece*). Neither degree *such* nor exclamative *what* has grade or
degree modification (\**sucher*, \**a very such mess*), and exclamative
*what* has no predicative use.

@VanEynde_2007_BigMess [§5] draws the same line from the other side:
*such a* and *what a* aren't instances of the Big Mess construction
(*how long a bridge*), since *what* and *such* "are invariably lexical"
and "lexically select a nominal which is either unmarked or introduced
by the indefinite article".

I treat exclamative *what* and both degree and kind *such* as
determinatives in the NP constructions discussed here. The two readings
of *such* share *such a person* and *such people*; kind *such* also
functions as an internal modifier in *many such problems*, as
determinatives such as *few* and cardinals can. *CGEL* treats both
ordinary readings as adjectives, reserving determinative status for
legal-register uses with a bare count singular [@huddleston2002 435 and
n. 52]. Their reclassification here preserves the distinctions among
Det, predeterminer, and internal modifier functions.

This extension concerns the NP uses just compared; I leave the
categorization of predicative *such* and *such ... that* constructions
open.

With peripheral *a* in *a few* and predeterminer *many* in *many a*,
none of the four remains a complex determinative in this account. Of
*CGEL*'s other candidates, cardinals above a hundred are the subject of
@reynolds2026numerals, and I leave aside *a certain*, which it adds
tentatively [@huddleston2002 393].

## Dependent and independent forms {#sec:form-selection}

*No*/*none* illustrates a restriction on forms within a paradigm. *CGEL*
treats them as inflectional forms of one determinative: dependent *no
students* contrasts with independent *none*, while both permit *almost*
[@huddleston2002 389--390]. The partitive *none of the students*
requires the independent form; \**no of the students* is excluded. Form
selection remains necessary, just as with *my*/*mine*.

The broader grouping preserves two distinctions: between members'
permitted constructions, and between the forms selected within a
paradigm. Whether independent expressions have ordinary Head or fusion
remains a separate question.

# Ordinary Head, fusion, and modifier attachment {#sec:evidence}

The D-noun analysis pairs the proposed categorization with ordinary
Head. This section compares that treatment with fusion in independent
*some*, *some of the wine*, and *the lucky few*. Ordinary headedness
gives the determinative Head alone; fusion combines Head with a
dependent function.

## The basic Head relations {#sec:head-relations}

A [nominal]{.smallcaps} (Nom) contains a head and its internal
dependents, excluding an external determiner. In *some apples*, the word
*apples* heads Nom, and that Nom heads NP. The same arrangement permits
an internal modifier, as in *some red apples*, without making the
determining phrase part of the Nom.

Head is the function of an immediate constituent within a phrase. The
[ultimate lexical head]{.smallcaps} is the word reached by following
Head relations through the intervening phrase layers. Thus *apples* is
the ultimate lexical head of *some apples*, although the NP's immediate
Head is Nom. Both accounts also have *some* as the ultimate lexical head
of independent *some*.

A standard tree has unique parentage: each constituent below the root
has one parent. Fusion allows branches to converge on a shared
constituent with two parents. In the ordinary-Head analysis, the
determinative's projection needs no such join.
Figure [2](#fig:some){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:some"}
compares the structures.[^18]

Under the D-noun analysis, a determinative can head this Nom--NP
structure too. Figure [2](#fig:some){reference-type="ref"

*take some*. The dependent expression still has *apples* as its ultimate
head. Independent *some* has ordinary Head under D-noun; in *CGEL*, its
DP jointly fills Det of NP and Head of Nom.

## Syntactic completeness and contextual interpretation {#sec:saturation}

With a group of people under discussion, *Some left*, *Many came*, and
*All agree* illustrate [syntactic completeness]{.smallcaps}: an argument
expression can be complete without another overt head or determiner.
Bare *few* shows the same with no group under discussion: *few would
dare underestimate Aaron Finch's men* and *few have had the opportunity
to digest its contents*.[^19] In *I'll take some*, the relevant
substance or set may still be supplied by discourse or the situation.
Ordinary pronouns also depend on context.

Generalizing expressions such as *Many are called, few are chosen* and
*Enough is enough* need no previously uttered common-noun phrase.
Interpretation can instead depend on the situation or a generic
restriction. Generic pronoun *one*, as in *One shouldn't judge*,
provides a parallel without a required overt antecedent.

The comparison with ordinary pronouns and proper nouns is structural as
well as interpretive. In *She left* and *Kim left*, *CGEL* permits an NP
headed by a nominal with no determiner. Applying that structure to *Some
left* preserves the same division between a complete NP and its
context-dependent reference.

Fusion represents the same complete expression differently: in
@Payne2007, one constituent jointly realizes Head and a dependent
function, subject to structural and adjacency conditions. Both analyses
keep an overt determinative and can express the restriction supplied by
context; §[5.4](#sec:costs){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:costs"}
compares them on the structures they share.

## Partitives and externally determined expressions {#sec:modification}

In *some of the wine*, *some* specifies a quantity drawn from an
identifiable amount of wine. The NP *the wine* denotes the partitive
domain and is complement of *of*. A pronoun-headed NP or independent
genitive NP can express the domain too: *many of them*, referring to
previously mentioned people, or *some of Kim's*, referring to Kim's
apples.

Compare where the common noun occurs in *some apples* and *some of the
wine*. In the partitive, *wine* is embedded inside the *of*-phrase, so
it can't be the ultimate lexical head of the whole NP. The Head
relations in both analyses lead instead to *some*.

by *some*, a DP in its sense, fills Det--Head. Both analyses let the

## Modifier selection and attachment {#sec:payne}

The selection and ordering contrast in
§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:adjectival-profile"} guides the attachment analysis.
Approximative and focusing modifiers have established NP parallels; the
degree series selects the four gradable quantifiers. Both ordinary-Head
accounts can represent that difference by distinguishing NP-peripheral
modification from modification inside Nom.

Peripheral modifiers attach outside an NP's internal dependents. Compare
*only you*, *for almost my entire life*, and *Usually a careful driver,
Anne found her mind wandering*. The modifiers precede any determiner
belonging to the NP they modify. *CGEL* assigns them NP-level attachment
[@huddleston2002 430--431]. Focusing *even* and *just*, and
approximatives such as *nearly*, *hardly*, *virtually*, and
*practically*, likewise have NP uses.

In *almost every experienced teacher*, *almost* belongs with *every*,
while *experienced* modifies *teacher*.
Figure [5](#fig:every){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:every"}
places *almost* at the periphery of the NP headed by *every*. That
smaller NP functions as Det in the larger NP, whose ultimate head is
*teacher*.

@payne2010 [40--42] defend keeping *few*, *any*, and related forms in
one lexical category across dependent and independent uses. In *hardly
any money* and independent *hardly any*, *hardly* remains an adverb and
*any* retains its category. The D-noun analysis preserves that
continuity. It also preserves their contrast between adverbial
premodification, as in *almost anybody*, and adjectival
postmodification, as in *nothing absolute* [@payne2010 41--42].

The proposed analysis gives adverbs such as *very* and *too* internal
attachment in dependent and independent uses alike. In *the very few
people*, *very* modifies the Nom headed by *few*; the resulting *very
few* NP functions as Mod in the larger NP. In *the very few who
objected*, *few* remains ordinary Head and *very* remains internal. The
degree relation is unchanged, and one internal condition covers both
constructions. The same analysis applies to *the very many*.

NP degree modifiers provide a further connection. Determinatives permit
determinative premodifiers such as *this* in *this much*
[@huddleston2002 393]. Comparative determinatives also take established
NP modifiers: *a lot* in *a lot fewer*, alongside *CGEL*'s *a lot more
than fifty* [@huddleston2002 432]. These modify the quantity expressed
by the head. Under the proposed analysis, they're internal NP modifiers
within its NP projection.

@payne2010 [42--47] establish that adverbs can postmodify common nouns,
as in their *the weather recently* (p. 47). They also note one adverb
that appears to premodify a noun, *almost* before *textbook* in *an
almost textbook case* [@payne2010 p. 75, n. 3]. Adverbial modification
therefore isn't categorically excluded from Noun, even internally. Its
broader availability with determinatives remains a difference of
distribution within the proposed category.

Approximatives also need internal attachment where they follow an
external determiner on an independent cardinal: *the almost thirty who
came*. The internal AdvP conditions at issue are thus the degree series
on *few*, *many*, *much*, and *little* across dependent and independent
uses, and approximatives on externally determined independent cardinals.

## Independent genitives and retained fusion {#sec:genitive-head}

*CGEL* also uses fusion with nouns. Anaphoric *mine*, understood as 'my
car', combines the possessive relation with an understood nominal
description and receives a fused analysis. Predicative *mine*, as in
*it's mine*, can instead mean 'belongs to me': it expresses the relation
directly, without an understood nominal head [@huddleston2002 410--411,
469--471]. A suitable context may permit either reading.

The proposed analysis gives *mine* ordinary Head in both anaphoric and
predicative uses. Its genitive form expresses a relation to the speaker;
context can supply the understood nominal description, as it can with
independent *few*. The *my*/*mine* alternation remains a form-selection
condition, parallel to *no*/*none*. This extends ordinary headedness to
an existing noun subcategory.

The genitive's person identifies the possessor; the independent NP's
agreement follows the possessed entity or entities. Compare *Mine is
ready* ('my contribution') and *Mine are ready* ('my slides'). Ordinary
Head needs a constructional agreement condition.

Anaphoric genitives also need a condition on complements. *CGEL*
contrasts the following NPs [@huddleston2002 420--421, (36ii), (37iib)]:
[]{#ex:genitive-complements label="ex:genitive-complements"} *Alice's
performance of the Schubert and* \[*Helen's of the Rachmaninov*\]
*Alice's attempt to play the Schubert and* \[*Helen's the Rachmaninov*\]
In the first, *Helen's* is understood as Helen's performance, and the
following *of*-PP has the complement relation licensed by *performance*.
The second can't omit *attempt to play* while retaining the bare object
of *play*. The grammatical NP also occurs outside coordination, after
*immediately after*, where clausal gapping is excluded [@huddleston2002
420--421, (37i)].

I allow the anaphoric genitive construction to recover the antecedent's
nominal description and its complement frame, without inheriting
complements of verbs embedded within it. The recovered frame supplements
the overt head's dependent permissions. This condition, like the
agreement condition, is an additional commitment of the ordinary-Head
extension to genitives. Functional fusion marks the recovered dependent
relation, but also requires an account of which complements that
relation licenses.

*CGEL* also considers ordinary Head for independent *many*, with no
change of category, but rejects this as a general replacement for fusion
[@huddleston2002 421]. Its counterexamples are *I prefer cotton shirts
to* \[*nylon*\] and *I earn three times the amount Bill does, and Mary
earns* \[*double*\]. Both words are nouns in its account, but *nylon* is
understood as a modifier of *shirts*, and *double* as a predeterminer
multiplier.

I retain fusion for those constructions. The proposal concerns the
determinative constructions compared here, with a further extension to
independent genitives. Its advantage comes from their shared structure;
nounhood alone doesn't establish ordinary Head in every construction.

Ordinary Head gives the independent determinatives one Head chain while
preserving their distinct modifier conditions. Both taxonomies can use
that structure; §[5](#sec:economy){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:economy"} asks whether Noun membership contributes
further economy.

(§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"

reference="sec:article-membership"} fall outside this fragment.

Target restrictions apply to the nominal being determined or modified,
including a nominal headed by an independent determinative. Thus *the*
permits plural *few* in *the few*. Determinative-headed phrases also
bear number and count properties: *few* is plural count, while *some*
takes its interpretation from the construction and domain. The modifier
*this* in *this much* expresses degree; its permission doesn't follow
from demonstrative Det selection.

@hudson2004determiners [7--8] distinguishes dependency from external
headedness. He argues that determiner and common noun depend on each
other, although only one connects the phrase to its surroundings. The
temporal-adjunct evidence he takes from @vanlangendonck1994 supports
common-noun headedness: *I saw him that day* is possible, whereas \**I
saw him that point in time* isn't, despite the similar temporal meanings
[@hudson2004determiners 10--12]. The lexical noun matters, as well as
the determiner restrictions.

Reciprocal selection is compatible with this headedness. A singular
count noun normally requires determination, while *every* normally
requires another nominal and *some* doesn't. The noun can remain Head
while both constituents impose conditions.

In the external uses listed above, an expression has to pass three
checks: permission for its use, selection of its form, and satisfaction
of target and dependent conditions. An occurrence is admissible in an
external function just when all three checks succeed. Permissions are
indexed to the lexeme of the phrase's lexical head. The form and
condition checks may consult the surrounding structure, including the
target: independent *none* is selected in *none of the students*, and
*apples* is the target for *some*'s phrase in *some apples*.

## Phrase categories and structural constraints {#sec:det-uniform}

In *some apples* and *Kim's apples*, the determining phrases share the
NP category under D-noun with ordinary Head. For D-noun with fusion, I
retain that NP category and add the combined function relations in
independent uses. *CGEL* instead distinguishes DP from genitive NP. It
also admits restricted plain-case NPs and PPs as determiners, as in
*what size hat* and *over thirty ties* [@huddleston2002 Ch. 5, §4].

I carry this PP-determiner provision over to the three NP-projecting
implementations for this comparison. Its inclusion in the alternatives
is an extrapolation.

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{\textit{CGEL}:} &\quad \mathrm{Det}:\{\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{NP},\mathrm{PP}\} \\
\text{NP-projecting implementations here:} &\quad \mathrm{Det}:\{\mathrm{NP},\mathrm{PP}\}
\end{aligned}$$

NP projection consolidates the principal phrase types in Det function;
determinative-headed, genitive, and other licensed NPs are identified
separately, and all four implementations have at most one Det per NP
with the same definiteness and target-selection conditions. In the
separate-D ordinary-Head account, an NP's ultimate lexical head can be
Noun or D.

The ordinary-Head accounts share three structural conditions. First, an
NP core has a Nom, the layer inside NP, as Head and at most one Det, the
determiner function. Second, a nominal core has a lexical Head and only
the dependents permitted for that head in its construction. Third,
peripheral modification forms an NP with the modifier as dependent and
another NP as Head, as in Figure [5](#fig:every){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:every"}. This keeps peripheral modification outside the
core's Det and Nom dependents.

Where a word occurrence $h$ directly fills Head in Nom, let
$\operatorname{Cat}(h)$ record its primary lexical category. Subcategory
distinctions remain available. The two taxonomies impose different
category conditions:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{D-noun, ordinary Head:} &\quad \operatorname{Cat}(h)=\mathrm{N} \\
\text{Separate D, ordinary Head:} &\quad \operatorname{Cat}(h)\in\{\mathrm{N},\mathrm{D}\}.
\end{aligned}$$

Every account states this condition for common nouns heading Nom. Fusion
is held fixed for adjectives and the nominal *nylon* and *double*
constructions of §[4.6](#sec:genitive-head){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:genitive-head"} in all four implementations, so what the
two ordinary-Head implementations differ in is how they remove the
determinative fusion: D-noun moves the determinatives one node down the
taxonomy and keeps the condition as it is, while separate D keeps D a
primary category and adds the disjunct. The coverage audit marks the
cells that use it (§[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:overall-assessment"}). Both permit the same Nom layers,
selected complements, and ordered modifiers. For anaphoric genitives,
the construction also permits complements licensed by the recovered
nominal description (§[4.6](#sec:genitive-head){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:genitive-head"}); the overt lexical head's permissions
alone don't license them.

The dependent conditions distinguish uses before another nominal from
independent uses. In Det or internal Mod function before a target,
ordinary *some* and *few* exclude external determination, partitive
complements, and relative postmodifiers within their own phrase.
Independent *some* and *few* permit selected partitives and relatives;
independent *few* also permits definite determination and adjectival
premodification, as in *the lucky few*. These dependent conditions don't
transfer to *every* or the articles.

Degree-modifier selection applies across the relevant dependent and
independent uses. It includes AdvPs such as *very*, determinative-headed
modifiers such as *this* in *this much*, and established NPs such as *a
lot* in *a lot fewer*. Comparative complements, as in *more than ten*,
are licensed separately from partitive *of*-phrases. Neither the
category NP nor a general modifier slot licenses arbitrary adjectives or
unrestricted complements.

Postmodification permits zero or more ordered dependents, subject to the
construction. The compounds permit at most one specialized post-head
restrictor followed by ordinary postmodifiers: *useful* precedes *that I
found* in *something useful that I found*. In their primary use they
exclude external determination and a corresponding pre-head adjective;
the set-denoting use of §[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:secondary-use"} (*a special someone*) falls outside the
construction. Their determinative bases supply the premodifier
conditions (§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:compounds"}).

The approximatives retain the two attachment sites motivated in
§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}:
peripheral NP attachment in *almost every* and internal attachment in
externally determined *the almost thirty who came*. Both ordinary-Head
accounts require this distinction.

The fragment incorporates the peripheral-*a* conditions for *a few*, *a
little*, and *a great many*, including the obligatory modifier, and
predeterminer *many* in *many a*, which requires a following *a*-NP
(§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

Each phrase's use permission follows its own lexical head. In *the
apple*, the smaller article phrase has Det permission; the outer NP has
argument permission through *apple*, whose determination requirement is
satisfied. Bare \**Book arrived* fails that requirement, while *Books
arrived* passes. The article itself lacks argument permission
(Table [6](#tab:permissions){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:permissions"}).

The fusion accounts retain these use, form, selection, and ordering
conditions but assign different Head relations. Let $p$ be the
independent NP, $m$ its Head Nom, and $x$ the shared determinative
phrase. Let $n$ be the additional Nom in the Mod--Head configuration.
Write $\operatorname{Head}(p,m)$ for '$m$ fills Head in $p$', and
likewise for Det and Mod. The two configurations satisfy the following
relations:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Det--Head:}\quad
 &\operatorname{Head}(p,m),\quad \operatorname{Det}(p,x),\quad \operatorname{Head}(m,x); \\
\text{Mod--Head:}\quad
 &\operatorname{Head}(p,m),\quad \operatorname{Mod}(m,x),\\
 &\operatorname{Head}(m,n),\quad \operatorname{Head}(n,x).
\end{aligned}$$

Det--Head fills the NP's Det function, excluding a second Det. Mod--Head
leaves that function available for an external determiner. Its
additional Nom $n$ matches Figure [4](#fig:few){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:few"}: the determinative phrase fills Mod in $m$ and Head
in $n$. The ordinary adjective *lucky* separately modifies $m$. These
are simultaneous relations on a shared constituent, with no sequence of
fusion operations assumed.

Under separate D with fusion, $x$ is a DP whose lexical Head is D.
Selected premodifiers and comparative complements belong within that DP;
the independent partitive complement, compound restrictor, and ordinary
postmodifiers belong to the Nom structure outside it, as in
Figure [6](#fig:compound){reference-type="ref"
reference="fig:compound"}. The permissions for the complete independent
NP still exclude articles; for *every*, the fragment represents only the
ordinary dependent pattern. Possessing a dependent Det use doesn't
itself license an independent fused use.

Under D-noun with fusion, $x$ is the NP projected by the determinative
noun. The same joint relations connect it to the outer Nom. In this
construction, the inner NP excludes external determination, partitives,
restrictors, and relatives: any licensed independent-use dependents
occur outside it, as in the separate-D fusion account. These
restrictions need stating separately from the limit of one fusion
configuration around the determinative's projection.

Ordinary headedness uses the determinative's NP directly for bare,
partitive, and externally determined expressions. In the fusion
accounts, its DP or NP occupies the further Nom structure just
specified. Section [5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:overall-assessment"} assesses the relation between the
taxonomy and the shared structure.

Nesting determinative inside pronoun would add pronoun to the
inheritance path from Noun without changing this fragment's judgments.
Applying Hudson's nesting to the full determinative inventory used here
is an extrapolation; §[6.2](#sec:inheritance){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:inheritance"} discusses the inventory differences.

## Degree uses outside noun phrases {#sec:enough}

When *enough* modifies *good* in *good enough*, the three NP-projecting
implementations analyse it as an NP modifier of an adjective. This would
be a cost if NPs couldn't occur in that function, or if their
distribution differed systematically from that of degree determinatives.
The comparison therefore includes the degree uses of established NPs.
Attributive uses are the crucial test, because *CGEL* restricts NP
modifiers there.

*Enough* tests both position and external function. It precedes a
nominal in *enough money* and can follow one in *money enough*;
post-head *enough* can't itself be premodified, as shown by \**money
almost enough* [@huddleston2002 396--397, 445].

NP modification inside an AdjP also permits post-head position. In
([\[ex:temporal-adjp\]](#ex:temporal-adjp){reference-type="ref"
reference="ex:temporal-adjp"}), I analyse *every Tuesday*, *this week*,
and *next week* as temporal NP modifiers within the bracketed AdjPs. The
separate degree modifiers and temporal specifications in the coordinated
example make that attachment clear. These NPs express time, while
*enough* expresses degree; the shared position leaves their selectional
differences intact.

**(2)**

  a. *Applicants* [*completely free every Tuesday*] *will be interviewed next month.*
  b. *Anyone* [*very busy this week*] *but* [*completely free next week*] *should contact me today.*

*Enough* also modifies adjectives, adverbs, verbs, and some PPs: *good
enough*, *quickly enough*, *I hadn't prepared enough*, and *enough in
control* [@huddleston2002 396--397]. The degree determinatives *much*
and *little*, and *no*/*none*, likewise have uses outside NP structure
[@huddleston2002 390, 395--397]. @reynolds2024why surveys the
determinatives that modify AdjPs, AdvPs, and PPs (*more*, *less*,
*enough*, *that*, *much*, *a little*, *all*) and finds their
distribution idiosyncratic lexeme by lexeme, as adjective intensifiers
are generally, with no pattern that sets any of them apart as an adverb;
the modifying phrases take modifiers of their own (*far fewer*, *much
more*, *so much*), the internal structure an NP headed by a
determinative supplies.[^21]

NPs modify adjectives in *three years old*, *a great deal smaller*, and
*plenty big enough*. *CGEL* also treats *lots better* and *heaps worse*
as containing quantificational NP modifiers [@huddleston2002 549--550].
These uses extend the connection with the quantificational common nouns
compared in §[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-nouns"}.

*CGEL* contrasts predicative *a great deal better* with \**some a great
deal better proposals*, against permitted *some much better proposals*
[@huddleston2002 551--552]. The excluded example also juxtaposes *some*
and *a*; it doesn't isolate NP category as the cause. *CGEL* permits
*She's a lot better player than me*, analysing the inner article as lost
after the outer one [@huddleston2002 p. 552, n. 8].

Informal attributive examples with nominal *heaps* and *lots* further
qualify a categorical exclusion.[^22] They don't establish the same
distribution as degree determinatives. If degree determinatives
systematically permit attributive uses excluded for established NP
modifiers, that would favour DP projection, a question of phrase
structure rather than of membership within Noun.

## Comparison of the four implementations {#sec:costs}

The articles' restrictions, *no*/*none* form selection, the compound
restrictor conditions, and the differences among existing noun
subcategories in determination and modification
(Table [3](#tab:existing){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:existing"}) are the same under all four implementations.

Both ordinary-Head accounts distinguish NP-peripheral modifiers from the
internal degree and cardinal approximative conditions
(§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}). For
compounds, this NP--Nom boundary preserves the modifier-domain contrast
that fusion expresses through its inner phrase and outer Nom
(§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:compounds"}).

In the ordinary-Head accounts, *few* heads Nom and Nom heads NP across
bare, partitive, and externally determined uses. In the fusion accounts,
where one constituent fills Head and a dependent function together, its
DP or NP fills Det--Head in bare and partitive uses and Mod--Head with
an external determiner
(Table [4](#tab:matched-heads){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:matched-heads"}). In these matched examples, all have Nom
as the NP's immediate Head. The contrast concerns the full Head chain
and the additional fused functions.

I favour sharing the direct Head chain across determinative uses and
with established nouns. The selection and attachment conditions preserve
the modifier-domain contrast. Fusion instead shares a construction with
adjectives. Both cover the matched cases with the same lexical
restrictions; the trade-off concerns which structures the grammar
shares. The wider profile comparison supplies the reason for preferring
the grouping with nouns.

@reynolds2026numerals distinguishes determinative numerals such as *ten*
in *ten men*, proper-noun cases such as *10* in *Room 10*, and
common-noun numerals such as *tens* in *tens of pens*. Under the D-noun
categorization, all fall within primary Noun, retaining their
subcategory and constructional differences. Ordinals remain adjectives,
and complex numeral phrases remain distinct from single lexemes.

@payne2013anaphoric [797--798] distinguish three lexemes spelled *one*:
determinative, anaphoric common noun, and generic pronoun. These remain
distinct under the D-noun categorization, but all belong within primary
Noun.

Complex cardinals also separate category from function. In *two hundred
books*, the whole *two hundred* fills Det; internally, *two* modifies
the magnitude head *hundred* [@reynolds2026numerals §4]. In *these two
hundred books*, *these* fills Det and *two hundred* is an internal
modifier. One Det function doesn't entail a limit of one determinative
lexeme per NP.

## Overall assessment {#sec:overall-assessment}

The D-noun analysis combines the two preferences. The lexical comparison
favours Noun membership because connections with nouns recur across more
of the determinative system
(§[2.6](#sec:membership){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:membership"}). The structural comparison favours the
direct Head chain across the licensed uses
(§[5.4](#sec:costs){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:costs"}). Within
the proposed grammar, determinatives then meet the same Noun condition
on lexical Heads in Nom as the existing subcategories
(§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:det-uniform"}). That category condition expresses the
grouping supported by the profile.

Separate D with ordinary Head licenses the same structures by admitting
N or D. The coverage audit (fifth supplement) compares four specified
implementations across the fragment and selected wider comparisons,
including degree uses (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:enough"}). It records the mechanism that covers each
construction family and lexeme group. Of its 103 cells, 35 are lexical
facts identical in all four accounts. The simple determinatives'
genitive restrictions and the anaphoric genitive complement condition
lie outside those cells; the audit doesn't measure their comparative
cost. The fusion accounts state where partitives and relatives attach as
constructional conditions, and *CGEL* states its peripheral-modifier,
number-transparency, and degree-modifier rules separately for NPs and
DPs.

Both ordinary-Head accounts consolidate these rules. D-noun does so by
reclassification, retaining the Noun condition on lexical Heads in Nom.
The stated separate-D implementation adds D to that condition; the
audit's 32 cells coded Dn, its label for a derivation that goes through
that added disjunct, record applications of this single condition. It
also retains the category change for secondary uses and extends the
head-genitive definition to D. These are commitments of the
implementation compared, rather than costs established for every
separate-D grammar.

A separate-D account could instead assign the same feature once to Noun
and once to D, with inheritance within each category. The condition on
lexical Heads in Nom and the head-genitive definition could each refer
to that feature. One secondary-use schema could cover the same three
input types, with category change specified for the D inputs if the
account retains *CGEL*'s output categories. Such sharing requires
neither per-lexeme feature assignments nor wholly separate rules for the
shared behaviour.

The feature would identify the same broad domain as the proposed Noun
category while retaining the primary Noun--D boundary. I favour Noun
membership because the profile comparison independently supports that
grouping (§[2.6](#sec:membership){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:membership"}); the category then states the domain of the
shared grammar directly. The audit shows what the four implementations
state, but doesn't establish a strict description-length advantage over
the feature formulation. The secondary use and the former complex
determinatives weren't among the diagnostics of
§[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"};
they're additional consequences, not tests set in advance.

The secondary set-denoting use of
§[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:secondary-use"} covers names, compound determinatives,
and cardinals within Noun. With *CGEL*'s categories the compound and
cardinal inputs change category; a separate-D account can share the
schema as just described.

Several rules *CGEL* states separately for nouns and determinatives can
likewise be stated once: number transparency for *lot*, *number*, and
*some* (§[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-nouns"}); NP and determinative degree modifiers of
adjectives (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:enough"}); and peripheral attachment in *almost every*
and *only you* (§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:payne"}). *CGEL* already states the head genitive once,
by the head noun's inflection, listing *everyone's* beside *Edward's*
(§[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:inflection"}); within Noun that statement holds as
written.

The four complex determinatives analysed here reduce to peripheral *a*
and predeterminer *many*
(§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}). These generalizations are
available under either ordinary-Head taxonomy: the proposed account
states their domain through Noun membership, the separate-D alternative
through the inherited feature. The profile of
§[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"}
supplies the case for lexical-category status.

If determinative-headed and genitive expressions require different
structural constraints after their independently motivated restrictions
are held fixed, the shared-projection proposal in
§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:det-uniform"} loses its advantage. That would favour
separate phrase types. Retaining a separate primary D requires the
further case that the category boundary captures the recurring
differences better than a determinative subcategory within Noun.

# Consequences and remaining taxonomic choices {#sec:consequences}

The grouping permits one schema for the secondary uses of names,
compound determinatives, and cardinals. Whether determinative and
pronoun should form an intermediate category is a further choice.

## Secondary set-denoting uses {#sec:secondary-use}

Compound determinatives have a secondary set-denoting use, in which they
take an article, a pre-head adjective, and plural inflection:
lexicalized *a special someone* and *a certain someone*, and
productively *an anonymous someone*, *the responsible someone*, *a furry
something*, and *such an anyone*.[^23]

*CGEL* records *nobody* in *a nobody* and *something* in *a little
something* as nouns formed by conversion [@huddleston2002 423, n. 43].
Under the D-noun analysis no category change is involved: the use is the
one *CGEL* gives proper nouns in *an Ophelia* and *the Smiths*, where
the name comes to "denote the set of people bearing this name" and then
"takes a full range of dependents, including determiners and restrictive
modifiers" [@huddleston2002 521].

In both subcategories the article and the pre-head adjective follow from
what the noun now denotes, a kind or an individual of the quantifier's
description, and quantificational force is gone: *an anybody* is a
person of no particular standing. The quantifier and its echo as a noun
can share a text: "anybody could have competed. I qualified as an
anybody" (*Skiing*, 1992, COCA). One process thus covers two
subcategories where *CGEL* uses conversion for one and a secondary use
for the other.

*CGEL* makes the same move a third time for cardinal numerals, which are
"primarily determinatives but they have a secondary use in which they
inflect for number and hence belong in the noun category": *in threes*,
*in their hundreds* [@huddleston2002 385]. Within Noun the plural
cardinal is the same secondary use again, with no category to change;
@reynolds2026numerals supplies the separate case that cardinals have
both uses.

The rule can be stated once, with its input class stated: a Noun lexeme
whose primary use identifies or quantifies over individuals, a name, a
compound determinative, or a cardinal, has a secondary use in which it
denotes the set of individuals fitting its description, the bearers of
the name, the persons or things of the quantifier's description, the
groups of the cardinal's size, and in that use it takes plural
inflection and the common noun's dependents, an article, a pre-head
adjective, and restrictive modifiers.

The articles and *every* have no such use (\**an every*); whether the
personal pronouns do (*the hes and shes*) I leave open. Within Noun the
rule has three inputs. With separate D and *CGEL*'s output categories,
the compound and cardinal inputs change category; a shared schema can
still state their common behaviour
(§[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:overall-assessment"}).[^24]

Locative compounds before a noun (*an anywhere operation*) are the
ordinary noun-as-modifier use and take their article from the head noun.

## Coordinate or nested subcategories {#sec:inheritance}

Including determinatives within Noun leaves a further question: where
within Noun do they belong? In the proposed hierarchy, common noun,
proper noun, pronoun, and determinative are four coordinate
subcategories. Hudson classes his determiners as pronouns, and pronoun
is itself inside Noun. Both group the relevant determinatives with
nouns. They differ in whether pronouns and determinatives form an
intermediate category that excludes common and proper nouns.

@hudson2010wordgrammar [253--254] makes taxonomic inclusion explicit.
His noun category contains common noun, proper noun, and pronoun; what
he calls a determiner is a pronoun whose [valency]{.smallcaps} permits
the relevant common-noun dependent [@hudson2004determiners 9--10]. The
word's category remains constant across independent and dependent uses,
which differ in their permitted dependents.

Hudson's criteria centre on licensing a singular count common noun and
on mutual exclusion in that use. He sets aside *all*, cardinal numerals,
and quantifiers restricted to plural or non-count nouns
[@hudson2004determiners 9--10]. Their place in his taxonomy remains
unsettled by those criteria. Extending the pronoun analysis to *CGEL*'s
fuller inventory requires comparing its modifier selection, grade, and
quantitative meanings with the existing pronouns' properties.

The intermediate category would be useful if it supported grammatical
generalizations applying to pronouns and determinatives together.
Coordinate rank permits unequal similarities among the noun
subcategories. The issue is whether their shared properties warrant
another level in the lexical hierarchy.

@reynolds2021 compares 138 word forms through properties recorded as
present or absent, such as accepting *almost*. Its unsupervised
clustering groups forms by these similarities without being given their
category labels. The groups broadly resemble the pronoun and
determinative inventories, though a replication finds that the outcome
varies with initialization and feature selection.[^25] But the study
contains no common or proper nouns, and extending the comparison to the
open categories would require new sampling, feature selection, and
coding. Distinguishing the two groups establishes neither their
taxonomic rank nor the proposed superordinate category; the examples
discussed here aren't representative samples of the open categories.

The grammatical case for nesting includes shared lexical properties
(Table [3](#tab:existing){reference-type="ref"
reference="tab:existing"}). Pronouns and determinatives both have closed
inventories, and both include gender-sensitive forms and interrogative
or relative forms. The contrasts *she*/*it* and *somebody*/*something*
are carried by the lexical head, whereas descriptions and names shape
referent construal (§[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:inflection"}). These connections are more specific than
their general dependence on context.

The *my*/*mine* and *no*/*none* paradigms supply a further grammatical
parallel (§[3.3](#sec:form-selection){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:form-selection"}). In ordinary NP uses, both distinguish
dependent and independent forms. The parallel has limited lexical reach,
but it supplies positive evidence for the proposed intermediate
grouping.

Modifier selection gives mixed support: restricted adjectival
modification in *poor old me* [@huddleston2002 429--430] has a
counterpart in *the lucky few*. Both groups permit NP-peripheral
modifiers (§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:payne"}), while the four gradable quantifiers also permit
the internal degree-AdvP series
(§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}).

Personal pronouns and primary naming uses of proper nouns both resist
free determination [@huddleston2002 429--430, 517, 519--520].
Determinatives allow *the few*, *the two*, and *these three*; *CGEL*
gives *these few here* and *the many who did* [@huddleston2002
415--416].

I retain coordinate rank because the closer affinities cross the
proposed intermediate boundary. Partitives, number transparency,
restricted dependents, and degree uses connect determinatives with
quantificational common nouns
(§[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:quant-nouns"}), while reference and selected form
alternations connect them with pronouns. The coordinate analysis records
both connections within Noun and preserves the distinct organization of
each subcategory: person, case, and reflexivity in much of the pronoun
system; quantification, determination, and degree in much of the
determinative system.

Both inheritance paths meet the fragment's Noun condition
(§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:det-uniform"}), and nesting remains a coherent
alternative. The coordinate preference rests on these intersecting
profiles; accepting Noun membership and ordinary Head leaves this
further choice open.

# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}

I propose including English determinatives within Noun. The strongest
positive comparison comes from quantificational common nouns: their
selected complements, number transparency, restricted dependents, and
degree uses connect with parts of the determinative inventory. The
connected adjectival profile of the four gradable quantifiers deserves
weight on the same terms. Broader constructional and referential
connections, with partial inflectional corroboration, favour the
grouping with nouns.

The grouping includes restricted members through their integration into
the determinative system. Articles participate in its contrasts in
definiteness, quantity, and count selection; *no*/*none* retains its
form-selection conditions.

The ordinary-Head analysis uses the same chain in bare, partitive, and
externally determined expressions: the word heads Nom, and Nom heads NP.
The taxonomic and structural choices complement each other: the shared
profile supports grouping determinatives with nouns, and that grouping
satisfies the proposed grammar's category condition on lexical Heads in
Nom. The coverage audit distinguishes the stated separate-D
implementation from an alternative that shares those conditions through
an inherited feature. The latter can express the shared domain too; the
profile comparison supplies the case for treating that domain as Noun.

Under D-noun, the three specified inputs -- names (*an Ophelia*),
compound determinatives (*a special someone*), and cardinals (*in
threes*) -- share one secondary-use schema without a change of primary
category (§[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:secondary-use"},
§[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:overall-assessment"}). The complex determinatives *a few*
and *many a* dissolve into peripheral modification by *a* and
predeterminer modification by *many*
(§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

Coordinate rank alongside pronoun is a further preference, since the
comparisons connect determinatives with both pronouns and
quantificational common nouns.

@palmer1924 [24] proposed placing "determinative adjectives" with
pronouns. He contrasted them with qualifying adjectives, which permit
predicative use, comparison, and adverbial modification. Most
determinatives, he observed, can "be used indifferently as pronouns or
as modifiers of nouns". @lyons1968 [279] adds a semantic connection: the
definite article, demonstratives, and third-person pronouns share the
feature "definite", and their traditional separation, he says, obscures
the relationship.

@postal1966 develops the article--pronoun connection through English
reflexives and combinations such as *we men*. His category assignments
depend on representational level: article segments occur at intermediate
stages, their deepest counterparts are features of nouns, and surface
pronouns can receive derivative Noun status. This differs from a
proposal about the lexical taxonomy of the surface words.

@sommerstein1972 [197--203] argues in the opposite direction, giving the
definite article and personal pronouns underlying NP structure. His
English comparison includes the count restriction on anaphoric *one*,
whereas *it* can stand for non-count as well as count nouns.[^26]

These predecessors challenge a fundamental article--pronoun separation
but don't settle the full modern determinative inventory's surface
taxonomy. Its contrasts in grade, modification, complementation, and
restricted independent use extend beyond their proposals. Hudson's
explicit nesting and Spinillo's restricted article grouping are assessed
in §§[6.2](#sec:inheritance){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:inheritance"} and [3](#sec:articles){reference-type="ref"
reference="sec:articles"}, respectively.
