## The claim, from the front matter alone

English determinatives, the whole of *CGEL*'s inventory including the articles, are a fourth coordinate subcategory of Noun. The evidence is a profile comparison with the three established subcategories and with adjectives. The package adds ordinary Head for independent uses in place of *CGEL*'s fusion, and an audit of four implementations is said to show that the account's economy is the economy of the reclassification. Two consequences are claimed: three secondary uses unified, and the complex determinatives dissolved. The kind of claim is a synchronic lexical-taxonomic one within *CGEL*'s framework, with a structural claim paired to it.

## 1. Does the title name the contribution?

Yes. *Determinatives as nouns in English* names the taxonomic claim, which the body argues for in §§2–3 and §6. The structural half of the package (ordinary Head, §§4–5) is not in the title, and needn't be: the body presents it as paired with, and motivated by, the categorization.

## 2. Does the abstract match the body?

Three points, one of them a wording fault.

- **A promise the body qualifies.** "the grammatical profile of the full inventory of *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*" reads as if the inventory belonged to the grammar rather than to its determinative category, and "full" overstates: cardinals above a hundred are handed to `reynolds2026numerals` (§3.1), and independent *what* and relative *which* are left open between pronoun and determinative (§2.5, footnote). Proposed repair, abstract side: "the grammatical profile of *CGEL*'s determinative inventory". The body's own scope statement (§5.1, "selected permissions") is the honest one.
- **A result the body has and the abstract omits.** §6 adjudicates between coordinate rank and Hudson's nesting of determiner inside pronoun, and prefers coordinate rank on the ground that the closer affinities cross the intermediate boundary. The abstract's "a fourth subcategory ... beside" implies coordinate rank without saying that the paper argues it against a named alternative. One clause would carry it. Abstract side, optional.
- **The economy sentence matches §5.5** as revised this week, and the three-cost argument is there in full (disjunct, duplicated category, conversion rule and extended genitive definition). The abstract names the first two; that is enough for an abstract.

## 3. Does the introduction say what problem this solves and what kind of claim it makes?

Yes. The first paragraph poses the question with four words, the second fixes the framework and the kind of claim (synchronic, English, lexical categories), the paragraph beginning "Earlier accounts" says what is new (the scope and the pairing, the four combinations, the fragment and the coverage audit), and the last paragraph gives the route.

One structural observation, not a mismatch: the *every* paragraph and its corpus footnote (List counts, 618 and 164 lines) sit in §1 as a pre-emptive answer to "every can't be independent, so it isn't a noun". It is body-level detail in the introduction, and the same exclusion is stated again in §5.1 under the use permissions. The footnote could move to §5.1 and leave one sentence in §1. Paper side, Brett's call.

## 4. Does the conclusion overclaim?

No. Every closing claim is carried by the body, and each is hedged as the body hedges it ("partial inflectional corroboration", "a further preference").

The fault runs the other way: **the conclusion lags the abstract.** The abstract's headline result, that the audit shows the account's economy to be the economy of the reclassification and that a separate category matches it only with a disjunct and a duplicated category, is absent from the conclusion; so is the dissolution of the complex determinatives. Both are this week's results, and the conclusion predates them. A desk editor who reads abstract and conclusion together will find the conclusion claiming less than the abstract. Proposed repair, paper side: one sentence after the ordinary-Head paragraph stating the three costs in the terms of §5.5, and a clause on the complex determinatives beside the three secondary uses.

## The heading sequence

Read in order, the headings describe an argument: the question and the alternatives; grounds for the grouping; the restricted members; ordinary Head against fusion; the fragment and its costs; rank; conclusion. Two placements to consider:

- **§2.7 "Category, projection, semantic type, and construction" follows §2.6 "Weighing the profiles".** §2.6 is the verdict of §2; §2.7 is a clarification (four separable questions, with Solt and Van Eynde answered) added this week. A verdict followed by a clarification reads as an afterthought. Either move §2.7 before §2.6, so that the weighing closes the section, or keep the order and retitle §2.7 to signal that it delimits what the verdict decided ("What the comparison decides, and what it leaves open"). Paper side.
- **§2.3 "Quantificational adjectives and nominal independence"** between the common-noun connection (§2.2) and the adjectival profile (§2.4) is the controls section and is in the right place: it separates the nominal connection from the adjectival one by the *numerous/many* contrast.
