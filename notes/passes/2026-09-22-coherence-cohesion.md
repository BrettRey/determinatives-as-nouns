# Paragraph flow: Determinatives as nouns
<!-- SUMMARY: Full sequential reading of the 22 September draft; eight priority joins and five smaller repairs implemented at Brett's instruction; rebuilt and verified · status: complete · updated: 2026-09-22 -->

The overall sequence works: the paper establishes the broader noun profile, handles restricted members, compares Head analyses, specifies their commitments, and considers the remaining taxonomic choices. Targeted repairs should be enough. The recurring difficulty is that a paragraph resumes an earlier thread after a qualification, source discussion, or methodological aside has intervened. Several sections also reach their verdict and then append another example or qualification.

The strongest continuous stretches are §§4.2, 4.5–4.6, and 6.2. They move from the comparison to the necessary restrictions and then to a consequence or the next question. I would preserve that pattern. The introductory JSON paragraph fits after the account of evidence; its later reminder currently interrupts the comparison of rules.

The review below records the initial read-only assessment of the whole manuscript, including the appendix and data statement. Paragraphs were read in sequence with their immediate predecessors and successors, including intervening examples, tables, and figures. Brett subsequently instructed implementation. The original findings and suggestions are preserved below; the completion record at the end identifies the applied repairs and verification.

## Eight priority repairs

### 1. §1 returns to setup after its roadmap

Location: source lines 90–100; PDF p. 3.

The contribution paragraph leads naturally into “The paper advances two separable claims,” which maps the coming sections. Three further paragraphs then reopen matters the reader reasonably thought had been settled: whole-expression NP status, category versus function, and the DP hypothesis.

> {==Both Head analyses give the whole independent expression NP status. Their difference concerns its internal structure.==}{>>Move this clarification next to the initial explanation of the two Head analyses. Place the category/function clarification with the earlier definition of determiner, and put the DP-hypothesis distinction before the contribution and roadmap paragraphs. The roadmap can then complete the introduction's setup.<<}{#c1}

This is a sequencing problem. Adding a connective to “Both Head analyses…” would leave the backward turn in place.

### 2. §2.2 withdraws a subject restriction and then seems to withdraw it again

Location: source lines 167–169; PDF p. 7.

The first paragraph ends by saying that amount subjects are one case of predicative use rather than its condition. The next opens “With a degree modifier the subject condition lapses,” as though that condition still held at the end of the preceding paragraph. The new corpus evidence has changed what this transition needs to do.

> {~~With a degree modifier the subject condition lapses:~>Degree-modified uses supply further predicative examples:~~}{#s1} *the pain and immobility became too much to bear*, *sympathy seemed too much to hope for*, *it seemed so little to give* …

The paragraph can then develop its existing comparison with quantity NPs. No change to the evidence or judgments is needed.

### 3. §2.6 needs to explain why Van Eynde enters the weighing

Location: source lines 285–293.

The first paragraph sets the noun and adjective profiles alongside one another. The next begins with Italian and Dutch inflection. Only the following paragraph tells the reader why this matters here: extrapolating that criterion would split the English inventory between the two categories.

> {++One possible response to these competing profiles is to split the determinative inventory between Noun and Adjective.++}{#s2} Van Eynde argues from Italian and Dutch that determiners form no category of their own …

Keep the existing qualification that the English application is an extrapolation. The added sentence frames the alternative without attributing that English proposal to Van Eynde. The subsequent paragraph about his functor analysis should remain subordinate to this comparison; its technical detail could go in a footnote if the interruption still feels long.

### 4. The transition from §3.2 to §3.3 should deliver the promised contrast first

Location: source lines 334 and 346–354; PDF p. 16.

§3.2 promises that the next subsection will handle *many a* through predeterminer modification. §3.3 instead opens with *such* and *what* “enter[ing] the same reanalysis from the adjective side.” The reader has to wait to learn which structure is now at issue and how it differs from peripheral *a*.

> {~~Such and exclamative *what* enter the same reanalysis from the adjective side.~>In *many a man*, *a* fills Det and *many* is a predeterminer modifier. *Such* and exclamative *what* enter the same predeterminer analysis from the adjective side.~~}{#s3}

The following discussion of CGEL's category criteria now has an explicit structural comparison to work from. This preserves the distinction between assigning a function and reclassifying a word.

### 5. §5.3 announces the attributive test before a different comparison

Location: source lines 762–777; PDF p. 30.

The opening identifies attributive distribution as the crucial test. The reader then gets nominal word order, temporal NPs after adjectives, the range of external functions, and established degree-NP examples before that test arrives. These paragraphs have a purpose, but the opening makes the reader expect a different next step.

> {==Attributive uses are the crucial test, because CGEL restricts NP modifiers there.==}{>>Move this sentence from the opening paragraph to immediately before the contrast beginning “CGEL contrasts predicative a great deal better…”. The existing intervening paragraphs can first establish the positional and functional parallels; the sentence then introduces the distributional test at the point where it is carried out.<<}{#c2}

This move is enough; the temporal-NP material needn't be deleted to make the section flow.

### 6. §5.4 changes from structural economy to taxonomic consequences without marking the turn

Location: source lines 787–795.

“I favour sharing the direct Head chain…” concludes the structural comparison. Three paragraphs on numerals, *one*, and complex cardinals follow. They belong to the comparison, but the reader has to recover their relationship to the taxonomic-consequences column of the earlier table.

> {++The taxonomies also differ in how they group numeral uses and the three lexemes spelled *one*.++}{#s4} Reynolds distinguishes determinative numerals such as *ten* in *ten men*, proper-noun cases such as *10* in *Room 10*, and common-noun numerals such as *tens* in *tens of pens* …

That makes the change of question explicit. The final complex-cardinal paragraph should then connect to the distinction between lexical categorization and the function of the whole numeral phrase, which its examples already demonstrate.

### 7. My new JSON reminder interrupts an explicit backward reference

Location: source lines 801–805; PDF p. 32.

The audit paragraph ends with the rules CGEL states separately for NPs and DPs. “Both ordinary-Head accounts consolidate these rules” should follow it directly. The new paragraph about revising JSON classifications separates the claim from that antecedent.

> {==Readers can revise individual classifications in the JSON file and regenerate the tables with the accompanying script. The reported sensitivity analyses show how alternative coding conventions affect the totals.==}{>>Retain this methodological point as a footnote attached to the sentence introducing the coverage audit. Keep the rule list and “Both ordinary-Head accounts consolidate these rules” consecutive in the main text. The fuller methodological explanation remains in §2.<<}{#c3}

The content is relevant, but this paragraph position was a poor choice in the last edit.

### 8. §5.5 refers backward to three inputs before introducing them

Location: source lines 807–811; PDF p. 32.

“The same three input types” presupposes a list. The intended inputs—names, compound determinatives, and cardinals—receive their explanation in §6.1. The immediately preceding discussion has also mentioned three numeral uses and three *one* lexemes, making the unexpressed antecedent particularly unhelpful.

> {~~One secondary-use schema could cover the same three input types,~>One schema could likewise cover the secondary uses of names, compound determinatives, and cardinals (§6.1),~~}{#s5} with category change specified for the D inputs if the account retains CGEL's output categories.

With that explicit preview, the later paragraph can go:

> {--The secondary set-denoting use of §6.1 covers names, compound determinatives, and cardinals within Noun. With CGEL's categories the compound and cardinal inputs change category; a separate-D account can share the schema as just described.--}{#s6}

The concession to the separate-D analysis remains explicit. The deletion removes a repeated verdict and lets §6.1 motivate and explain the schema once readers reach it.

## What already flows well

- The close of §3.4 hands §4 its exact question: the form restrictions survive either analysis, so the next issue is ordinary Head versus fusion.
- §4.2 moves coherently from completeness through contextual interpretation to what both analyses can express.
- §4.5 keeps the compound example in view while comparing the two modifier domains and their constituent groupings.
- §4.6 makes the genitive extension, specifies the extra conditions it needs, retains fusion where warranted, and hands §5 the question of comparative economy.
- §6.2 states the nesting question, explains Hudson's proposal, distinguishes the matrix's evidential limits, weighs the grammatical parallels, and gives a qualified preference.
- The conclusion follows the order of the main argument. It doesn't need another general summary sentence to close it.
