# Four analytical findings: source check and proposed decisions
Claude has identified two definite reporting errors (_every_ and _such_), a missing explanation of polarity, and a relevant argument about fusion that the paper needs to address. None of these establishes that determinatives cannot be nouns. The fusion issue bears chiefly on the separate ordinary-Head proposal.

This file records the proposals Brett reviewed and the subsequent application. The original proposals and inline comments are preserved below; the review response at the end records the final decisions and checks.
## 1. CGEL pp. 419–421: what the objections reach
### The traditional pronoun analysis
Brett is right about (a) and (b). The paper doesn't change _many_ from determinative to pronoun when it becomes independent. It preserves the determinative subcategory across functions, including its distinctive dependents. Nor does it turn independent adjectives or genitive common nouns into pronouns. Those objections to the traditional analysis therefore don't transfer directly.

Point (c) is a real cost. CGEL proposes that fusion explains the resistance to genitive forms such as _many's_: the word must meet the case requirements of both Det and Head. Ordinary Head removes that proposed explanation. Our existing statement that most determinatives lack case inflection records the distribution, but doesn't acknowledge this loss of motivation.

We should retain a lexical restriction on genitive formation and state the cost. Noun membership doesn't entail an unrestricted genitive paradigm. CGEL's own compound determinatives admit _everyone's_, so fusion isn't by itself a universal ban on genitive determinatives; its rationale needs the appropriate lexical and constructional scope. That qualification doesn't make its explanation worthless.

**Proposed addition to §2.3:**

> Most simple determinatives resist genitive marking. CGEL connects this restriction with fusion: {==a fused determiner-head must satisfy the case requirements of both functions==}{>>This is one possible explanation, but does CGEL argue for it or simply assert it? Are there better justifications?<<}{id="c1" by="user" at="2026-09-19T20:55:11.813Z"} (p. 420). Ordinary Head loses that proposed motivation and must retain a lexical restriction on genitive formation. The compounds differ, admitting forms such as _everyone's_. Their genitives support the broader nominal connection without extending the paradigm to every determinative.
### Ellipsis and [37]
Brett is also right that [35] doesn't damage this account: ordinary Head doesn't posit a missing noun, and the paper retains adjectival fusion.

[37] distinguishes NP anaphora from clausal gapping. Its crucial contrast is that independent _Helen's_ can occur with _of the Rachmaninov_, understood through the earlier noun _performance_, but cannot take _the Rachmaninov_ as though _attempt to play_ had simply disappeared. The coordination restriction on clausal gapping supplies the other contrast.

These are not counterexamples to ordinary Head merely because they refute a general ellipsis account. They do expose something missing from our genitive extension: saying that context supplies a description doesn't yet specify which complements the resulting NP permits. The current fragment normally licenses dependents through its overt lexical head; _Helen_ doesn't itself license the relevant _of_-complement.

Fusion represents the recovered dependent relation explicitly, which gives it an advantage here. But the fusion tree doesn't contain an overt _performance_ either: the connection between the anaphoric construction and that noun's complement frame still needs to be stated. We can state a corresponding condition for ordinary Head. We shouldn't claim that the two accounts have identical costs without doing that work, or rely on the construction's rarity or uncertainty about gapping.

**Proposed addition to §4.4, retaining ordinary independent genitives:**

> Anaphoric genitives also need a condition on complements. In CGEL's {==_Helen's of the Rachmaninov_==}{>>the reader needs more context than this<<}{id="c2" by="user" at="2026-09-19T20:57:18.317Z"}, the antecedent supplies the description _performance_ and its nominal complement frame (pp. 420–421). I allow the anaphoric genitive construction to recover that frame. It doesn't thereby inherit complements of verbs embedded in an antecedent: _Helen's the Rachmaninov_ cannot stand for _Helen's attempt to play the Rachmaninov_. Ordinary Head thus needs a constructional condition beyond contextual interpretation and agreement.

This is a proposed condition, not a claim that the present formal fragment already implements it. Its statement and scope must be added to the fragment/cost discussion if accepted. Retaining fusion for anaphoric genitives is the available narrower alternative; I don't think [37] forces that retreat.
### The third approach is relevant, but its rejection has a wider scope
I disagree with Brett's last sentence. CGEL's [38ii] explicitly considers _many_ as determiner in _many people_ and as ordinary Head in independent _many_, with no change of category. That overlaps directly with our structural proposal. Moving determinative into Noun doesn't make the argument irrelevant: CGEL's counterexamples _nylon_ and _double_ are already nouns.

Claude's formulation nevertheless overstates the result. CGEL is seeking a general alternative to fusion across the constructions in its §9. Its examples show why simply labelling something Noun and Head doesn't capture every interpretation associated with a missing nominal description. Our paper needn't abolish fusion throughout NP grammar.

**Recommended scope:** retain fusion for CGEL's particular _nylon_ and _double_ examples, alongside the retained adjective cases. State explicitly that nounhood alone doesn't eliminate fusion. Argue for ordinary determinative Head through the particular unifications of bare, partitive, externally determined, and modified uses established in §4. Whether an ordinary-Head analysis could also capture _nylon_ and _double_ through contextual interpretation can remain outside this paper.

**Proposed paragraph near the close of §4:**

> CGEL considers category-preserving changes of function, including ordinary Head for independent _many_, but rejects this as a general replacement for fusion (p. 421). Its counterexamples are nominal _nylon_ understood as a modifier of _shirts_, and _double_ understood as a predeterminer multiplier. I retain fusion for those constructions. The proposal concerns the determinative constructions compared here, with a further extension to independent genitives. Its advantage must come from their shared structure; nounhood alone doesn't establish ordinary Head in every construction.

The fragment should consequently say that retained fusion includes these nominal constructions, rather than mentioning only adjectives. The coverage audit shouldn't imply that the two examples have been tested or that the new genitive condition is costless.
## 2. Few, a few, and polarity
The polarity finding is sound. The summary relayed to Brett said ‘a few in determiner position’, inviting a misreading; this was ambiguity in the summary wording, not an error in the underlying finding. In CGEL's cited example, **bare _few_** makes the clause negative; _a few_ is positive (pp. 393–394).

The September 17 appeal to polarity tracking the Det slot no longer supports the peripheral-_a_ analysis. That rationale must be explicitly superseded. But the new tree doesn't itself require _a few mistakes_ to be negative. That result follows only if we also assume that negative polarity passes unchanged from _few_ through this construction. The paper needs to say why it doesn't.

Brett's examples help against an inference from specialized interpretation to a complex lexical noun. Expressions such as _a bit_ can have a minimizer interpretation under negation without becoming single nouns. CGEL itself contrasts affirmative and negated _a bit_ in its discussion of degree adjuncts (p. 725).

They are not a knockdown argument about _few_. In _doesn't matter a bit_, external _not_ supplies the negation and licenses the minimizer reading. Bare _few_ is itself negative; _a few_ is positive. CGEL explicitly distinguishes negative items from negatively oriented polarity-sensitive expressions (pp. 822–823). The expressions also differ among themselves: _a bit_ has an ordinary affirmative use.

**Recommended decision:** retain peripheral _a_, but make its contribution a stated convention of the construction, alongside its already restricted distribution. This saves the complex lexical determinative, not every construction-specific semantic condition. Neither non-compositional meaning nor polarity behaviour alone decides lexical unity.

**Proposed addition to §3.2:**

> The combination also has its own polarity: bare _few_ and _little_ are negative, whereas _a few_ and _a little_ are positive (CGEL, pp. 393–394). Peripheral _a_ licenses a positive quantificational interpretation in these constructions. Polarity therefore depends on the combination, not simply on the position occupied by _few_ or _little_. This interpretation remains a convention of the construction under the proposed syntactic decomposition.

The initial revision left the minimizer comparison in these notes. Following Claude's further comment, the manuscript now gives the reply in two sentences: conventional interpretation doesn't force lexical unity, and *a bit* illustrates that compatibility without having the same polarity-licensing relation as *few*.

Keep those totals as search provenance, not a probability or measured acceptability contrast.

The saved NOW lines nevertheless contain anaphoric _every_ without a following nominal in published prose, including two cases confirmed against their publication pages. ## 4. Such: a definite attribution error and a defensible extension
CGEL p. 435 assigns ordinary kind _such_, including _many such problems_, to Adjective. Both its kind and degree readings are adjectival there. Footnote 52 reserves Determinative for the legal-register use allowing a count singular without _a_. Claude is right; the present sentence must change.

Calling the NP uses discussed here D is coherent with our existing degree-_such_ reanalysis. Kind and degree readings share _such a person_ and _such people_; treating the readings as determinatives avoids a category boundary within that overlapping distribution. Internal modifier use in _many such problems_ is compatible with D, just as internal modification is available to _few_ and cardinals. It doesn't become Det merely because we recategorize the word.

This is a case for extending **our** treatment, not a decisive test against CGEL. Lack of grade doesn't exclude Adjective; predeterminer position admits adjectives; and CGEL already groups the two ordinary readings together as adjectives. Our positive case remains the shared distribution with the proposed determinative system, including its restricted functions. It should be presented at that strength.

I recommend treating both ordinary NP-internal readings as D here and correcting the attribution explicitly. I would leave a universal claim about every use of _such_ open: predicative _Such is life_ and _such … that_ constructions need their own consideration before “all” can cover them.

**Proposed replacement for the misattributed sentence:**

> I treat both degree and kind _such_ as determinatives in the NP constructions discussed here, with predeterminer use in _such a person_ and internal modifier use in _many such problems_. This extends the reclassification beyond degree _such_: CGEL treats both ordinary readings as adjectives, reserving determinative status for legal-register uses with a bare count singular (p. 435 and n. 52). The internal modifier use preserves its function under the change of category.

Keep the separate argument about exclamative _what_. Adjust the earlier degree-only descriptions so that the extension's scope is clear, without claiming that every non-NP construction has now been analysed.
