## The argument decision

Three opening readers recovered the same central problem and all advanced it to full review. The full board's most useful question is what makes inheritance through Noun preferable to the separate-D account that already states shared nominal projection once. Both readers who received only the artifact, goal and audience raised this without being given the named board's diagnostic frame. That makes it worth addressing; it does not make their preferred remedy authoritative.

The broader adjective comparison is the concrete pressure point. CGEL pp. 416–417 covers more than the human-class and comparative/superlative examples in this draft. The first row below gives the determinative side of the comparison; the remaining rows describe adjectival constructions in CGEL. Those rows use its descriptions, not new invented judgments:

| CGEL category | Family | What the current evidence provides | What the argument still has to assess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Determinative | Demonstratives and quantifiers | Independent uses across numerous established forms; partitives and permitted modifier constructions; lexical exceptions retained. | What combination gives this pattern category-level weight? |
| Adjective | Superlative and definite comparative adjectives | Ranking interpretations, external determination and partitive possibilities. | Whether their restrictions differ in kind or merely in extent from determinative restrictions. |
| Adjective | Ordinal adjectives | Independent use permits indefinite determination, including *a second*. | A definite-article requirement cannot characterize adjectival independence generally. |
| Adjective | Colour and provenance modifiers | Bare or definitely determined independent NPs, including *blue* and *Polish* in a supplied discourse contrast. | Breadth has to be compared across these families too, with contexts held comparable. |
| Adjective | Age and size adjectives | Bare independent *old* and *small* in the source's contrastive contexts, with restrictions on other adjectives and indefinite determination. | The argument cannot use bare independence alone to separate the categories. |
| Adjective | Human-class adjectives | The familiar definite-article and interpretation restrictions remain. | This is one controlled comparison, not the entire adjectival baseline. |

Source checked directly: [CGEL, printed pp. 416–417](</Users/brettreynolds/projects/LLM-CLI-projects/literature/huddlestonpullum2002.pdf>). Text was extracted from PDF pages 436–437; no page image was opened. The manuscript's local statement about the human-class construction is accurate. The issue is the breadth of the larger comparison.

I would develop this in place of the final paragraph of §4.4, with the membership criterion stated in §3.1 and applied again in §7.3. I would not add a new empirical section, a feature score or a necessary-and-sufficient test merely to satisfy the reviews. The comparison needs to make the grammatical reason for the boundary explicit and then assess both determinatives and adjectives by it. The evidence above establishes the need for that comparison; it does not yet supply the winning criterion.

Two related decisions belong in the same revision. First, §5's requirement that an intermediate pronoun category collect otherwise repeated generalizations needs to use the same standard as the Noun argument. Both hierarchies can be mimicked through features or cross-category rules. Second, the articles' substitutional and semantic connections differ from the inflectional *my/mine* connection. The two-step argument is valid if its premises hold; the positive grounds for retaining articles within determinative need comparison with redistribution rather than an assertion of equivalence between those kinds of paradigm.
