# Unification hunt: what one Noun category states once that *CGEL* states twice
<!-- SUMMARY: candidates for the argument shape elevated in §5.5 (a phenomenon CGEL handles separately for nouns and determinatives that becomes one statement under D-noun), each checked against CGEL's text and the manuscript · status: for Brett to choose from; nothing applied · updated: 2026-09-17 -->

The shape: a phenomenon *CGEL* must state twice, once inside Noun and once for determinatives, that becomes one statement when determinatives are a Noun subcategory. Economy of categories doesn't count; a rule stated once does. Each candidate below was checked against *CGEL*'s wording (pages from the PDF) and against what the manuscript already says.

## The pattern behind the candidates

*CGEL* crosses its own determinative/noun boundary by secondary use or conversion in three places, and in each the member is one D-noun places in Noun from the start:

1. Compound determinatives: "There are one or two nouns formed by conversion from compound determinatives: *He's a nobody*, *Would you care for a little something?*" (p. 423, n. 43). COCA shows the use is productive (*an anonymous someone*, *a furry something*, *such an anyone*); now in §4.5 and §5.5.
2. Proper nouns: "As a set-denoting noun, the name *Mary* then takes a full range of dependents, including determiners and restrictive modifiers" (p. 521). Already the parallel in §4.5.
3. Cardinal numerals: "The cardinal numerals are primarily determinatives but they have a secondary use in which they inflect for number and hence belong in the noun category: *They set off in threes* / *enrolled in their hundreds*" (p. 385).

Under D-noun, all three are the same secondary set-denoting use of a noun, and the third needs no category change at all. The manuscript cites \textcite{reynolds2026numerals} for both uses of cardinals (§4.4, §5.4) but treats their unification as "conditional on that analysis". *CGEL*'s own sentence makes it unconditional in the relevant respect: *CGEL* already says plural cardinals are nouns; the question is only whether they changed category to get there.

## Candidates, ranked

### 1. Cardinals' plural use (strong; new to the paper in this form)

Add to §5.5's unification paragraph: the plural cardinal (*in threes*, *in their hundreds*, *the hundreds*) joins the compound and the proper noun as a third case where *CGEL* invokes a category shift and D-noun invokes none. One sentence plus the p. 385 quotation. This also converts §5.4's conditional numeral unification into an instance of the paper's general claim, with Reynolds (2026) as the independent argument that cardinals have both uses.

### 2. Head and phrasal genitives on compounds (moderate; and it removes a hedge)

*CGEL* §16.6 (p. 479) defines head genitives as marked "by the inflection of the head noun" and lists *everyone's responsibility* beside *Edward's daughter*, with phrasal *everyone else's* and *somebody local's* beside *the King of England's*. On *CGEL*'s own categorization the head of *everyone's* is a determinative, so the definition is stretched at exactly this point; under D-noun the head is a noun and one definition covers both columns. The phrasal cases also bear on the manuscript's hedge at §2.5 ("compounds such as *someone* and *something* may owe their genitives to the nominal component", pp. 423–424, 479–480): in *everyone else's* and *somebody local's* the suffix follows the postmodifier, so it is the NP's genitive, not the *-one* component's. The hedge can be dropped and the connection stated as a unification. Corpus check if wanted: `[at*] [j*] someone/somebody/something 's`? no; `everyone else's`, `somebody else's` counts are enough.

### 3. NP as degree modifier (present as a cost comparison; reframe)

§5.3 already shows NPs modifying adjectives (*a great deal better*, *heaps better*, temporal *this week*) beside determinative *much better*, *enough*. *CGEL* needs a DP-modifier permission and an NP-modifier permission for the same slot; D-noun needs one. Reframe the section's conclusion as the unification rather than as "not a cost".

### 4. Peripheral modification (present as a parallel; reframe)

§4.4 says approximative and focusing modifiers "have established NP parallels" (*almost every* beside *only you*, *even Kim*). Under *CGEL*, *almost* attaches to a DP in one case and to an NP in the other; under D-noun both are NP-peripheral. One clause in §5.5.

### 5. Partitive complements and number transparency (the paper's core; reframe)

*Some of the wine* and *a lot of the wine*: two structures in *CGEL* (fused-head DP with partitive PP; noun with PP complement), one under D-noun, and the transparency rule ("the NP's number is the complement's") stated once for *lot*, *number*, *some*. This is already the paper's strongest positive comparison; it can be named as the first unification rather than a connection.

### 6. Determiner function: fewer realizing categories (structural; already in §5.2)

*CGEL* lets Det be realized by DP, genitive NP, or PP; D-noun by NP or PP (*the*, *Kim's*, *my*, *someone's*, *what size*; PP as in the paper's §5.2). Corrected by Brett 2026-09-17: not NP alone. Already the shared-projection proposal; a reduction of realizing categories, not a rule stated once, so the weakest of the six.

## Precedent, not unification

*CGEL* defines pronouns as nouns that "allow a much narrower range of dependents, and in particular they do not combine with determiners" (p. 328), and keeps proper nouns in Noun despite their determinerless primary use. The manuscript already uses the *my*/*mine* precedent (pp. 470–471) for restricted forms. That is the right answer to "why call articles nouns if they can't head an argument NP": restriction within Noun is *CGEL*'s own practice.

## Recommendation

Write candidate 1 into §5.5 now (one sentence and a quotation), and drop the genitive hedge in §2.5 with candidate 2's argument. Then re-present 3, 4 and 5 in §5.5 as instances of the same pattern, so the assessment reads as one argument with five cases rather than a list of connections. That is the paper's answer to "so what": five places where *CGEL* states a rule twice and the taxonomy states it once.
