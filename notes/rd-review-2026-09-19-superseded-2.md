**Keywords:** determinatives; nouns; lexical categories; noun phrases; English
# The question and the alternatives {#sec:intro}
What's the categorial relationship among words such as _some_, _me_, _apple_, and _Brett_? I argue that all four are nouns: determinatives form a coordinate subcategory alongside common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns. I write the superordinate category as Noun.

I adopt the general framework of _The Cambridge grammar of the English language_ (_CGEL_; [@huddleston2002]). Unlike _CGEL_, though, I include determinatives within Noun. The claims concern synchronic English lexical categories; examples without a source are constructed, and attested examples name theirs.[^2]

I use [determinative]{.smallcaps} for the category containing articles, demonstratives, and quantifiers such as _the_, _this_, _some_, _every_, and _many_.[^3] I reserve [determiner]{.smallcaps} for the syntactic function within the noun phrase (NP) characteristically performed by phrases headed by these words. This distinction separates what kind of word _some_ is from what syntactic relationships its phrase participates in.

Compare _take some apples_ with _take some_. The word _some_ remains determinative in both. Before _apples_, its phrase functions as determiner; without _apples_, the whole expression functions as object.

In these NP comparisons, [dependent]{.smallcaps} describes a determinative's use as determiner or internal modifier with a nominal target; [independent]{.smallcaps} describes use without that target. An independent expression can still have dependents of its own, as in _some of the wine_, and rely on context for its interpretation.

The taxonomic choice. _CGEL_ makes determinative (D) a primary category beside Noun; the proposal makes it a fourth subcategory within Noun. How independent uses are structured is a separate question (Table [1](#tab:accounts)).

Two choices are involved. The first is whether determinatives belong within Noun. The second concerns the Head relations within independent NPs. In _CGEL_, the determinative phrase (DP) headed by independent _some_ jointly fills determiner and Head, a [fusion of functions]{.smallcaps} [@huddleston2002 410--412]. The alternative has _some_ head a nominal (Nom) directly, with Nom heading NP: the [ordinary-Head analysis]{.smallcaps}.

I call the inclusion of determinatives within Noun the [D-noun categorization]{.smallcaps}, D-noun for short. The [D-noun analysis]{.smallcaps} proposed here combines it with ordinary headedness. Noun membership makes ordinary NP structure a natural treatment; that structural regularity strengthens the case for the grouping.

Neither commitment, though, requires the other: a noun's projection, the phrase it heads, can fill a fused function, and a grammar can keep determinative (D) separate while assigning ordinary Head. Earlier accounts connect articles with pronouns or give both nominal structure, at different analytical levels (Appendix [8](#sec:historical){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:historical"}); @vaneynde2003determiner argues from inflection that determiners are heterogeneous, some adjectives and some nouns (§[2.6](#sec:membership){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:membership"}). Relative to those accounts, what's new is the scope and the pairing: the full inventory of _CGEL_'s determinative category, articles included, as one coordinate subcategory of Noun; ordinary Head for its independent uses; and an explicit comparison of the four combinations of taxonomy and Head analysis, through a matched fragment and a coverage audit. Hudson's nested noun taxonomy is considered in §[6.2](#sec:inheritance){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inheritance"}; Spinillo's restricted article grouping in §[3](#sec:articles){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:articles"}.

The paper advances two separable claims. Sections [2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"}--[3](#sec:articles){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:articles"} argue that determinatives belong within Noun; §[4](#sec:evidence){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:evidence"} compares ordinary Head with fusion; §[5](#sec:economy){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:economy"} compares the four implementations and asks what further economy the taxonomy contributes. Section [6](#sec:consequences){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:consequences"} develops the secondary-use schema and considers coordinate versus nested subcategories.

Both Head analyses give the whole independent expression NP status. Their difference concerns its internal structure. In the separate-D ordinary-Head implementation, words of category D project NP (§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:det-uniform"}).

Lexical categories and syntactic functions cut across one another. An NP can function as determiner, as in _[Kim's]{.underline} book_, and a determinative-headed phrase can function as modifier, as in _the [many]{.underline} people_ [@payne2010; @pullummiller2022nps].

The DP hypothesis of @abney1987 concerns a different Head relation. Under that hypothesis, D heads expressions such as _some apples_. I retain noun-headed NPs and reject the DP analysis for English, following the arguments of @pullummiller2022nps and @bruening2020nominal. Here DP means [determinative phrase]{.smallcaps}, as in _CGEL_.

# Grounds for a broader Noun category {#sec:proposal}
The existing Noun category contains common nouns, proper nouns, and pronouns with markedly different meanings, inflection, and dependents. Ordinary count nouns alone are an inadequate comparison group. The question is how determinatives connect with that broader range, and how those connections compare with their affinities to adjectives.

{++Like many modern treatments of English,++}{id="s1" by="user" at="2026-09-19T23:33:48.617Z"} _CGEL_ includes pronouns within Noun on the basis of their phrases' functions, despite differences from common and proper nouns in inflection and dependents [@huddleston2002 327--328]. The inclusion of auxiliaries within Verb supplies a further precedent for preserving distinctive properties within a broader lexical category [@pullumwilson1977]. These precedents motivate comparing the whole grammatical [profile]{.smallcaps}, the functions, dependents, forms, and meanings a category's members show, rather than requiring every member to display each nominal property.

Shared external functions alone don't determine lexical category. The comparison weighs three considerations: how broadly a property occurs, how it recurs across constructions, and how specifically it connects the groups. These considerations apply equally to nominal and adjectival connections. A pattern concentrated in a few words can be substantial evidence; its contribution depends on its grammatical relationships as well as its reach.

Four kinds of evidence recur in this comparison, and they establish different things. A _CGEL_ description records an established possibility; a starred form records a judgment; a positive attestation records an occurrence, in a register and a context; and a bounded search with no result records absence where opportunity was frequent, which supports an exclusion in proportion to the opportunity. The category comparison rests on the constructions established in the first two ways and on their recurrence across the inventory; attestations and searches inform, or motivate a restatement of, individual exclusions.
## The connection from quantificational common nouns {#sec:quant-nouns}
Quantificational common nouns make the connection more specific than shared quantity meanings. I use [quantifier]{.smallcaps} across lexical categories for expressions specifying quantity. In _a lot of the delegates_ and _many of the delegates_, both heads quantify over a [partitive domain]{.smallcaps}: the whole from which a quantity is drawn, here the delegates. But _lot_ also permits _a lot of delegates_, whereas *_many of delegates_ is excluded [@huddleston2002 349].

Agreement provides another connection. Compare _A lot of the people were waiting_ and _Some of the people were waiting_, with plural agreement, against _A lot of the work was finished_ and _Some of the work was finished_, with singular agreement. The whole subject NP's number depends on the complement of _of_. _CGEL_ calls quantificational _lot_ [number-transparent]{.smallcaps} [@huddleston2002 349--350, 501--503].

_Each of the people_ takes singular agreement by default, and ordinary _a photograph of the people_ takes it categorically, so neither is number-transparent in _CGEL_'s sense. The contrast isn't absolute, though. Plural agreement after _each of them_ is well attested; plural verb forms account for about a sixth of the relevant COCA query hits. Plural agreement also occurs after _each of the_ with a plural noun, including in edited prose.[^5] That variation is agreement with the nearer or notional plural, the pattern familiar from _a number of the people were_, and it leaves _each_ singular; with _some of the people_, by contrast, plural agreement is the only option. Number transparency specifically connects quantificational _lot_ with number-neutral _some_.

Restricted dependents also occur on the common-noun side. _CGEL_ categorizes quantificational _plenty_ as a common noun whose use resists determination and modification; _lot_ requires _a_ and permits only limited modification [@huddleston2002 349--350]. Established common nouns thus approach the determinative profile as determinatives approach theirs.

The connection also extends beyond argument NPs. Quantificational nouns occur in degree modifiers such as _a great deal smaller_ and _plenty big enough_ [@huddleston2002 549--550]. Section [5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:enough"} compares these with determinative degree modifiers, including their attributive distribution. Meaning, complementation, agreement, and restricted dependents give the proposed grouping a specific basis within established Noun.

Common nouns also permit a wider range of PP and clausal complements, whereas pronouns and primary naming uses of proper nouns have much more restricted dependents [@huddleston2002 429--430, 439--443, 517--521]. The shared partitive in _a lot/some of the wine_ connects these different complement systems.
## The connected adjectival profile {#sec:adjectival-profile}
The strongest adjectival connection also concerns a restricted group: _few_, _many_, _much_, and _little_. They distinguish plain, comparative, and superlative forms: _few_/_fewer_/_fewest_, _many_/_more_/_most_, and corresponding paradigms for _little_ and _much_ [@huddleston2002 393--395]. _CGEL_ excepts _more_ and _less_ before adjectives and adverbs, which it classes as adverbs [@huddleston2002 395, 1123]; @reynolds2024why shows the contrasts with _much_ and _little_ that the exception denies, so the paradigm holds across their uses. Grade combines with degree-modifier selection and comparative complementation, as in _more than ten_. The three properties form a connected profile.

Degree _very_, _so_, and _too_ select gradable heads: _very tall_ and _very few_, but *_so you_, *_so the book_, and *_too my life_. These degree modifiers also exclude non-gradable determinatives: *_very every_, *_very some_, and *_very this_. The associated series includes _so few_, _too few_, _how few_, and _as few as_. These degree patterns select _few_, _many_, _much_, and _little_, the four determinatives that inflect for grade [@huddleston2002 393--395, 431--432].[^6]

With _a few_, modifier position distinguishes two attachment sites. _CGEL_ contrasts internal _very_ in _a very few mistakes_ with peripheral _quite_ in _quite a few mistakes_ [@huddleston2002 p. 392, (61)]; the reversed orders *_very a few_ and *_a quite few_ are excluded. On the analysis of §[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}, _few_ heads the phrase in Det and _very_ is an internal modifier within it. Both _a_ and _quite_ are peripheral modifiers of the larger NP, with _quite_ preceding _a_.

Approximative and focusing modifiers have a broader distribution. Compare _almost every_, _nearly all_, _hardly any_, _practically no_, _almost everyone_, and _almost ten_. They have counterparts in established NP modification: _almost the whole class_, _hardly a day_, and _only the best_. In _almost every student_, _almost_ approximates the quantity; it doesn't intensify a gradable property of _every_.

The broader approximative pattern supplies NP parallels distinct from the four quantifiers' adjectival profile. Section [4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"} develops an attachment analysis of that contrast, available under either ordinary-Head taxonomy.

Overlap with adjectives likewise needs phrase levels kept distinct. Both _the people_ and _the rich_ are NPs in _CGEL_, though only the former has a noun as lexical head. In _a soccer/round ball_, a noun-headed expression and an AdjP share modifier function; in _Jones became president/ill_, NP and AdjP share predicative complement function. External functions alone therefore don't establish nounhood.

_She is a beauty_ ascribes a property, whereas _That is Kim_ identifies a person. _CGEL_ excludes specifying _be_ clauses from its noun--adjective diagnostic because phrases from other categories occur in them too [@huddleston2002 536].

Demonstratives also occur as predicative complements of _become_: _religious leaders have suddenly become those who are regularly invited to public meetings_ and _the details become those that would add to his culpability_.[^7] These NPs combine predicative use with relative postmodification. _Become_ doesn't itself distinguish ascription from specification, and the examples are compatible with ordinary Head and fusion.

NP grammar also distinguishes predicative and argument uses. A bare-role NP such as _president_ is licensed in _I'd like to be president_, but requires determination in the corresponding object use _I'd like to meet the president_ [@huddleston2002 328]. Determinatives' asymmetries fit this broader variation within Noun.

@solt2015qadjectives treats _few_, _many_, _much_, and _little_ as adjectives of quantity and gives them one semantics, as gradable predicates of sets of degrees, across the quantificational, predicative, attributive, differential, and adverbial uses the class shows.

_Much_ and _little_ take the predicative use only with an amount-denoting subject, her _Ten gallons is not much_ against *_John's patience is much_, and that's the case usage supplies at volume: COCA's sentence-final _be much_ is mostly _it won't be much_, _pay won't be much_, _the hurt can not be much_, beside existential _there hasn't been much_, and NOW has existential _there is little_, all of them independent NP uses.

With a degree modifier the subject condition lapses: _the pain and immobility became too much to bear_, _sympathy seemed too much to hope for_, _it seemed so little to give_, the flexibility of modified _much_ that @reynolds2024why notes and the pattern of a quantity NP in predicative function, as in _a first-year festival seems a lot to ask of the public_ and _CGEL_'s _Kim isn't much of an actor_ [@huddleston2002 395].[^8]

That unity is the semantic side of this adjectival profile, and it sits as well with Noun as with Adjective: the same four carry the nominal profile of §[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-nouns"}.

The same relation holds between distribution and category. @reynolds2024why largely explains why _much_ and _more_ differ in what they modify from their semantics and the scale structure of the heads, without a category difference between them, following @payne2010 [61] on why complementary distribution alone doesn't assign category.

Bare colour expressions and evaluative comparative subjects complicate a simple distributional boundary. _CGEL_'s _Henrietta likes red shirts, and I like blue_ permits reduction licensed by coordination. The constructed _Blue is good_ raises a separate ambiguity between noun and adjective, while the attested _Bluer is better_ establishes a bare comparative subject without settling its phrase category.[^9]
## Number, genitive marking, and reference {#sec:inflection}
Inflection supplies several connections with established nouns, though no one contrast runs through the whole category. Common nouns typically distinguish singular and plural; proper nouns permit number inflection in restricted uses such as _the Smiths_ [@huddleston2002 521]. Demonstratives likewise distinguish _this_/_these_ and _that_/_those_ [@huddleston2002 373]. These are contrasts between forms of a lexeme, unlike the fixed number restrictions of _each_ and _several_.

Number needn't be inflectional wherever it matters. _CGEL_ treats singular and plural _you_ as distinct lexemes, differentiated overtly only by the reflexive forms _yourself_ and _yourselves_ [@huddleston2002 486]. Number-neutral _some_ has no corresponding singular--plural alternation. The demonstratives supply positive morphological evidence; the absence of that alternation elsewhere doesn't distinguish determinatives from pronouns as whole categories.

The demonstrative number contrasts remain visible in independent uses. Generic human _the rich_, by contrast, is a plural NP whose adjective lacks number inflection and still takes adverb modifiers: _the very rich_ [@huddleston2002 418]. The difference concerns overt paradigms, rather than the mere possession of a number value by the whole NP.

Case gives a further partial connection. Genitives occur across the proposed subcategories: _dog's_, _Kim's_, _my_, and _someone's_. Personal pronouns have fuller case paradigms. Most determinatives lack case inflection. The compounds' genitives belong to the NP: _CGEL_ lists _everyone's_ beside _Edward's_ among head genitives, marked by "the inflection of the head noun", and _everyone else's_ and _somebody local's_ beside _the King of England's_ among phrasal genitives, with the suffix after the postmodifier [@huddleston2002 479]. On _CGEL_'s categorization the head of _everyone's_ is a determinative, so that definition is stretched at this point; within Noun it isn't. This supports the compounds' nominal affinity more directly than the category's membership as a whole.

Simple forms such as *_many's_ and *_some's_ resist genitive marking. _CGEL_ suggests that fusion supplies a reason: the form has to meet the case requirements of both Det and Head [@huddleston2002 420]. Its analogy with case matching in fused relatives [@huddleston2002 1073--1074] motivates that suggestion, but the restriction still depends on the case conditions assigned to the determinative use. Genitive NPs themselves freely fill Det.

The ordinary-Head account states the restriction on these forms lexically. Noun membership doesn't guarantee a genitive paradigm: reflexive pronouns likewise lack genitive forms [@huddleston2002 426]. That precedent establishes compatibility with Noun, without explaining the determinatives' particular restriction.

Meaning and reference vary within Noun. _Apple_ supplies descriptive content; _Kim_, in its primary naming use, identifies through a name; _she_ depends on context [@huddleston2002 425--428, 516--521]. Many determinatives also have pro-form uses. Determinative meanings range from deictic _this_ to quantitative _many_ and universal _every_ [@huddleston2002 358--360, 370--405]. Deixis connects demonstratives with pronouns, while quantity connects determinatives with common nouns such as _number_ and _majority_.

Semantic affinities contribute to the comparison without settling its boundaries: _every_ quantifies over a nominal restriction, while _Kim_ identifies an individual.

Pro-form gender [@reynolds2025proformgender] connects these ways of referring. Descriptions such as _the woman_ and names such as _Kim_ identify referents whose construal bears on pro-form choice. Pronouns and determinatives express contrasts between personal _she_/_somebody_ and non-personal _it_/_something_. Relative _who_/_which_ supplies another contrast.[^10]

Interrogative and relative properties are distinct from functions such as object. An interrogative object may be headed by a pronoun (_who_) or a common noun (_which book_); the latter obtains its interrogative property from a dependent.

Inflection and reference provide partial connections, with different reach. Demonstrative number directly compares forms of determinative lexemes with nominal number paradigms; compound genitives follow the head and phrasal pattern of noun-headed NPs. The _no_/_none_ alternation concerns form selection, discussed with restricted membership in §[3.3](#sec:form-selection){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:form-selection"}.
## Functions and constructional range {#sec:independent}
NPs serve as subjects, objects, and complements of prepositions; independent determinative expressions share these functions.

Independent use is widespread within the determinative inventory. _CGEL_ discusses such uses for demonstratives and quantifiers including _some_, _all_, _both_, _many_, _few_, _several_, _each_, _either_, _neither_, _much_, and _enough_, while recording lexical restrictions and the separate forms _no_/_none_ [@huddleston2002 371--372, 410--424]. The generalization concerns the availability of independent constructions across a lexical category, not unrestricted acceptability in every sentence frame.[^11]

NPs containing fused-head adjective phrases (AdjPs) fill the same positions. _The rich_ and _the poor_ are nominal arguments, and comparative and superlative adjectives head expressions without a generic human interpretation: _CGEL_'s _the most important of her criticisms_ is an NP containing a partitive _of_-phrase [@huddleston2002 332--333, 416--423].

Independent _some_ can form a one-word NP, whereas an NP with _rich_ as fused Head requires the definite article _the_ on the generic human reading [@huddleston2002 417--418]. Singular count common nouns also need determination in argument NPs.

With possible solutions under discussion, _There are several_ places the independent quantifier in displaced-subject position; dummy _there_ is the subject [@huddleston2002 1391--1393]. Attested _there is little_ and _there hasn't been much_ place _little_ and _much_ there too (§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}). Section [2.5](#sec:quant-controls){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-controls"} tests the contrast with quantificational adjectives.

Determiner function also cuts across the existing noun subcategories. In _my preferences_, _Kim's preferences_, and _people's preferences_, the determiner is an NP ultimately headed by a pronoun, a proper noun, and a common noun respectively [@huddleston2002 354--355, 470--471].[^12]

The compound determinative _someone_, following the categorization of @Payne2007 [§1.3], participates in _someone's preferences_. Plain-case NPs also fill Det: _what size_ in _what size shoes_, _that size_ in _that size shoes_, and _Sunday_ in _Sunday morning_ [@huddleston2002 355, 357].

Determiner remains the characteristic function of determinative phrases. This specialization could support a separate primary category. Its tasks are themselves closely connected with nominal reference: Det marks definiteness and often contributes quantification [@huddleston2002 354--359]. The existing NP determiners supply a nominal comparison. In _Kim's preferences_, the embedded NP supplies an identifying anchor through its own referent; _Sunday_ and _what size_ specify a day or a dimension. Determinatives' deictic and quantitative specifications fit this nominal pattern. I take that fit to support treating their specialization in Det as a distinction within Noun.

Preposition phrases (PPs) remain a restricted alternative, as in _up to twenty minutes_ and _between fifty and sixty tanks_ [@huddleston2002 357, 433]. Number, countability, and other selectional conditions distinguish the determining expressions.

Compare the modifiers in _dog houses_, _Canada Day_, _the manager herself_, and _the few people_. Emphatic _herself_ also functions as a clause adjunct in _The manager detected the error herself_ [@huddleston2002 1496--1497]. Temporal NPs such as _that day_ and _Sunday_ supply adjuncts, as does degree _enough_ in _I hadn't prepared enough_ (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:enough"}).

Common nouns freely take integrated relatives, as in _people who came_; personal pronouns permit a restricted range, including _we who have read the report_ [@huddleston2002 430]. Determinative examples include _few who come ever leave_, _those who came_, _that which remains_, and _something that you need to know_.

Compounds also permit _anyone who asks_ and _everything that matters_. With books under discussion, compare _some that I saw_ and _two that I have seen_. _CGEL_ describes relative postmodification with demonstratives and compounds [@huddleston2002 414, 422--423].

Compound determinatives also take post-head adjectives, as in _I need something reliable and good looking_.[^13] The position and interpretation of these modifiers have specialized conditions (§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:compounds"}).
## Quantificational adjectives and nominal independence {#sec:quant-controls}
_Numerous people_ and _many people_ both quantify, but their syntax differs. Degree modification exposes a contrast: _so many mistakes_ is possible, while *_so numerous mistakes_ is excluded [@huddleston2002 539--540]. Quantity meaning and prenominal position provide a semantic control for the nominal comparison. Independent use tests whether their further distributions sustain the distinction.

_CGEL_'s boundary cuts through this semantic range. It includes _several_ among determinatives and treats quantificational _certain_ and _various_ as marginal members, partly on the evidence of partitives. Their _of_-phrases are hardly omissible, with speaker and register restrictions on the partitive uses [@huddleston2002 392--393, 411--413]. Predicative _Its advantages are several_ and _Their enemies were many_ provide further overlap, though the latter is uncommon and formal [@huddleston2002 392, 395--396].

Independent uses extend to _numerous_, _multiple_, and _countless_. Attested examples include subject _numerous were injured_, anaphoric _are there multiple?_, and _paving the way for countless who followed him_, where a relative postmodifies the complement of _for_.[^14] These selected occurrences establish overlap, without establishing equal acceptability or frequency. Assigning every independent quantity word to determinative would make independent use a circular category diagnostic.

Existentials admit fused-head AdjPs too. Adjective fusion permits indefinite ordinal _a second_ [@huddleston2002 416], as in _There was a second_. Plural agreement likewise occurs with adjective-containing _the rich_, whose adjective lacks number inflection [@huddleston2002 418]. The number transparency of _some_ and _a lot_ in §[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-nouns"} supplies a more specific comparison, with explicit count and non-count domains.

The nominal and adjectival comparisons each involve connected properties. On the noun side, quantity combines with selected complements, number transparency, and restricted dependents. On the adjective side, grade combines with degree modifiers and comparative complements. The four gradable quantifiers' limited number isn't itself a reason to discount their adjectival pattern.

@vaneynde2003determiner argues from Italian and Dutch that determiners form no category of their own: those that inflect and agree like prenominal adjectives are adjectives, and genitives and non-agreeing pronouns are nouns. The criterion is inflection and agreement shared with prenominal adjectives, and English has no agreement to share, since its adjectives don't agree.

The inflection English does have points two ways, demonstrative number toward nouns and grade toward adjectives (§§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}--[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inflection"}), and his criterion, applied to English by extrapolation (he applies it to Italian and Dutch determiners; his English examples don't test it on determiners), would make the gradable four adjectives and the rest nouns. That's the split I decline here, because the four also share partitive patterns, restricted dependents, and degree uses with quantificational common nouns.

His own treatment keeps determiner status apart from lexical category, as a marking value a functor contributes to the phrase it forms with the nominal it selects, so that an adjective and a pronoun can both be determiners without sharing a category; the question here is the different one of what category a word has when it heads the NP itself. Appendix [8](#sec:historical){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:historical"} lists the alternative beside the others.

The nominal connections recur across more of the determinative system, in more constructions and for more lexeme groups, as the claim register records lexeme by lexeme. Partitives link _many_ with _some_; relative postmodification links _few_ with demonstratives and compounds. Determination and reference connect determinatives with genitive NPs and pronouns, while demonstrative number supplies a morphological parallel. These links recur across constructions whose conditions were specified separately. The gradable quantifiers participate in this wider range alongside their adjectival pattern.

I favour Noun membership because it brings the words with these recurring nominal properties into one category. Grade and degree selection still characterize a narrower subgroup. Separate D can describe the same distributions, but places a primary boundary across those connections. Determinative retains its specialization in determination and its distinctive dependent patterns within Noun, just as pronouns retain their differences from common nouns.
## Four separable questions {#sec:four-questions}
Four questions about these words are separable, and the comparison above answers one of them. The lexical category is the question of this section: Noun. The Head analysis, ordinary Head or fusion, is the question of §[4](#sec:evidence){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:evidence"}, and a grammar can answer it either way for either category (Table [1](#tab:accounts){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:accounts"}).

The semantic type is a third. The unified semantics of _few_, _many_, _much_, and _little_ is compatible with either Noun or Adjective (§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}). The construction is a fourth: _such_ and _what_ lexically select an _a_-NP, outside the Big Mess construction (_how long a bridge_; §[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

The broader Noun grouping still needs an account of restricted members. Their inclusion depends on how they participate in the determinative system.
# Restricted members and the scope of the grouping {#sec:articles}
The category-level argument draws on constructions that many determinatives enter. Articles lack ordinary independent argument uses, so their inclusion needs a further argument: they belong to the determinative system whose broader profile supports Noun membership. The _no_/_none_ paradigm raises a related question about restrictions on forms.
## Articles within the determinative system {#sec:article-membership}
Why categorize the articles _the_ and _a_ as nouns if they can't form independent argument NPs? _Every_ is restricted too, while _no_ has the distinct independent form _none_ [@huddleston2002 371--372, 379, 410--412].

_CGEL_'s treatment of _my_ supplies a precedent for a restricted noun form [@huddleston2002 470--471].[^15] Dependent _my_ and independent _mine_ belong to one pronoun paradigm. _My_ remains a noun even though, in the ordinary NP construction, it requires a following nominal. The articles lack an independent argument form; their positive evidence is integration into determinative, and their nounhood depends on the category-level argument. The inference has two stages. The first places the articles within the determinative category: they fill the Det position the unrestricted members fill, take part in its contrasts of definiteness, quantity, and count selection, and sit inside the determinative cluster of the 2021 matrix [@reynolds2021 9--10]. The second places that category within Noun by the profile of §[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"}. The criterion for a restricted member is the first stage; nothing in it asks the articles for a use they lack.

The articles participate in the determinative system of definiteness, quantity, and count restrictions. In _the/a/this/every book_, they occupy the same Det position and contribute to the interpretation of the NP. _The_ permits singular, plural, and non-count targets; _a_ selects a singular count target and contributes individuation. Their connections extend beyond shared position, although neither has the full determinative profile [@huddleston2002 368--373].

_The_ also functions as a degree modifier. In _the bigger the better_, it modifies comparative AdjPs, as other determinatives do in _much bigger_ or _no better_ [@huddleston2002 1131--1132, 1135--1136]. This supplies a connection outside ordinary Det function while preserving its exclusion from independent argument use.

_A_ also has a restricted peripheral modifier use. In _a few mistakes_, _few_ heads the NP in Det, while _a_ modifies the larger NP headed by _mistakes_. In independent _a few_, _a_ modifies the NP headed by _few_. The singular-count target restriction belongs to _a_'s Det use; its peripheral use selects a quantificational construction. Section [3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"} develops this analysis and contrasts it with predeterminer modification in _many a_.

@spinillo2004reconceptualising [153--158] (earlier [@spinillo2000determiners]) instead retains _the_, _a_, and _every_ as an expanded article category and redistributes other determinatives among adjectives and pronouns [@spinillo2004reconceptualising 194--195]. Her grounds include dependence on a following nominal, lack of predicative and partitive uses, and limited descriptive content. She also recognizes differences within the article trio.

Category membership doesn't remove restrictions on use. _Every apple_ is grammatical, and _every_ normally requires a following nominal, unlike independent _each_ or _some_. But the saved NOW lines contain anaphoric _every_ without a following nominal in published prose, including two cases confirmed against their publication pages.[^16] These occurrences defeat a claim of complete absence, while leaving their productivity and status in standard usage unsettled. I treat dependence as the ordinary pattern and limit the fragment to that pattern.

_Every_ connects with independent _each_ through universal quantification and singular count selection. It also permits _almost_/_nearly_ and occurs after genitives in _her every move_. @spinillo2004reconceptualising [156--158] recognizes these differences from _the_ and _a_, as well as the articles' greater phonological dependence.

The ordinary dependence of the articles and _every_, and the exclusions of predicative and partitive uses, concern occurrence without a following nominal; they aren't three separate reasons for a primary category. Little descriptive content extends well beyond the trio. Meanwhile, _a_'s quantificational constructions and _every_'s modifier conditions cross the proposed article boundary. I retain the restricted members within determinative because these connections preserve a broader system without granting unrestricted use to any member.

@spinillo2004reconceptualising [140--144] draws an analogy with verbs and prepositions whose complements can be omitted. For demonstratives and quantifiers such as _some_ and _all_, occurrence before a nominal (_some teachers_) alternates with occurrence without one (_some left_). The analogy supports category continuity, but leaves the category to be established: _CGEL_, Hudson, and the D-noun analysis can all preserve it.

The argument is synchronic: the development of definite articles from demonstratives and of articles expressing cardinality from numerals [@lyons1999 331--336] bears on content and use, not on the categorization.
## Reanalysing complex determinatives {#sec:complex-determinatives}
_CGEL_ lists _a few_, _a little_, and _a great many_ as complex determinatives while recording that _a few_ "admits _very_, _good_, and _fair_" and calling the internal structure "sui generis" [@huddleston2002 392, 394]. Its case against a complex quantifier _a number of_ appeals to the potential for replacement and expansion [@huddleston2002 351--352]. Expansion is available here too: _a good few_, _a fair few_, and _a select few_ beside _a very few_.[^17]

_A little_ and _a great many_ have the same peripheral _a_. Before another nominal, _little_ and _many_ head the determining phrases; independently, they head the NPs that _a_ modifies. The modifier in _a great many_ is obligatory [@huddleston2002 394]. Definite _the few mistakes_ retains Det _the_ and internal modifier _few_ [@huddleston2002 392]. The functions differ from those in _a few mistakes_.

The combination also has its own polarity: bare _few_ and _little_ are negative, whereas _a few_ and _a little_ are positive [@huddleston2002 393--394]. Peripheral _a_ licenses a positive quantificational interpretation in these constructions. Polarity depends on the combination, not simply on the position occupied by _few_ or _little_. This interpretation remains a convention of the construction under the proposed syntactic decomposition.

Conventional polarity behaviour doesn't force lexical unity: the syntactically decomposable NP _a bit_ has a minimizer interpretation in _They didn't enjoy it a bit_ [@huddleston2002 549--550, 725]. Here _a bit_ is licensed by negation rather than itself negative [@huddleston2002 822--823]; the parallel with _a few_ is that a conventional interpretation can belong to a phrase with internal syntactic structure.

Independent _a few_ takes plural agreement [@huddleston2002 392]. That follows from plural _few_; peripheral _a_ contributes no singular-count requirement. Its distribution remains restricted: _several_ can be a modifier after a definite determiner (_its several advantages_) [@huddleston2002 392] but excludes peripheral _a_. The permitted internal modifiers likewise depend on the quantifier and the construction.

_CGEL_ records that _quite_, _not_, and _only_ combine readily with _a few_, in contrast to _several_ [@huddleston2002 392]. I retain those combinations as conditions on peripheral modification, including the order _quite a few_. The lower bound of three for _a few_ [@huddleston2002 392] remains a convention of the combination; _CGEL_ itself declines to let semantic motivation alone establish a complex unit [@huddleston2002 621].

_Such_ and exclamative _what_ enter the same reanalysis from the adjective side. _CGEL_ classes both as adjectives, exclamative _what_ because it "fails all of the determinative criteria (a)--(c)": it combines with _a_, licenses no count singular head without it, and has no partitive use [@huddleston2002 539--540]. The first two criteria test Det function, and in _such a mess_ and _what a mess_ the words aren't in Det.

_CGEL_'s inventory of predeterminer modifiers includes _all_, _both_, fractions, multipliers, "a small range of adjectives (e.g. _such_ in [ic])", and AdjPs such as _how large a piece_, and "a predeterminer modifier normally precedes a determiner" [@huddleston2002 331]. In _CGEL_ itself, then, _such a mess_ has _a_ in Det and _such_ as predeterminer modifier, and only the category of _such_ is at issue.

The same structure covers _what a mess_ and _many a man_, whose _many aCGEL_ treats as a "syntactically inert" complex determinative [@huddleston2002 394]. Each inertness fact follows from the function. Nothing intervenes between the word and _a_ (*_what long a speech_) because a predeterminer sits against the determiner of its NP, as in _all the books_ and _half a cake_, and the count singular head and singular agreement of _many a man has_ come from _a_ and _man_.

Neither _many a_ nor _what a_ has an independent use: predeterminer _many_ and _what_ require a following _a_-NP. With peripheral _a_, _few_ and _many_ retain their independent uses as well as their Det uses before another nominal [@huddleston2002 392, 394]. That _few_ can't replace _many_ (*_few a man_) is lexical, as admission to predeterminer function is anyway (*_most the books_).

So _many a_ stands to _many_ as _such a_ to _such_ and _what a_ to _what_. Each word has Det function with plural or non-count heads (_many men_, _such promise_, _what fools_) and predeterminer function with an indefinite-article NP, where the modifier is an NP, as _CGEL_'s fractions are [@huddleston2002 385 n. 25], or an AdjP (_how large a piece_). Neither degree _such_ nor exclamative _what_ has grade or degree modification (*_sucher_, *_a very such mess_), and exclamative _what_ has no predicative use.

@VanEynde_2007_BigMess [§5] draws the same line from the other side: _such a_ and _what a_ aren't instances of the Big Mess construction (_how long a bridge_), since _what_ and _such_ "are invariably lexical" and "lexically select a nominal which is either unmarked or introduced by the indefinite article".

I treat exclamative _what_ and both degree and kind _such_ as determinatives in the NP constructions discussed here. The two readings of _such_ share _such a person_ and _such people_; kind _such_ also functions as an internal modifier in _many such problems_, as determinatives such as _few_ and cardinals can. _CGEL_ treats both ordinary readings as adjectives, reserving determinative status for legal-register uses with a bare count singular [@huddleston2002 435 and n. 52]. Their reclassification here preserves the distinctions among Det, predeterminer, and internal modifier functions.

This extension concerns the NP uses just compared; I leave the categorization of predicative _such_ and _such ... that_ constructions open.

With peripheral _a_ in _a few_ and predeterminer _many_ in _many a_, none of the four remains a complex determinative in this account. Of _CGEL_'s other candidates, cardinals above a hundred are the subject of @reynolds2026numerals, and I leave aside _a certain_, which it adds tentatively [@huddleston2002 393].
## Dependent and independent forms {#sec:form-selection}
_No_/_none_ illustrates a restriction on forms within a paradigm. _CGEL_ treats them as inflectional forms of one determinative: dependent _no students_ contrasts with independent _none_, while both permit _almost_ [@huddleston2002 389--390]. The partitive _none of the students_ requires the independent form; *_no of the students_ is excluded. Form selection remains necessary, just as with _my_/_mine_.

The broader grouping preserves two distinctions: between members' permitted constructions, and between the forms selected within a paradigm. Whether independent expressions have ordinary Head or fusion remains a separate question.
# Ordinary Head, fusion, and modifier attachment {#sec:evidence}
The D-noun analysis pairs the proposed categorization with ordinary Head. This section compares that treatment with fusion in independent _some_, _some of the wine_, and _the lucky few_. Ordinary headedness gives the determinative Head alone; fusion combines Head with a dependent function.
## The basic Head relations {#sec:head-relations}
A [nominal]{.smallcaps} (Nom) contains a head and its internal dependents, excluding an external determiner. In _some apples_, the word _apples_ heads Nom, and that Nom heads NP. The same arrangement permits an internal modifier, as in _some red apples_, without making the determining phrase part of the Nom.

Head is the function of an immediate constituent within a phrase. The [ultimate lexical head]{.smallcaps} is the word reached by following Head relations through the intervening phrase layers. Thus _apples_ is the ultimate lexical head of _some apples_, although the NP's immediate Head is Nom. Both accounts also have _some_ as the ultimate lexical head of independent _some_.

A standard tree has unique parentage: each constituent below the root has one parent. Fusion allows branches to converge on a shared constituent with two parents. ## Syntactic completeness and contextual interpretation {#sec:saturation}
With a group of people under discussion, _Some left_, _Many came_, and _All agree_ illustrate [syntactic completeness]{.smallcaps}: an argument expression can be complete without another overt head or determiner. Bare _few_ shows the same with no group under discussion: _few would dare underestimate Aaron Finch's men_ and _few have had the opportunity to digest its contents_.[^19] In _I'll take some_, the relevant substance or set may still be supplied by discourse or the situation. Ordinary pronouns also depend on context.

Generalizing expressions such as _Many are called, few are chosen_ and _Enough is enough_ need no previously uttered common-noun phrase. Interpretation can instead depend on the situation or a generic restriction. Generic pronoun _one_, as in _One shouldn't judge_, provides a parallel without a required overt antecedent.

The comparison with ordinary pronouns and proper nouns is structural as well as interpretive. In _She left_ and _Kim left_, _CGEL_ permits an NP headed by a nominal with no determiner. Applying that structure to _Some left_ preserves the same division between a complete NP and its context-dependent reference.

Fusion represents the same complete expression differently: in @Payne2007, one constituent jointly realizes Head and a dependent function, subject to structural and adjacency conditions. Both analyses keep an overt determinative and can express the restriction supplied by context; §[5.4](#sec:costs){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:costs"} compares them on the structures they share.
## Partitives and externally determined expressions {#sec:modification}
In _some of the wine_, _some_ specifies a quantity drawn from an identifiable amount of wine. The NP _the wine_ denotes the partitive domain and is complement of _of_. A pronoun-headed NP or independent genitive NP can express the domain too: _many of them_, referring to previously mentioned people, or _some of Kim's_, referring to Kim's apples.

Compare where the common noun occurs in _some apples_ and _some of the wine_. In the partitive, _wine_ is embedded inside the _of_-phrase, so it can't be the ultimate lexical head of the whole NP. The Head relations in both analyses lead instead to _some_.

## Modifier selection and attachment {#sec:payne}
The selection and ordering contrast in §[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:adjectival-profile"} guides the attachment analysis. Approximative and focusing modifiers have established NP parallels; the degree series selects the four gradable quantifiers. Both ordinary-Head accounts can represent that difference by distinguishing NP-peripheral modification from modification inside Nom.

Peripheral modifiers attach outside an NP's internal dependents. Compare _only you_, _for almost my entire life_, and _Usually a careful driver, Anne found her mind wandering_. The modifiers precede any determiner belonging to the NP they modify. _CGEL_ assigns them NP-level attachment [@huddleston2002 430--431]. Focusing _even_ and _just_, and approximatives such as _nearly_, _hardly_, _virtually_, and _practically_, likewise have NP uses.

In _almost every experienced teacher_, _almost_ belongs with _every_, while _experienced_ modifies _teacher_. Figure [5](#fig:every){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:every"} places _almost_ at the periphery of the NP headed by _every_. That smaller NP functions as Det in the larger NP, whose ultimate head is _teacher_.

Peripheral _almost_ modifies the smaller NP headed by _every_; the resulting _almost every_ NP functions as Det. The adjective _experienced_ modifies the common-noun nominal. Internal structure within the one-word modifier phrases is suppressed.

@payne2010 [40--42] defend keeping _few_, _any_, and related forms in one lexical category across dependent and independent uses. In _hardly any money_ and independent _hardly any_, _hardly_ remains an adverb and _any_ retains its category. The D-noun analysis preserves that continuity. It also preserves their contrast between adverbial premodification, as in _almost anybody_, and adjectival postmodification, as in _nothing absolute_ [@payne2010 41--42].

The proposed analysis gives adverbs such as _very_ and _too_ internal attachment in dependent and independent uses alike. In _the very few people_, _very_ modifies the Nom headed by _few_; the resulting _very few_ NP functions as Mod in the larger NP. In _the very few who objected_, _few_ remains ordinary Head and _very_ remains internal. The degree relation is unchanged, and one internal condition covers both constructions. The same analysis applies to _the very many_.

NP degree modifiers provide a further connection. Determinatives permit determinative premodifiers such as _this_ in _this much_ [@huddleston2002 393]. Comparative determinatives also take established NP modifiers: _a lot_ in _a lot fewer_, alongside _CGEL_'s _a lot more than fifty_ [@huddleston2002 432]. These modify the quantity expressed by the head. Under the proposed analysis, they're internal NP modifiers within its nominal projection.

@payne2010 [42--47] establish that adverbs can postmodify common nouns, as in their _the weather recently_ (p. 47). They also note one adverb that appears to premodify a noun, _almost_ before _textbook_ in _an almost textbook case_ [@payne2010 p. 75, n. 3]. Adverbial modification therefore isn't categorically excluded from Noun, even internally. Its broader availability with determinatives remains a difference of distribution within the proposed category.

Approximatives also need internal attachment where they follow an external determiner on an independent cardinal: _the almost thirty who came_. The internal AdvP conditions at issue are thus the degree series on _few_, _many_, _much_, and _little_ across dependent and independent uses, and approximatives on externally determined independent cardinals.

The relative clauses in §[2.4](#sec:independent){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:independent"} are ordinary postmodifiers, distinct from the specialized restrictor. When both occur, the relative follows it: _something useful that I found_. _CGEL_ gives compounds the common noun's range of ordinary postmodifiers while preserving this ordering condition [@huddleston2002 423].

The same division applies to _someone_ and _everybody_: the compound construction licenses the post-head restrictor, while the determinative base supplies its premodifier conditions. The example _something reliable and good looking_ illustrates the post-head pattern (§[2.4](#sec:independent){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:independent"}).

Both accounts separate the modifier domains structurally: fusion uses the DP--Nom boundary, and ordinary headedness uses the NP--Nom boundary. Their constituent groupings differ: ordinary Head groups _anyone present_ in the inner NP, whereas fusion groups _hardly anyone_ in DP.
## Independent genitives and retained fusion {#sec:genitive-head}
_CGEL_ also uses fusion with nouns. Anaphoric _mine_, understood as 'my car', combines the possessive relation with an understood nominal description and receives a fused analysis. Predicative _mine_, as in _it's mine_, can instead mean 'belongs to me': it expresses the relation directly, without an understood nominal head [@huddleston2002 410--411, 469--471]. A suitable context may permit either reading.

The proposed analysis gives _mine_ ordinary Head in both anaphoric and predicative uses. Its genitive form expresses a relation to the speaker; context can supply the understood nominal description, as it can with independent _few_. The _my_/_mine_ alternation remains a form-selection condition, parallel to _no_/_none_. This extends ordinary headedness to an existing noun subcategory.

The genitive's person identifies the possessor; the independent NP's agreement follows the possessed entity or entities. Compare _Mine is ready_ ('my contribution') and _Mine are ready_ ('my slides'). Ordinary Head needs a constructional agreement condition.

Anaphoric genitives also need a condition on complements. _CGEL_ contrasts the following NPs [@huddleston2002 420--421, (36ii), (37iib)]: []{#ex:genitive-complements label="ex:genitive-complements"} _Alice's performance of the Schubert and_ [_Helen's of the Rachmaninov_] _Alice's attempt to play the Schubert and_ [_Helen's the Rachmaninov_] In the first, _Helen's_ is understood as Helen's performance, and the following _of_-PP has the complement relation licensed by _performance_. The second can't omit _attempt to play_ while retaining the bare object of _play_. The grammatical NP also occurs outside coordination, after _immediately after_, where clausal gapping is excluded [@huddleston2002 420--421, (37i)].

I allow the anaphoric genitive construction to recover the antecedent's nominal description and its complement frame, without inheriting complements of verbs embedded within it. The recovered frame supplements the overt head's dependent permissions. This condition, like the agreement condition, is an additional commitment of the ordinary-Head extension to genitives. Functional fusion marks the recovered dependent relation, but also requires an account of which complements that relation licenses.

_CGEL_ also considers ordinary Head for independent _many_, with no change of category, but rejects this as a general replacement for fusion [@huddleston2002 421]. Its counterexamples are _I prefer cotton shirts to_ [_nylon_] and _I earn three times the amount Bill does, and Mary earns_ [_double_]. Both words are nouns in its account, but _nylon_ is understood as a modifier of _shirts_, and _double_ as a predeterminer multiplier.

I retain fusion for those constructions. The proposal concerns the determinative constructions compared here, with a further extension to independent genitives. Its advantage comes from their shared structure; nounhood alone doesn't establish ordinary Head in every construction.

Ordinary Head gives the independent determinatives one Head chain while preserving their distinct modifier conditions. Both taxonomies can use that structure; §[5](#sec:economy){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:economy"} asks whether Noun membership contributes further economy.

Target restrictions apply to the nominal being determined or modified, including a nominal headed by an independent determinative. Thus _the_ permits plural _few_ in _the few_. Determinative-headed phrases also bear number and count properties: _few_ is plural count, while _some_ takes its interpretation from the construction and domain. @hudson2004determiners [7--8] distinguishes dependency from external headedness. He argues that determiner and common noun depend on each other, although only one connects the phrase to its surroundings. The temporal-adjunct evidence he takes from @vanlangendonck1994 supports common-noun headedness: _I saw him that day_ is possible, whereas *_I saw him that point in time_ isn't, despite the similar temporal meanings [@hudson2004determiners 10--12]. The lexical noun matters, as well as the determiner restrictions.

Reciprocal selection is compatible with this headedness. A singular count noun normally requires determination, while _every_ normally requires another nominal and _some_ doesn't. The noun can remain Head while both constituents impose conditions.

In the external uses listed above, an expression has to pass three checks: permission for its use, selection of its form, and satisfaction of target and dependent conditions. An occurrence is admissible in an external function just when all three checks succeed. Permissions are indexed to the lexeme of the phrase's lexical head. The form and condition checks may consult the surrounding structure, including the target: independent _none_ is selected in _none of the students_, and _apples_ is the target for _some_'s phrase in _some apples_.
## Phrase categories and structural constraints {#sec:det-uniform}
In _some apples_ and _Kim's apples_, the determining phrases share the NP category under D-noun with ordinary Head. For D-noun with fusion, I retain that NP category and add the combined function relations in independent uses. _CGEL_ instead distinguishes DP from genitive NP. It also admits restricted plain-case NPs and PPs as determiners, as in _what size hat_ and _over thirty ties_ [@huddleston2002 Ch. 5, §4].

I carry this PP-determiner provision over to the three NP-projecting implementations for this comparison. Its inclusion in the alternatives is an extrapolation.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{\textit{CGEL}:} &\quad \mathrm{Det}:{\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{NP},\mathrm{PP}} \ \text{NP-projecting implementations here:} &\quad \mathrm{Det}:{\mathrm{NP},\mathrm{PP}} \end{aligned}$$

NP projection consolidates the principal phrase types in Det function; determinative-headed, genitive, and other licensed NPs are identified separately, and all four implementations have at most one Det per NP with the same definiteness and target-selection conditions. In the separate-D ordinary-Head account, an NP's ultimate lexical head can be Noun or D.

The ordinary-Head accounts share three structural conditions. First, an NP core has a Nom, the layer inside NP, as Head and at most one Det, the determiner function. Second, a nominal core has a lexical Head and only the dependents permitted for that head in its construction. Third, peripheral modification forms an NP with the modifier as dependent and another NP as Head, as in Figure [5](#fig:every){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:every"}. This keeps peripheral modification outside the core's Det and Nom dependents.

Where a word occurrence $h$ directly fills Head in Nom, let $\operatorname{Cat}(h)$ record its primary lexical category. Subcategory distinctions remain available. The two taxonomies impose different category conditions:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{D-noun, ordinary Head:} &\quad \operatorname{Cat}(h)=\mathrm{N} \ \text{Separate D, ordinary Head:} &\quad \operatorname{Cat}(h)\in{\mathrm{N},\mathrm{D}}. \end{aligned}$$

Every account states this condition for common nouns heading Nom. Fusion is held fixed for adjectives and the nominal _nylon_ and _double_ constructions of §[4.6](#sec:genitive-head){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:genitive-head"} in all four implementations, so what the two ordinary-Head implementations differ in is how they remove the determinative fusion: D-noun moves the determinatives one node down the taxonomy and keeps the condition as it is, while separate D keeps D a primary category and adds the disjunct. The coverage audit marks the cells that use it (§[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:overall-assessment"}). Both permit the same nominal layers, selected complements, and ordered modifiers. For anaphoric genitives, the construction also permits complements licensed by the recovered nominal description (§[4.6](#sec:genitive-head){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:genitive-head"}); the overt lexical head's permissions alone don't license them.

The dependent conditions distinguish uses before another nominal from independent uses. In Det or internal Mod function before a target, ordinary _some_ and _few_ exclude external determination, partitive complements, and relative postmodifiers within their own phrase. Independent _some_ and _few_ permit selected partitives and relatives; independent _few_ also permits definite determination and adjectival premodification, as in _the lucky few_. These dependent conditions don't transfer to _every_ or the articles.

Degree-modifier selection applies across the relevant dependent and independent uses. It includes AdvPs such as _very_, determinative-headed modifiers such as _this_ in _this much_, and established NPs such as _a lot_ in _a lot fewer_. Comparative complements, as in _more than ten_, are licensed separately from partitive _of_-phrases. Neither the category NP nor a general modifier slot licenses arbitrary adjectives or unrestricted complements.

Postmodification permits zero or more ordered dependents, subject to the construction. The compounds permit at most one specialized post-head restrictor followed by ordinary postmodifiers: _useful_ precedes _that I found_ in _something useful that I found_. In their primary use they exclude external determination and a corresponding pre-head adjective; the set-denoting use of §[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:secondary-use"} (_a special someone_) falls outside the construction. Their determinative bases supply the premodifier conditions (§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:compounds"}).

The approximatives retain the two attachment sites motivated in §[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}: peripheral NP attachment in _almost every_ and internal attachment in externally determined _the almost thirty who came_. Both ordinary-Head accounts require this distinction.

The fragment incorporates the peripheral-_a_ conditions for _a few_, _a little_, and _a great many_, including the obligatory modifier, and predeterminer _many_ in _many a_, which requires a following _a_-NP (§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

Each phrase's use permission follows its own lexical head. In _the apple_, the smaller article phrase has Det permission; the outer NP has argument permission through _apple_, whose determination requirement is satisfied. Bare *_Book arrived_ fails that requirement, while _Books arrived_ passes. The article itself lacks argument permission (Table [6](#tab:permissions){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:permissions"}).

The fusion accounts retain these use, form, selection, and ordering conditions but assign different Head relations. Let $p$ be the independent NP, $m$ its Head Nom, and $x$ the shared determinative phrase. Let $n$ be the additional Nom in the Mod--Head configuration. Write $\operatorname{Head}(p,m)$ for '$m$ fills Head in $p$', and likewise for Det and Mod. The two configurations satisfy the following relations:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Det--Head:}\quad &\operatorname{Head}(p,m),\quad \operatorname{Det}(p,x),\quad \operatorname{Head}(m,x); \ \text{Mod--Head:}\quad &\operatorname{Head}(p,m),\quad \operatorname{Mod}(m,x),\ &\operatorname{Head}(m,n),\quad \operatorname{Head}(n,x). \end{aligned}$$

Det--Head fills the NP's Det function, excluding a second Det. Mod--Head leaves that function available for an external determiner. Its additional Nom $n$ matches Figure [4](#fig:few){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:few"}: the determinative phrase fills Mod in $m$ and Head in $n$. The ordinary adjective _lucky_ separately modifies $m$. These are simultaneous relations on a shared constituent, with no sequence of fusion operations assumed.

Under separate D with fusion, $x$ is a DP whose lexical Head is D. Selected premodifiers and comparative complements belong within that DP; the independent partitive complement, compound restrictor, and ordinary postmodifiers belong to nominal structure outside it, as in Figure [6](#fig:compound){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:compound"}. The permissions for the complete independent NP still exclude articles; for _every_, the fragment represents only the ordinary dependent pattern. Possessing a dependent Det use doesn't itself license an independent fused use.

Under D-noun with fusion, $x$ is the NP projected by the determinative noun. The same joint relations connect it to the outer nominal structure. In this construction, the inner NP excludes external determination, partitives, restrictors, and relatives: any licensed independent-use dependents occur outside it, as in the separate-D fusion account. These restrictions need stating separately from the limit of one fusion configuration around the determinative's projection.

Ordinary headedness uses the determinative's NP directly for bare, partitive, and externally determined expressions. In the fusion accounts, its DP or NP occupies the further nominal structure just specified. Section [5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:overall-assessment"} assesses the relation between the taxonomy and the shared structure.

Nesting determinative inside pronoun would add pronoun to the inheritance path from Noun without changing this fragment's judgments. Applying Hudson's nesting to the full determinative inventory used here is an extrapolation; §[6.2](#sec:inheritance){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inheritance"} discusses the inventory differences.
## Degree uses outside noun phrases {#sec:enough}
When _enough_ modifies _good_ in _good enough_, the three NP-projecting implementations analyse it as an NP modifier of an adjective. This would be a cost if NPs couldn't occur in that function, or if their distribution differed systematically from that of degree determinatives. The comparison therefore includes the degree uses of established NPs. Attributive uses are the crucial test, because _CGEL_ restricts NP modifiers there.

_Enough_ tests both position and external function. It precedes a nominal in _enough money_ and can follow one in _money enough_; post-head _enough_ can't itself be premodified, as shown by *_money almost enough_ [@huddleston2002 396--397, 445].

NP modification inside an AdjP also permits post-head position. In ([[ex:temporal-adjp]](#ex:temporal-adjp){reference-type="ref" reference="ex:temporal-adjp"}), I analyse _every Tuesday_, _this week_, and _next week_ as temporal NP modifiers within the bracketed AdjPs. The separate degree modifiers and temporal specifications in the coordinated example make that attachment clear. These NPs express time, while _enough_ expresses degree; the shared position leaves their selectional differences intact.

**(2)**

a. _Applicants_ [_completely free every Tuesday_] _will be interviewed next month._ b. _Anyone_ [_very busy this week_] _but_ [_completely free next week_] _should contact me today._

_Enough_ also modifies adjectives, adverbs, verbs, and some PPs: _good enough_, _quickly enough_, _I hadn't prepared enough_, and _enough in control_ [@huddleston2002 396--397]. The degree determinatives _much_ and _little_, and _no_/_none_, likewise have uses outside NP structure [@huddleston2002 390, 395--397]. @reynolds2024why surveys the determinatives that modify AdjPs, AdvPs, and PPs (_more_, _less_, _enough_, _that_, _much_, _a little_, _all_) and finds their distribution idiosyncratic lexeme by lexeme, as adjective intensifiers are generally, with no pattern that sets any of them apart as an adverb; the modifying phrases take modifiers of their own (_far fewer_, _much more_, _so much_), the internal structure an NP headed by a determinative supplies.[^21]

NPs modify adjectives in _three years old_, _a great deal smaller_, and _plenty big enough_. _CGEL_ also treats _lots better_ and _heaps worse_ as containing quantificational NP modifiers [@huddleston2002 549--550]. These uses extend the connection with the quantificational common nouns compared in §[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-nouns"}.

_CGEL_ contrasts predicative _a great deal better_ with *_some a great deal better proposals_, against permitted _some much better proposals_ [@huddleston2002 551--552]. The excluded example also juxtaposes _some_ and _a_; it doesn't isolate NP category as the cause. _CGEL_ permits _She's a lot better player than me_, analysing the inner article as lost after the outer one [@huddleston2002 p. 552, n. 8].

Informal attributive examples with nominal _heaps_ and _lots_ further qualify a categorical exclusion.[^22] They don't establish the same distribution as degree determinatives. If degree determinatives systematically permit attributive uses excluded for established nominal modifiers, that would favour DP projection, a question of phrase structure rather than of membership within Noun.
## Comparison of the four implementations {#sec:costs}
The articles' restrictions, _no_/_none_ form selection, the compound restrictor conditions, and the differences among existing noun subcategories in determination and modification (Table [3](#tab:existing){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:existing"}) are the same under all four implementations.

Both ordinary-Head accounts distinguish NP-peripheral modifiers from the internal degree and cardinal approximative conditions (§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}). For compounds, this NP--Nom boundary preserves the modifier-domain contrast that fusion expresses through its inner phrase and outer Nom (§[4.5](#sec:compounds){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:compounds"}).

In the ordinary-Head accounts, _few_ heads Nom and Nom heads NP across bare, partitive, and externally determined uses. In the fusion accounts, where one constituent fills Head and a dependent function together, its DP or NP fills Det--Head in bare and partitive uses and Mod--Head with an external determiner (Table [4](#tab:matched-heads){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:matched-heads"}). In these matched examples, all have Nom as the NP's immediate Head. The contrast concerns the full Head chain and the additional fused functions.

I favour sharing the direct Head chain across determinative uses and with established nouns. The selection and attachment conditions preserve the modifier-domain contrast. Fusion instead shares a construction with adjectives. Both cover the matched cases with the same lexical restrictions; the trade-off concerns which structures the grammar shares. The wider profile comparison supplies the reason for preferring the nominal grouping.

@reynolds2026numerals distinguishes determinative numerals such as _ten_ in _ten men_, proper-noun cases such as _10_ in _Room 10_, and common-noun numerals such as _tens_ in _tens of pens_. Under the D-noun categorization, all fall within primary Noun, retaining their subcategory and constructional differences. Ordinals remain adjectives, and complex numeral phrases remain distinct from single lexemes.

@payne2013anaphoric [797--798] distinguish three lexemes spelled _one_: determinative, anaphoric common noun, and generic pronoun. These remain distinct under the D-noun categorization, but all belong within primary Noun.

Complex cardinals also separate category from function. In _two hundred books_, the whole _two hundred_ fills Det; internally, _two_ modifies the magnitude head _hundred_ [@reynolds2026numerals §4]. In _these two hundred books_, _these_ fills Det and _two hundred_ is an internal modifier. One Det function doesn't entail a limit of one determinative lexeme per NP.
## Overall assessment {#sec:overall-assessment}
The D-noun analysis combines the two preferences. The lexical comparison favours Noun membership because nominal connections recur across more of the determinative system (§[2.6](#sec:membership){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:membership"}). The structural comparison favours the direct Head chain across the licensed uses (§[5.4](#sec:costs){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:costs"}). Within the proposed grammar, determinatives then meet the same Noun condition on lexical Heads in Nom as the existing subcategories (§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:det-uniform"}). That category condition expresses the grouping supported by the profile.

Separate D with ordinary Head licenses the same structures by admitting N or D. The coverage audit (fifth supplement) compares four specified implementations across the fragment and selected wider comparisons, including degree uses (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:enough"}). It records the mechanism that covers each construction family and lexeme group. Of its 103 cells, 35 are lexical facts identical in all four accounts. The simple determinatives' genitive restrictions and the anaphoric genitive complement condition lie outside those cells; the audit doesn't measure their comparative cost. The fusion accounts state where partitives and relatives attach as constructional conditions, and _CGEL_ states its peripheral-modifier, number-transparency, and degree-modifier rules separately for NPs and DPs.

Both ordinary-Head accounts consolidate these rules. D-noun does so by reclassification, retaining the Noun condition on lexical Heads in Nom. The stated separate-D implementation adds D to that condition; the audit's 32 cells coded Dn, its label for a derivation that goes through that added disjunct, record applications of this single condition. It also retains the category change for secondary uses and extends the head-genitive definition to D. These are commitments of the implementation compared, rather than costs established for every separate-D grammar.

A separate-D account could instead assign a nominal feature once to Noun and once to D, with inheritance within each category. The condition on lexical Heads in Nom and the head-genitive definition could each refer to that feature. One secondary-use schema could cover the same three input types, with category change specified for the D inputs if the account retains _CGEL_'s output categories. Such sharing requires neither per-lexeme feature assignments nor wholly separate rules for the shared behaviour.

The feature would identify the same broad domain as the proposed Noun category while retaining the primary Noun--D boundary. I favour Noun membership because the profile comparison independently supports that grouping (§[2.6](#sec:membership){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:membership"}); the category then states the domain of the shared grammar directly. The audit shows what the four implementations state, but doesn't establish a strict description-length advantage over the feature formulation. The secondary use and the former complex determinatives weren't among the diagnostics of §[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"}; they're additional consequences, not tests set in advance.

The secondary set-denoting use of §[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:secondary-use"} covers names, compound determinatives, and cardinals within Noun. With _CGEL_'s categories the compound and cardinal inputs change category; a separate-D account can share the schema as just described.

Several rules _CGEL_ states separately for nouns and determinatives can likewise be stated once: number transparency for _lot_, _number_, and _some_ (§[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-nouns"}); NP and determinative degree modifiers of adjectives (§[5.3](#sec:enough){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:enough"}); and peripheral attachment in _almost every_ and _only you_ (§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}). _CGEL_ already states the head genitive once, by the head noun's inflection, listing _everyone's_ beside _Edward's_ (§[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inflection"}); within Noun that statement holds as written.

The four complex determinatives analysed here reduce to peripheral _a_ and predeterminer _many_ (§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}). These generalizations are available under either ordinary-Head taxonomy: the proposed account states their domain through Noun membership, the separate-D alternative through the inherited feature. The profile of §[2](#sec:proposal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:proposal"} supplies the case for lexical-category status.

If determinative-headed and genitive expressions require different structural constraints after their independently motivated restrictions are held fixed, the shared-projection proposal in §[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:det-uniform"} loses its advantage. That would favour separate phrase types. Retaining a separate primary D requires the further case that the category boundary captures the recurring differences better than a determinative subcategory within Noun.
# Consequences and remaining taxonomic choices {#sec:consequences}
The grouping permits one schema for the secondary uses of names, compound determinatives, and cardinals. Whether determinative and pronoun should form an intermediate category is a further choice.
## Secondary set-denoting uses {#sec:secondary-use}
Compound determinatives have a secondary set-denoting use, in which they take an article, a pre-head adjective, and plural inflection: lexicalized _a special someone_ and _a certain someone_, and productively _an anonymous someone_, _the responsible someone_, _a furry something_, and _such an anyone_.[^23]

_CGEL_ records _nobody_ in _a nobody_ and _something_ in _a little something_ as nouns formed by conversion [@huddleston2002 423, n. 43]. Under the D-noun analysis no category change is involved: the use is the one _CGEL_ gives proper nouns in _an Ophelia_ and _the Smiths_, where the name comes to "denote the set of people bearing this name" and then "takes a full range of dependents, including determiners and restrictive modifiers" [@huddleston2002 521].

In both subcategories the article and the pre-head adjective follow from what the noun now denotes, a kind or an individual of the quantifier's description, and quantificational force is gone: _an anybody_ is a person of no particular standing. The quantifier and its nominal echo can share a text: "anybody could have competed. I qualified as an anybody" (_Skiing_, 1992, COCA). One process thus covers two subcategories where _CGEL_ uses conversion for one and a secondary use for the other.

_CGEL_ makes the same move a third time for cardinal numerals, which are "primarily determinatives but they have a secondary use in which they inflect for number and hence belong in the noun category": _in threes_, _in their hundreds_ [@huddleston2002 385]. Within Noun the plural cardinal is the same secondary use again, with no category to change; @reynolds2026numerals supplies the separate case that cardinals have both uses.

The rule can be stated once, with its input class stated: a Noun lexeme whose primary use identifies or quantifies over individuals, a name, a compound determinative, or a cardinal, has a secondary use in which it denotes the set of individuals fitting its description, the bearers of the name, the persons or things of the quantifier's description, the groups of the cardinal's size, and in that use it takes plural inflection and the common noun's dependents, an article, a pre-head adjective, and restrictive modifiers.

The articles and _every_ have no such use (*_an every_); whether the personal pronouns do (_the hes and shes_) I leave open. Within Noun the rule has three inputs. With separate D and _CGEL_'s output categories, the compound and cardinal inputs change category; a shared schema can still state their common behaviour (§[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:overall-assessment"}).[^24]

Locative compounds before a noun (_an anywhere operation_) are the ordinary noun-as-modifier use and take their article from the head noun.
## Coordinate or nested subcategories {#sec:inheritance}
Including determinatives within Noun leaves a further question: where within Noun do they belong? In the proposed hierarchy, common noun, proper noun, pronoun, and determinative are four coordinate subcategories. Hudson classes his determiners as pronouns, and pronoun is itself inside Noun. Both group the relevant determinatives with nouns. They differ in whether pronouns and determinatives form an intermediate category that excludes common and proper nouns.

@hudson2010wordgrammar [253--254] makes taxonomic inclusion explicit. His noun category contains common noun, proper noun, and pronoun; what he calls a determiner is a pronoun whose [valency]{.smallcaps} permits the relevant common-noun dependent [@hudson2004determiners 9--10]. The word's category remains constant across independent and dependent uses, which differ in their permitted dependents.

Hudson's criteria centre on licensing a singular count common noun and on mutual exclusion in that use. He sets aside _all_, cardinal numerals, and quantifiers restricted to plural or non-count nouns [@hudson2004determiners 9--10]. Their place in his taxonomy remains unsettled by those criteria. Extending the pronoun analysis to _CGEL_'s fuller inventory requires comparing its modifier selection, grade, and quantitative meanings with the existing pronouns' properties.

The intermediate category would be useful if it supported grammatical generalizations applying to pronouns and determinatives together. Coordinate rank permits unequal similarities among the noun subcategories. The issue is whether their shared properties warrant another level in the lexical hierarchy.

@reynolds2021 compares 138 word forms through properties recorded as present or absent, such as accepting _almost_. Its unsupervised clustering groups forms by these similarities without being given their category labels. The groups broadly resemble the pronoun and determinative inventories, though a replication finds that the outcome varies with initialization and feature selection.[^25] But the study contains no common or proper nouns, and extending the comparison to the open categories would require new sampling, feature selection, and coding. Distinguishing the two groups establishes neither their taxonomic rank nor the proposed superordinate category; the examples discussed here aren't representative samples of the open categories.

The grammatical case for nesting includes shared lexical properties (Table [3](#tab:existing){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:existing"}). Pronouns and determinatives both have closed inventories, and both include gender-sensitive forms and interrogative or relative forms. The contrasts _she_/_it_ and _somebody_/_something_ are carried by the lexical head, whereas descriptions and names shape referent construal (§[2.3](#sec:inflection){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inflection"}). These connections are more specific than their general dependence on context.

The _my_/_mine_ and _no_/_none_ paradigms supply a further grammatical parallel (§[3.3](#sec:form-selection){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:form-selection"}). In ordinary NP uses, both distinguish dependent and independent forms. The parallel has limited lexical reach, but it supplies positive evidence for the proposed intermediate grouping.

Modifier selection gives mixed support: restricted adjectival modification in _poor old me_ [@huddleston2002 429--430] has a counterpart in _the lucky few_. Both groups permit NP-peripheral modifiers (§[4.4](#sec:payne){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:payne"}), while the four gradable quantifiers also permit the internal degree-AdvP series (§[2.2](#sec:adjectival-profile){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:adjectival-profile"}).

Personal pronouns and primary naming uses of proper nouns both resist free determination [@huddleston2002 429--430, 517, 519--520]. Determinatives allow _the few_, _the two_, and _these three_; _CGEL_ gives _these few here_ and _the many who did_ [@huddleston2002 415--416].

I retain coordinate rank because the closer affinities cross the proposed intermediate boundary. Partitives, number transparency, restricted dependents, and degree uses connect determinatives with quantificational common nouns (§[2.1](#sec:quant-nouns){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:quant-nouns"}), while reference and selected form alternations connect them with pronouns. The coordinate analysis records both connections within Noun and preserves the distinct organization of each subcategory: person, case, and reflexivity in much of the pronoun system; quantification, determination, and degree in much of the determinative system.

Both inheritance paths meet the fragment's Noun condition (§[5.2](#sec:det-uniform){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:det-uniform"}), and nesting remains a coherent alternative. The coordinate preference rests on these intersecting profiles; accepting Noun membership and ordinary Head leaves this further choice open.
# Conclusion {#sec:conclusion}
I propose including English determinatives within Noun. The strongest positive comparison comes from quantificational common nouns: their selected complements, number transparency, restricted dependents, and degree uses connect with parts of the determinative inventory. The connected adjectival profile of the four gradable quantifiers deserves weight on the same terms. Broader constructional and referential connections, with partial inflectional corroboration, favour the nominal grouping.

The grouping includes restricted members through their integration into the determinative system. Articles participate in its contrasts in definiteness, quantity, and count selection; _no_/_none_ retains its form-selection conditions.

The ordinary-Head analysis uses the same chain in bare, partitive, and externally determined expressions: the word heads Nom, and Nom heads NP. The taxonomic and structural choices complement each other: the nominal profile supports grouping determinatives with nouns, and that grouping satisfies the proposed grammar's category condition on lexical Heads in Nom. The coverage audit distinguishes the stated separate-D implementation from an alternative that shares nominal conditions through an inherited feature. The latter can express the shared domain too; the profile comparison supplies the case for treating that domain as Noun.

Under D-noun, the three specified inputs -- names (_an Ophelia_), compound determinatives (_a special someone_), and cardinals (_in threes_) -- share one secondary-use schema without a change of primary category (§[6.1](#sec:secondary-use){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:secondary-use"}, §[5.5](#sec:overall-assessment){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:overall-assessment"}). The complex determinatives _a few_ and _many a_ dissolve into peripheral modification by _a_ and predeterminer modification by _many_ (§[3.2](#sec:complex-determinatives){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:complex-determinatives"}).

Coordinate rank alongside pronoun is a further preference, since the comparisons connect determinatives with both pronouns and quantificational common nouns.
# Earlier accounts {#sec:historical}
Keeping a word in one category across dependent and independent uses leaves its taxonomic position open. As @lyons1968 [232--235] observes, distribution can be compared at different levels: two expressions may belong together at one level and differ at a more specific level. @palmer1924 [24] proposed placing "determinative adjectives" with pronouns. He contrasted them with qualifying adjectives, which permit predicative use, comparison, and adverbial modification. Most determinatives, he observed, can "be used indifferently as pronouns or as modifiers of nouns". @lyons1968 [279] adds a semantic connection: the definite article, demonstratives, and third-person pronouns share the feature "definite", and their traditional separation, he says, obscures the relationship.

@postal1966 develops the article--pronoun connection through English reflexives and combinations such as _we men_. His category assignments depend on representational level: article segments occur at intermediate stages, their deepest counterparts are features of nouns, and surface pronouns can receive derivative Noun status. This differs from a proposal about the lexical taxonomy of the surface words.

@sommerstein1972 [197--203] argues in the opposite direction, giving the definite article and personal pronouns underlying NP structure. His English comparison includes the count restriction on anaphoric _one_, whereas _it_ can stand for non-count as well as count nouns.[^26]

These predecessors challenge a fundamental article--pronoun separation but don't settle the full modern determinative inventory's surface taxonomy. Its contrasts in grade, modification, complementation, and restricted independent use extend beyond their proposals. Hudson's explicit nesting and Spinillo's restricted article grouping are assessed in §§[6.2](#sec:inheritance){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:inheritance"} and [3](#sec:articles){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:articles"}, respectively.
