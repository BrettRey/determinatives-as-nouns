## A. Structural paper (to *Journal of Linguistics* first)

**Working title:** *Ordinary heads for English determinatives*

**Independence test:** the paper holds CGEL's separate determinative category fixed. A reader who rejects "determinatives are nouns" can accept all of it.

### Abstract (≈210 words; JL asks for about 200)

> *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* (CGEL) analyses independent determinatives, as in *take some*, *some of the wine* and *the lucky few*, with fused functions: one word realizes Head together with Determiner or Modifier. Holding CGEL's separate determinative category fixed, I argue that ordinary Head relations do better. In bare, partitive and externally determined uses alike, the determinative heads a nominal that heads the noun phrase, so *the lucky few* has the structure of *the lucky survivors*. CGEL licenses a fused modifier-head only for determinatives that also occur as internal modifiers. Attested *the some who* (COCA, iWeb) lacks that licence: *some* doesn't modify after *the*, and in the lines checked, the string *the some people* turns up only as typos and a stray *the*. ⚑ The available repairs (converting *some* to a noun, a construction-specific rule, or a weaker licensing condition) each add a stipulation that ordinary Head avoids. The same structure dissolves CGEL's complex determinatives *a few*, *a little*, *a great many* and *many a* into peripheral modification by *a* and predeterminer modification by *many*. It keeps the modifier contrasts CGEL draws in *almost every* and *hardly anyone present*. Fusion survives where it has independent work, with adjectives (*the idle rich*). This isn't the DP hypothesis: the noun phrase stays noun-headed.

### Conclusion (≈300 words)

> Independent determinatives don't need fused functions. With CGEL's categories unchanged, an ordinary-Head analysis gives bare, partitive and externally determined uses a single chain: the determinative heads a nominal, and the nominal heads the noun phrase. That groups *the lucky few* with *the lucky survivors*, not with *the idle rich*, and it treats independent *few* alike with and without *the*.
>
> The case that separates the analyses is *the some who*. CGEL's licence for a fused modifier-head requires a modifier use, and *some* has none. So fusion has two options: list *the some* as an exception, as CGEL already does for *the both of us*, or weaken its own condition. ⚑ Treating *the some* as a converted noun predicts noun behaviour it doesn't show: it keeps its quantificational force and takes no plural [*somes* not yet searched]. Under ordinary Head nothing needs licensing. The rarity of *the some* follows from what existential *some* adds once *the* has identified the set.
>
> The same structure retires the complex determinatives. In *a few*, *a little* and *a great many*, a peripheral *a* modifies an NP headed by *few*, *little* or *many*. *Many a* has predeterminer *many* before an *a*-NP, like *such a* and *what a*. Their restrictions, including the polarity contrast between *few* and *a few*, become conditions on those modifiers.
>
> Fusion remains for adjectives, where it has independent work, and for the nominal *nylon* and *double* constructions. The open question is phrase structure. If determinative-headed and genitive expressions turn out to need different constraints once their lexical restrictions are held fixed, separate phrase types would be favoured. Nothing here depends on whether determinatives are a kind of noun. A grammar that keeps them a separate category adopts the analysis by licensing ordinary Head for both categories.

**What this commits the structural paper to (new work):**
1. **⚑ Answer the repairs Codex named:** conversion, a construction-specific rule, a revised licensing condition. The conversion answer can borrow from the taxonomic material (*the few*/*the many*/*the some* keep quantificational force), stated briefly here so the paper stands alone.
2. **Engage the noun-ellipsis / empty-noun literature as a rival:** inward check first, then a light `/hyperresearch` pass.
3. **Run the main comparison with D separate,** and put the full four-way audit in an appendix or supplement.
4. **Cite Payne, Huddleston & Pullum 2007** (*JL* 43, fusion of functions; in the central bib, not yet cited). It's the analysis this paper argues against, in the same journal.

## B. Taxonomic paper (Corela's December call, or JL later)

**Working title:** *Determinatives as nouns in English*

**Independence test (GPT pro's):** "Even where a grammar retains fusion, the comparative grammatical profile supports treating determinatives as a subcategory of Noun."

### Abstract (≈175 words; Corela allows 10 lines, in French and English, so this may need trimming)

> English determinatives (*the*, *some*, *every*, *few* …) are a fourth subcategory of Noun beside common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns, not the separate primary category of *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language*. Comparing the whole determinative inventory with the established subcategories and with adjectives, I find nominal connections recurring across it. Quantificational common nouns share partitives, number transparency and restricted dependents with determinative quantifiers; demonstratives inflect for number; compounds take head genitives. The adjectival profile of grade and degree modification is confined to four quantifiers. The articles and *every* belong through their integration into the determinative system. Within Noun, one secondary-use schema covers proper nouns (*an Ophelia*), personal pronouns (*a he*, *the real me*), compound determinatives (*a special someone*) and cardinals (*in threes*) without a change of category. Attested pronoun uses take plural inflection and adjectives, and keep the pronoun's person for reflexives, as a secondary use would and a conversion wouldn't. The classification stands whatever a grammar says about the internal structure of independent determinatives.

### Conclusion (≈280 words)

> Determinatives belong within Noun. The strongest positive comparison comes from quantificational common nouns. Their selected complements, number transparency, restricted dependents and degree uses connect with parts of the determinative inventory. The adjectival profile of the four gradable quantifiers deserves weight on the same terms, but it stays confined to them, while the nominal connections recur across the inventory. Restricted members belong through the determinative system: the articles take part in its contrasts of definiteness, quantity and count selection, and *no*/*none* keeps its form-selection conditions.
>
> The grouping lets the grammar state once what it now states piecemeal. One secondary-use schema covers proper nouns, personal pronouns, compound determinatives and cardinals. Each carries its own description of the individuals it identifies or quantifies over, and none changes category. Stated by that property, the schema predicts the pronoun input, which the corpus confirms. The pronoun's split fits a secondary use, not a conversion: third-person agreement on the verb, but the pronoun's own person kept for reflexives.
>
> A grammar that keeps determinatives separate can share these generalizations through a feature that tracks Noun membership. ⚑ What separates the two is [the observation the separate-category rival fails to predict: not yet identified]. Within Noun, coordinate rank beside pronoun fits the comparisons better than nesting, since determinatives connect with both pronouns and quantificational common nouns. The classification doesn't depend on how independent determinatives are headed: it holds under fusion and under ordinary Head.

**What this commits the taxonomic paper to (new work):**
1. **⚑ The open slot in paragraph 3.** All three outside readers (GLM-5.3, GPT pro, Codex) said the paper needs one observation that a separate category with an inherited feature fails to predict. The pronoun prediction supports the schema, not the taxonomy, because pronouns are already in Noun in CGEL. This is the taxonomic paper's main open problem, and it should be argued, not conceded.
2. **A short consequences section** saying what the grouping lets the grammar state, citing the structural paper (under review) without depending on it.
3. **A French version of the abstract,** if it goes to Corela. Disclose any AI help with the translation.
