# Fusion of functions revisited: Independent determinatives in English (NBER-style abstract)

Draft, 2026-10-15. Plain-language version: question, finding, consequence; no citations.

How is a noun phrase built when its only word is a determinative, as in *take some* or *the lucky few*? *The Cambridge Grammar of the English Language* says that one word fills two grammatical functions at once. I show that the ordinary analysis, in which the determinative simply heads the phrase, covers the same facts with less machinery. The test case is *the some who say it*, attested in speech, academic prose, and web text. By the grammar's own rules, a determinative can fill two functions only when it also has a matching modifier use, and *some* has none. The ordinary analysis also explains why *a few* and *many a* behave as two words, not one: they expand (*a good few*), and their agreement follows the parts. Two-function analysis remains where it does real work, with adjectives (*the idle rich*). The result simplifies the grammar of English noun phrases without adopting the DP hypothesis.
