## Bottom line

The idea survived; the paper did not. Reviewer 2 says so in terms ("there is a meaningful
proposal here") and invites a new submission to ELL or elsewhere. Reviewer 1, who says up front
that CGEL is not their framework, read the whole thing as a terminological quibble inside CGEL.
That reading is wrong on the merits (the paper's claim is about supercategory membership, not
about the determiner/pro-form functional split R1 offers as the "received" solution), but it is
the reading a competent generalist arrived at, and the paper gave them nothing early enough to
prevent it. Both reports converge on one diagnosis: the paper assumes the reader shares the
author's investment in CGEL's internal analysis.

The editor's "only a totally new paper" is right as a description of the work, and R2's "not a
resubmission but an entirely new submission" is the procedural route. This is a rebuild, not a
revision: same claim, new paper.

| Sommerstein 1972 and Lyons 1968 missing; Postal/Anderson/Hudson listed without engaging their arguments; Hudson does not leave common nouns outside the noun supercategory. | **Adopt** (Hudson verified) | Table 3's Hudson row says "Common N status: separate", and §7 says the paper "diverges by refusing... to leave common nouns outside the same supercategory". Both are wrong about Hudson. Hudson 2004 (held): "Determiners, therefore, are a subset of pronouns" and "there is no need for a word class 'determiner'". Hudson 2010, *An Introduction to Word Grammar* (Mendeley, now central): "'Common noun', 'proper noun' and 'pronoun' give the word-class 'noun'"; "'pronoun' isA 'noun', and stands alongside two well established traditional subclasses, 'common noun' and 'proper noun'"; "nouns include pronouns, which in turn include determiners (10.1)"; and "In 'pronoun' we find ANY and THE as well as ME, WHO". So Hudson's hierarchy is noun > {common, proper, pronoun > determiner}: the same supercategory as this paper, with determinatives nested inside pronoun rather than coordinate with it. That makes the paper's distinctive claim narrower and sharper than the draft states: not "determinatives are nouns" (Hudson and Sommerstein already have that) but "determinative and pronoun are coordinate sub-classes, not nested", which is exactly what the 2021 clustering was cited for and never argued in the text. The rebuild should say this on page one. Sommerstein 1972 was already in Brett's Mendeley library and is now central as `literature/sommerstein-1972-so-called-definite-article-english` (verified from the JSTOR cover page: Alan H. Sommerstein, "On the So-Called Definite Article in English", *Linguistic Inquiry* 3(2), Spring 1972, pp. 197–209). His hypothesis, in his words, is "articles as pronouns" against Postal's "pronouns as articles": *the*, *I*, *he* are "underlying pronouns (that is, NPs in their own right)" (sidecar lines 27, 65). That is the nested position (article inside pronoun) the paper has to distinguish itself from. Crossref also lists A. H. Sommerstein 1974, "Article and noun in English", *Lingua* 33(4): 367–376, doi:10.1016/0024-3841(74)90046-1, not held. ### Rejected

- R1's claim that the received functional split (determiner versus true pro-form) already solves the problem. It answers a different question. The rebuild says so, once, with Rapoport's rules: state the functional split fairly, agree that it is real, then show that it leaves the category question open.
