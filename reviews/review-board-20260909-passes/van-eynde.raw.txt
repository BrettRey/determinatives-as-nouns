# Review: "Determinatives as nouns in English"

*Reviewer perspective: HPSG; NP-internal syntax and agreement.*

**Note on sources.** I have read only the article and its two supplements. Where I refer to Van Eynde (2003), Payne et al. (2007, 2010), Hudson (2004) or Spinillo (2004), I rely solely on the present paper's characterisations; I have not verified their contents here.

## 1. Summary of the main claim

English determinatives should be classified inside the lexical category Noun — provisionally as a fourth subcategory coordinate with common noun, proper noun and pronoun — because the rationale *CGEL* already uses to admit pronouns (shared phrasal distribution despite divergent inflection and dependents) applies equally to determinatives, with the separable further proposal that independent determinatives have ordinary Head rather than fused functions.

## 2. Strengths

**(a) Taxonomy and headedness are properly disentangled (§2.1, §7.2, Table 7).** The paper does not knock down the DP hypothesis and declare victory. It constructs a 2×2 (separate/nominal D × fusion/ordinary Head), concedes that separate-D-with-ordinary-Head "gives the strongest separate-D alternative," and concedes further that it "shares the D-noun ordinary-Head account's reduced phrase-type inventory and Head relations." Identifying and strengthening the best rival is uncommon in this literature and is the paper's chief methodological virtue.

**(b) Unusual discipline about the author's own prior work (§5 and the matrix audit).** §5 declines to use Reynolds (2021) as support, on the correct ground that the matrix contains no common or proper nouns and that "even perfect recovery of the distinction would be compatible with shared nounhood." The audit documents the 232-vs-155 feature discrepancy, a feature silently dropped by the published clustering code, initialisation sensitivity (129 vs. 125 vs. 131 matches), and retracts an earlier singleton-feature claim. The corpus supplement likewise refuses to read the 65 Det–Head tokens as independent argument uses (*about 30 seconds*). This is how supporting material should behave.

**(c) §6's test for defective members** — paradigmatic integration, participation in the category's contrasts, locality of the gap — is portable, is applied concretely against Spinillo's expanded article class, and is correctly labelled as supporting *determinative* membership rather than nounhood directly.

## 3. Substantive weaknesses

**W1. The uniformity of N_D is assumed, not argued, and the paper's own data point against it (§2.2, §4.4, §4.7). Severity: major.**

§2.2 disposes of the adjective/noun split in three sentences, noting its evidence is "chiefly from Italian and Dutch" and conceding that "a noun-headed NP account therefore needn't unify every determining expression as nominal" — after which the paper proceeds as though it does. This is a scope deflection, not an argument, and it is unnecessary, because the relevant evidence is available English-internally and is already in the paper. The items that take degree modification and comparative/superlative inflection (*very many*, *too few*, *fewer*, *fewest*, *less*, *least*) are the same items reported as predicatively usable in §4.7 (*Their enemies were many*, *Its advantages are several*) and the same items assigned to Mod in §4.4 (*the few mistakes*, *these few here*). Three adjectival diagnostics converging on one subset is not a set of independent "lexical restrictions."

The exposure is sharpest in Table 5, which sets *the lucky few* beside *the idle rich* and treats the fusion analysis's grouping of the two as a neutral trade-off. On a heterogeneous account, that grouping is *explained*. Compounding this, the fragment's determinative workhorse is *few* — precisely the contested item — while *the*, *a* and *every*, which carry §6, are never run through the modification comparisons.

*Remedies:* (i) tabulate roughly five adjectival diagnostics against the full inventory and report whether they co-vary; if they do, either admit a nominal/adjectival division inside the proposal or argue the clustering follows from gradable-quantifier semantics rather than category; (ii) failing that, narrow the thesis explicitly to the non-gradable core and rerun the fragment with *this*, *some* or *each* in *few*'s role.

**W2. The parity criterion is never stated in a form that discriminates (§3.1, §4.1, §4.4). Severity: major.**

The load-bearing argument is: *CGEL* admits pronouns on grounds G; determinatives meet G; admit them. But §4.1 concedes external distribution is insufficient (*the rich*); §4.1 further concedes that superlative adjectival heads take partitive *of*-phrases (*the most important of her criticisms*); §4.4 concedes adjectival fusion admits external determiners and modifiers. What remains of G by §4.4 is "breadth ... across demonstratives and quantifiers with varied domains," which the paper itself calls "a comparative reason, not a necessary-and-sufficient membership test." G is thus left gradient at exactly the point where it must exclude adjectives. The reply that *the rich* requires a determiner while singular count common nouns do too (§4.1) does not settle it, since the question is what licenses the head, not whether determination is present.

*Remedy:* state G as an explicit conjunction — e.g. independent use available lexeme-by-lexeme rather than construction-by-construction; partitive complement licensed by the head's own entry rather than by an external determiner — and demonstrate that adjectival fusion fails a named conjunct. If no such G exists, reframe the paper as a defence of the coherence of a nominal classification rather than an argument for it.

**W3. The Det-uniformity economy (§3.2, §7.1) is not shown to be more than notational, and the fragment states determination twice, in both directions. Severity: moderate-to-major.**

§7.1's Det:{DP,NP,PP} → Det:{NP,PP} is immediately qualified: "Selection still distinguishes determinative-headed, genitive and other licensed NPs." The disjunction has moved from category labels to feature specifications on NP. §7.3 states the disqualifying condition — the economy lapses "if determinative-headed and genitive NPs required different projection rules after their independently motivated feature restrictions were held fixed" — but never discharges it.

The fragment compounds this. The determinative's entry carries the target restriction (Table 6, col. 4: determiner selects head), while the common noun carries a determination requirement (§7.2: *a book* / \*_Book arrived_: head selects specifier), and the article additionally needs a separate argument-use prohibition, since §7.2 concedes "requiring determination for a common noun doesn't itself block an article-headed NP from argument use." That is roughly four lexical stipulations for *the*, with no matching ledger for the separate-D fusion account. I am not applying a minimum-description-length standard — the paper explicitly disclaims one — but the paper's own standard, "allocation of generalizations," requires an exhibit.

*Remedies:* (i) an itemised stipulation ledger per account, keyed to Table 7; (ii) at least one condition statable over a uniform NP Det slot but not over the disjunction; the near-absence of NP-internal concord in English is itself the difficulty, and is where a short cross-linguistic remark would earn its keep; (iii) settle whether both selection directions are needed or one is derivative.

## 4. Key question

State G. Given that adjectival fused heads occupy argument positions, take partitive *of*-phrases, and admit external determiners and premodifiers, what property do pronouns and determinatives share and adjectives lack, such that G admits determinatives to Noun without admitting *the rich* — and on which side of G do *many*, *few* and *little* fall?

## 5. Recommendation: Revise and Resubmit

The central idea is worth pursuing and the comparative architecture is sound; the present support is not yet adequate to the thesis as stated.

**Required repairs:** W1 (diagnostic tabulation or explicit narrowing of the claim); W2 (statement of G); W3 (stipulation ledger, plus discharge or withdrawal of the §7.1 economy claim). Two housekeeping items: the audit's "An independent review reran the matrix and corpus analyses" should say whether that review was human or model-assisted, given the acknowledgements; and the prepared public-matrix correction notice should be posted to the original resource before the paper relies on §5's characterisation of Reynolds (2021).

**Optional extensions:** replication in a concord language; a worked formal implementation of the fragment; the cardinals consequence in §7.3.
