The article makes a credible case that determinatives can be included within Noun without disrupting the category–function distinction or losing familiar lexical restrictions. Its strongest contribution is separating three questions often conflated: nounhood, ordinary headedness, and the hierarchy within Noun. The matched alternatives also give fusion a fair hearing. My principal reservation is that the article establishes the proposal’s coherence more convincingly than its superiority. For Journal of Linguistics readers, the revision should concentrate on the criterion for preferring the proposed taxonomy and reduce the repeated explanations of what individual observations cannot establish.

The central argument currently oscillates between an internal consistency challenge and a positive theory of classification. The internal challenge is clear: if CGEL admits pronouns because their phrases share nominal functions despite distinctive morphology and dependents, why exclude determinatives with comparable behaviour? That deserves an answer. But it does not yet establish that inclusion is preferable to revising or qualifying the pronoun rationale. The stronger separate-D alternative makes this especially visible: its ordinary-headedness rule differs mainly by admitting `{N, D}` where the proposed rule admits N. The paper needs to explain why representing shared projection through taxonomic inheritance is better than stating this shared property directly. It need not prove a unique minimum description length. It does need an explicit comparative principle whose application here amounts to more than preferring inheritance because inheritance groups the forms together.

The adjective comparison is where that principle should earn its force. The paper appropriately recognizes *the rich*, *the idle rich*, and *the most important of her criticisms*. These examples substantially weaken an inference from argument distribution, partitives, and adjectival modification to nounhood. The response is that independent determinative use is broader and more varied. However, the comparison is not yet presented on equal terms. Adjectives receive a few constructional restrictions; determinatives receive extensive allowances for differences among lexemes, readings, and constructions. I would replace the scattered discussion with one compact comparison covering human-class adjectives, comparative and superlative adjectives, demonstratives, quantifiers, and the restricted determinatives. Specify which contrasts favour a category-wide projection rule and which require construction-specific licensing. The present wording risks making “subcategory” the preferred name for determinative restrictions and “construction” the preferred name for adjectival restrictions without sufficiently motivating that difference.

The articles remain the proposal’s most vulnerable extension. The discussion correctly says that their determining behaviour supports membership in determinative rather than nounhood directly. Yet the transfer from that membership to nounhood is the point requiring scrutiny. The analogy with *my* is suggestive but unequal: *my* belongs to a morphological paradigm containing independently nominal forms; *the* and *a* participate in a distributional and semantic system shared with other lexemes. Both kinds of connection can matter, but their evidential strength should not be treated as interchangeable. The three stated conditions for retaining restricted members are also broad enough to accommodate numerous classifications. I would strengthen the comparison with redistribution: identify the positive generalizations lost by separating articles, then show whether those losses exceed the restrictions retained by including them. *Every* supplies a useful bridge through *each* and modification by *almost*; the articles need an equally explicit accounting.

The fragment is valuable, but its formal presentation promises more than its specification delivers. The unrestricted condition \(C_h(f,c)\) can accommodate every relevant judgment, while much of its content remains in explanatory prose. Holding those conditions fixed fairly isolates a structural comparison, but it also means that the fragment cannot itself establish how much grammatical work the taxonomy performs. Make a limited set of conditions explicit for the crucial cases: bare *few*, *the lucky few*, *hardly anyone present*, and dependent-only articles. Then state precisely which conditions are shared and which require different formulations under the competing structures. In particular, the compound comparison offers a real trade-off: fusion supplies separate modifier domains structurally, whereas ordinary headedness distinguishes them through base and construction restrictions. That trade-off deserves more analytical weight than the modest reduction in phrase-type labels.

The coordinate arrangement should occupy less space. The article candidly concedes that its judgments do not distinguish coordination from nesting, and it identifies possible shared properties without resolving their taxonomic significance. That is sufficient for a boundary statement, but not for the prominence given to “preferably as a fourth coordinate subcategory” in the abstract. Preserving the determinative–pronoun distinction does not itself favour coordination: a nested taxonomy can preserve that distinction too. Present inclusion as the thesis and coordination as an unresolved implementation choice. A shorter discussion could specify what an intermediate category would need to explain and leave the matter there.

The exposition is lucid sentence by sentence but repetitive at the level of sections. Readers repeatedly learn that nounhood does not imply unrestricted distribution, ordinary headedness does not entail nounhood, fusion remains available, and the evidence does not select a unique taxonomy. These qualifications are responsible; their recurrence makes the article feel hesitant and delays its positive argument. Establish the three independent questions once near the beginning, show the four-way comparison early, and let subsequent sections supply evidence and costs. Merge “structural saturation” with independent use, shorten the separate cumulative summary, and reduce the logical alternatives in the taxonomy table. Retain the trees that expose substantive differences, especially *some*, *the lucky few*, and the compound; several simpler trees mainly repeat the notation.

The supplements are commendably transparent about what their evidence can establish. The matrix lacks common and proper nouns, and the corpus annotations encode analytical decisions rather than independently validating them. Those limitations are correctly stated. Keep both supplements available, but remove process history that does not help a reader reproduce or interpret the results—for example, references to an earlier draft’s mistake can become a concise correction record. The matrix’s principal role in this article is to prevent the earlier distributional result from being misread as evidence against shared nounhood.

I would preserve the paper’s central proposal and most of its examples. The needed revision is a sharper argument about where generalizations belong, supported by a balanced adjective comparison and a more explicit treatment of restricted members. That would turn a careful demonstration of analytical possibility into a more persuasive case for changing the taxonomy.
