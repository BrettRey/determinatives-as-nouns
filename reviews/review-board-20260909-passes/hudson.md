This is a simulated review from a Word Grammar research perspective, not Richard Hudson’s actual opinion.

The paper argues that English determinatives belong within Noun, provisionally alongside common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns, while treating ordinary headedness as a separate proposal.

The strongest relevant alternative is a nominal analysis in which determining words belong within pronoun and their dependent uses follow from their valencies. That alternative agrees with the principal inclusion claim but questions both the fourth coordinate subcategory and the decision to preserve CGEL’s constituent architecture. The strongest alternative retaining primary D is also clearly represented: D and N can share nominal projection without sharing a lexical supercategory.

Three strengths deserve emphasis.

First, the paper separates classification, headedness and function unusually carefully. The matched fragment in §7.2 prevents an improvement attributable to ordinary headedness from being credited automatically to nominal classification. Allowing separate D to project an NP is particularly valuable: it exposes the remaining disagreement instead of making the preferred analysis win through an unnecessarily weak opponent.

Second, the difficult examples receive substantive treatment. The comparison between *the lucky few* and *the idle rich*, followed by the two modifier domains in *hardly anyone present*, makes the structural trade-off inspectable. Fusion receives credit for connecting independent expressions with their dependent counterparts and for distinguishing modifier domains. Ordinary headedness receives credit for unifying head relations while retaining the restrictions. This is a serious comparison, even where it does not select a winner.

Third, the treatment of evidence is disciplined. The matrix supplement correctly denies that separation of determinatives and pronouns determines their taxonomic rank, and the corpus supplement distinguishes attestations from independent confirmation of annotations. The articles are also treated as requiring an additional membership argument, rather than being smuggled into Noun through examples involving *some*. One limitation of the supplied materials is that the supplementary tables are represented by external `\tablebody` references without their contents. I can assess the stated interpretation and prose-reported results, but not inspect those complete tables.

I have three substantive reservations.

**1. The preference for coordinate subcategories uses an uneven evidential standard. Severity: moderate, but a required repair to the advertised preference.**

In §5, an intermediate pronoun category must capture generalizations that would otherwise be repeated. Yet when shared contextual interpretation and pro-form gender are considered, the coordinate analysis can retain them through features or cross-classification. That availability does not itself favour coordination: the same response could preserve separate D by assigning nominal projection across categories, as §7.2 explicitly demonstrates.

The issue is therefore not whether the intermediate category is logically necessary. No category node is necessary if equivalent feature statements are permitted. The issue is why one allocation of shared properties is preferable. The paper’s central argument gives inheritance through Noun positive weight; its discussion of an intermediate pronoun category needs a comparable assessment.

A repair would be a small, aligned comparison of the lexical items actually shared by the competing inventories. Show where nominal projection, reference-related properties and dependent-selection restrictions are stated under each hierarchy. Apply the same criterion to both levels. If this yields no preference, retain coordination as a presentation convention rather than an argumentatively supported preference. The central claim survives that adjustment.

**2. The architectural alternative is described more fully than it is tested. Severity: moderate; required if the paper retains its present comparative claims.**

Section 2.2 acknowledges mutual dependency and construction-sensitive external headedness, including the temporal contribution of *day* in *We met that day*. However, the later comparison holds fixed an architecture in which the common noun ultimately heads the dependent construction and a determinative-headed NP occupies Det. Hudson’s hierarchy is then inserted into these projection rules. That tests the portability of the hierarchy, not the explanatory contribution of its associated dependency analysis.

This matters especially for §6 and the permissions in §7.2. The paper stipulates an article’s lack of argument permission separately from its selection of a common-noun target. A valency alternative would ask whether obligatory versus optional realization of that dependent captures part of this relationship. Such an account would still need to handle agreement, interpretation and which element external constructions select; it cannot simply declare victory by calling the relation valency.

The required repair is bounded: compare *the book*, *some books* and independent *some* under the two organizations. Identify which selection conditions are equivalent restatements and which architectural commitments do further work. Alternatively, explicitly restrict the matched comparison to CGEL-compatible constituency accounts and narrow the resulting comparative claims.

The supplied source summaries do not permit me to certify the exact fidelity of the Hudson paraphrases. Van Langendonck’s role in the headedness debate may merit contextual discussion, but I cannot establish a substantive omission from the supplied materials alone.

**3. The positive reason for privileging nominal projection needs a sharper boundary. Severity: substantial for the principal argument, although repairable without demonstrating superior coverage.**

Sections 3.1 and 7.3 give nominal organization greater taxonomic weight than modifier differences, invoking CGEL’s rationale for pronoun membership. This is a credible consistency argument. Nevertheless, §4.4 distinguishes adjectives partly because their nominal constructions have narrower lexical, interpretive and determiner restrictions, while §6 accommodates determinatives without independent argument uses through category-level integration.

The unresolved inference concerns how much breadth and integration suffice to make nominal projection a category property rather than a constructional permission. Since the fragment explicitly delegates use and dependent restrictions to lexical entries and constructions, its shared projection schema does not itself supply that boundary.

I am not requesting a necessary-and-sufficient definition or a minimum-description-length proof. A compact comparison would suffice: specify which combination of independent-use breadth, internal constructions and paradigmatic integration favours determinative inclusion, and explain why the adjectival comparison falls short under the same assessment. State one plausible descriptive finding that would weaken the inclusion argument itself. The existing closing condition about separate projection rules chiefly addresses phrase-type economy.

My key question is: **What makes inheritance through Noun preferable to cross-category projection, and why does that same reason favour—or fail to favour—inheritance through a broader pronoun category?**

**Recommendation: Revise and Resubmit.** The main inclusion proposal is worth pursuing, and the matched structural comparison is a substantial contribution. Required revisions concern consistent evaluation of inheritance, the scope of the architectural comparison, and the positive boundary of the membership argument. A full dependency grammar, new population-level corpus study or conclusive resolution of coordinate versus nested classification would be optional research extensions.
