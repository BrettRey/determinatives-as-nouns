The paper argues that English determinatives should be included within Noun, provisionally alongside common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns, while treating their ordinary headedness as a separate analytical proposal.

The strongest alternative is the separate-D, ordinary-Head grammar developed in §7.2. It preserves determinative as a primary category but allows both N and D to project Nom and NP, retaining precisely the lexical restrictions imposed by the preferred analysis. The issue is therefore whether nominal projection should define a lexical superclass or be shared across lexical categories. The paper deserves credit for confronting this alternative directly.

Three strengths are substantial. First, the distinctions among lexical category, syntactic function and headedness are unusually clear. The account keeps *apples* as the ultimate head of *some apples*, and neither recruits arguments against the DP hypothesis as direct evidence for determinative nounhood nor assumes that nounhood eliminates fusion. The discussion of independent genitives, including the different analyses of *mine*, is particularly useful.

Second, the treatment of fusion is fair and analytically serious. The discussion of *the lucky few* acknowledges Mod–Head fusion, thereby avoiding the erroneous objection that an external determiner makes the CGEL analysis unavailable. The compound comparison likewise recognizes a benefit of the DP–Nom distinction: it provides separate structural domains for premodification and the post-head restrictor. On the representations supplied, I find no clear mischaracterization of these central CGEL commitments. That assessment does not independently verify every cited passage.

Third, the matched fragment makes the proposal assessable. It distinguishes projection from use permissions, explains why the article inside *the apple* does not determine the outer NP’s distribution, and explicitly retains restrictions on *every*. The supplements show similar discipline: corpus annotations are not presented as independent confirmation of their own categories, and the feature matrix is not made to establish a taxonomic rank it cannot test.

I nevertheless see three substantive problems.

First, the principal preference still needs a more explicit criterion. This is a major issue, located especially in §7.3 and the conclusion. The argument asks why systematic nominal projection should warrant inclusion for pronouns but require cross-category licensing for determinatives. However, whether determinatives have that projection is itself one of the analytical choices under comparison. Their occurrence in NPs is common ground; their ordinary lexical projection into Nom is not. Once the strongest rival grants that projection, the preferred grammar and rival differ locally between a superclass restriction and a disjunction over two primary categories. Calling the former “inheritance” identifies the organization chosen; it does not yet establish why that organization is preferable.

I am not asking for superior coverage or a minimum-description-length proof. A defensible architectural principle could suffice. The paper should formulate one explicitly—for example, when shared phrase-building organization warrants a common lexical superclass despite differences in dependent selection—and apply it to both a favourable and an unfavourable case. It should also distinguish two claims: CGEL’s justification of pronoun membership creates pressure for reconsideration, and that justification commits CGEL to determinative inclusion. The first is persuasive; the second requires showing that the relevant similarities and differences receive consistent weight. The present conclusion comes closer to settling that question than the comparison warrants.

Second, the adjective control remains too narrow for the taxonomic work assigned to it. This is a moderate-to-major issue in §§4.1 and 4.4. The paper recognizes that *the rich*, *the idle rich* and *the most important of her criticisms* collectively supply external NP distribution, determination, modification and a partitive construction. It then distinguishes determinatives by breadth of independent use and variety of domains, illustrating the adjective restriction with *these very poor*. That is a useful contrast within the human-class construction, but the paper has already acknowledged adjectival constructions outside that interpretation.

The exact inference needing support is that these differences justify licensing at determinative-subcategory level while leaving adjectives to construction-specific fusion. Determinative independence also varies by item and construction, and the fragment expressly preserves that variation. A compact matched comparison should show how much organization is shared across demonstratives and quantifiers, versus human-class, comparative and superlative adjective constructions. This need not be a representative corpus study. Its purpose would be to identify the grammatical distinction that matters, rather than count favourable examples. Alternatively, the author could state that the proposed boundary is provisionally motivated by the selected constructions and narrow the corresponding conclusion.

Third, the extension to articles needs a sharper account of category cohesion. This is a moderate issue in §6, but it concerns the paper’s headline claim about *the* and *a*. The analogy with dependent *my* is limited: an inflectional paradigm provides a different kind of connection from substitution among *the/a/this/every book*. The latter is initially a shared functional environment. The paper itself establishes that determining expressions can belong to different lexical categories, so substitution in that environment cannot carry the entire membership argument.

The discussion of *every* is stronger because it adds connections to *each* and shared modification by *almost/nearly*. For articles, definiteness and count selection plausibly support integration, but the paper should explain why these connections establish a lexical category whose independently motivated nounhood extends to its restricted members. One remedy is to make the argument explicitly conditional on retaining CGEL’s determinative inventory, presenting the restricted members as consequences of that retained classification. A stronger remedy would compare the article connections with those of genitive determiners and identify what makes only the former categorially decisive. A comprehensive alternative article grammar is unnecessary.

My key question is: **What independently stated principle makes ordinary nominal projection sufficient for lexical inclusion here, while keeping the acknowledged adjectival nominal constructions outside Noun?**

I recommend **Revise and Resubmit**. The central idea warrants publication-level consideration, and the descriptive presentation is already careful. Required repairs are a clearer criterion for the taxonomic preference, a better controlled adjective comparison, and an explicit account of the restricted-member inference. Broader corpus sampling, deciding coordination versus nesting, and extending the fragment to predication are optional research extensions.
