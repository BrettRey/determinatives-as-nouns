# Review: "Determinatives as nouns in English" (Reynolds, draft September 2026)

## 1. Summary of the main claim

English determinatives should be classified within a superordinate Noun category — preferably as a fourth coordinate subcategory alongside common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns — because they share the constructions (independent argument use, partitives, external determination, modifier patterns) on which *CGEL* already admits pronouns to Noun, with ordinary headedness proposed as a separable further step.

## 2. Strengths

**(a) The taxonomy/headedness/function separation is genuine and consistently maintained.** Many papers in this area conflate "X is a noun" with "X heads the phrase" with "X fills determiner function." This paper explicitly factors the questions (§2.1, §3.2, §7) and, unusually, shows that its own favoured headedness proposal is *not* a consequence of the taxonomic claim — a separate-D grammar with ordinary determinative heads derives the same benefits (Table 6, §7.2). This is exemplary analytical hygiene, and the fragment method (holding judgments and lexical restrictions fixed while varying taxonomy) makes the real locus of disagreement — inheritance through Noun vs. a cross-category projection rule — visible rather than obscured.

**(b) Fair-minded engagement with alternatives.** Table 1 locates the taxonomic space honestly; the discussions of Hudson (noting the inventory mismatch with *CGEL*), Van Eynde, Anderson, Postal and Sommerstein are accurate at the level of detail given, and the paper concedes where its evidence is underdetermined (coordination vs. nesting; fusion vs. ordinary Head).

**(c) Transparency of the supplements.** The matrix audit and corpus documentation are candid to a degree rarely seen: they document reporting discrepancies in the author's own prior study (Reynolds 2021), state that the recovered working matrix is unauthenticated, and repeatedly disclaim that the CGELBank counts test the taxonomies. This is the right epistemic posture, even if (see below) it leaves the empirical support for the main claim thinner than the framing suggests.

## 3. Substantive weaknesses

**(a) The fourfold coordinate architecture is a preference, not a result — and the paper's own framing overstates what remains.** The abstract and §5 concede that the fragment's judgments don't distinguish coordination from Hudson-style nesting (determinative within pronoun). The stated criterion for preferring coordination is that an intermediate category "would be useful if it collected generalizations otherwise repeated" (§5), but this is never tested: the paper never aligns the inventories and asks, concretely, which generalizations the intermediate node would capture or fail to capture. The pro-form gender discussion (Reynolds 2025) is offered as a shared generalization "spanning pronouns and determinatives" — but that is precisely the kind of generalization an intermediate category exists to state, and the paper doesn't say why a feature or cross-classification is preferable. Similarly, Reynolds 2021's feature-matrix result — which, per the audit, robustly recovers the determinative/pronoun partition — is *evidence for a sharp boundary between the two groups*, and hence at least prima facie support for nesting (or even for two primary categories). The paper's response ("even perfect recovery... is compatible with shared nounhood") is correct but incomplete: a boundary that strong between pronouns and determinatives, with no comparable boundary demonstrated between determinatives and the rest of the grammar, arguably predicts a two-level structure. The severity is moderate-to-high for the "preferably as a fourth coordinate subcategory" claim specifically; the weaker thesis (inclusion in Noun) survives.

**(b) The argument for the articles risks circularity, and the "defectiveness" test is asserted rather than applied.** §6 proposes three conditions for restricted membership (paradigmatic integration, participation in characteristic contrasts, missing uses as local restriction) — this is the right kind of test and I welcome it — but for the articles the paper concedes that the positive evidence supports only "determinative membership, rather than establishing nounhood independently" (§6). So the argument runs: articles are nouns because determinatives are nouns; determinatives are a coherent category partly because it includes the articles. The escape route offered — that no separate-article cluster of synchronic restrictions exists — is stated as a comparison concern but not established: the paper never systematically surveys whether *the/a/every* share restrictions (e.g., the *almost/nearly* modification facts divide *every* from the articles, as the paper itself notes) that would support Spinillo-style redistribution or a separate article category. The alternative deserves a section, not two sentences. This connects to a point where the paper under-credits and under-uses prior diagnostic work: my 2004 proposal was grounded in a systematic re-examination of the whole determiner class with explicit criteria, and my expansion of the article category was argued on distributional and morphological grounds the paper does not engage. The paper correctly reports my rejection of category-switching for demonstratives and *some/all* (§2.2) and correctly notes the disagreement about *every*, but it treats redistribution as a position to be located rather than as an argument to be answered. A related gap: my 2000 *AAA* paper, which raises the "get rid of the determiner class" question at the class level, is absent from the reference list entirely, though the present paper's framing ("determiner" as function vs. "determinative" as category) is close to terrain explored there. (I flag the 2000 point as source-dependent: I cannot verify its specific claims from the supplied materials, only its absence.)

**(c) The corpus and matrix evidence does no independent argumentative work, and the paper is not always clear about this.** The supplements candidly state that the CGELBank inventory "doesn't estimate productivity or test the competing taxonomies" and that the matrix "can't test their inclusion within a superordinate Noun category." Given that, the quantitative material functions mainly as documentation of examples the paper could have presented as constructed judgments. This is not fatal, but it means the paper's empirical base reduces to: (i) a handful of attested sentences plus constructed paradigms, (ii) *CGEL*'s own descriptions, and (iii) the pronoun-membership analogy. The analogy is doing most of the load-bearing work, and its strength depends on how closely determinatives' deviations from common nouns parallel pronouns' — a comparison sketched in Table 2 but never made rigorous. A moderate severity: the paper should either present the analogy as a systematically scored comparison (the Reynolds 2021 matrix, extended with common/proper nouns, would actually do this — and the audit shows the tooling exists) or temper claims that the "combined pattern supports" the extension (§4.6).

## 4. A key question for the author

You state that an intermediate pronoun-plus-determinative category "would be useful if it collected generalizations otherwise repeated" (§5). Can you give one concrete, independently motivated generalization over the aligned inventory that coordination captures but nesting repeats (or vice versa)? If not, on what non-stipulative grounds is coordination "provisionally favoured"?

## 5. Recommendation: **Revise and Resubmit**

The central idea — extending *CGEL*'s pronoun-membership rationale to determinatives, with taxonomy, headedness and function kept distinct — is worth pursuing and is here argued with unusual care about what the evidence does not show. But three repairs are required before the architecture claims are warranted: (i) a substantive, inventory-aligned comparison of coordination vs. nesting, or a downgrade of the fourfold claim to a stated convention; (ii) a systematic application of the §6 three-condition test to the articles against the redistribution alternative, engaging the diagnostic basis of the 2004 thesis (and ideally the 2000 paper); and (iii) either an extended matrix comparison including all four proposed subcategories, or explicit repositioning of the pronoun analogy as the argument's primary — and currently informal — engine. Optional extensions include corpus-based estimation of independent-use breadth across the full determinative inventory, and applying the fragment to predicative constructions, which the paper honestly excludes but which materially affect the common-noun comparison.
