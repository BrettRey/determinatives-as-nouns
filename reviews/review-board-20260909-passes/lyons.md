# Review: "Determinatives as nouns in English"

**Reviewer persona note:** This review is written from the perspective of a reader concerned with definiteness and article typology (cf. Lyons 1999). All judgments rest on the supplied article and two supplements; I flag where a source-dependent point cannot be verified from them.

## 1. Main claim

English determinatives belong within the lexical category Noun — preferably as a fourth coordinate subcategory alongside common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns — on the grounds that they share the same nominal projection, external distribution and structural organization that CGEL already uses to justify pronouns' inclusion, with ordinary (Head) headedness as a further separable proposal.

## 2. Strengths

**(a) Separation of the three questions.** The paper is unusually disciplined in distinguishing (i) lexical-category membership, (ii) position within Noun (coordinate vs. nested), and (iii) headedness (fusion vs. ordinary Head). The matched four-way fragment (§7.2, Tables 5–6) that holds judgments and lexical restrictions fixed while varying taxonomy and headedness is a genuine methodological contribution: it shows exactly what the data do and do not decide, and candidly concedes that the fragment cannot distinguish coordination from Hudson's nesting.

**(b) The restricted-member argument (§6).** The three-part standard for articles and *every* — paradigmatic integration, participation in the category's characteristic contrasts, and local restrictions — is the right kind of argument, and the *my*/*mine* precedent from CGEL is well chosen. The observation that a separate article category would need a cluster of restrictions *absent elsewhere in Noun and otherwise requiring repeated exceptions* states a falsifiable condition rather than an intuition.

**(c) Transparency of supporting materials.** The matrix audit reports a reporting discrepancy (232 vs. 155 features), an unseeded clustering procedure, and sensitivity of the published agreement figure, while explicitly denying that even perfect recovery of the determinative/pronoun partition would test superordinate Noun membership. The corpus supplement correctly notes that its 65 Det–Head tokens conflate distinct constructions and cannot estimate productivity. This is honest self-auditing, and it usefully insulates the grammatical argument from over-claiming.

## 3. Weaknesses

**(a) The English-only scope is asserted, not defended (severity: major).** The paper says "The claims concern synchronic English lexical categories" (§1) and never returns to justify the restriction. For a reader of the article typology, this is the crucial question. The Scandinavian suffixal articles (Swedish *boken*) and the bound/free article contrast in Balkan languages bear directly on whether "determinative" is a coherent cross-linguistic class and on what it would mean for such items to be "nouns." The paper cites Lyons (1999: 331–336) on grammaticalization but only as "background" (§6). Two distinct issues need separating: (i) is the analysis *defeasibly* English-specific because the argument is built entirely on CGEL's English descriptions and judgments (a defensible, principled reason), or (ii) does the D-noun analysis make predictions for article diachrony — e.g., whether articles can acquire independent/argument uses as they grammaticalize — that the author declines to draw? The paper's grammaticalization endpoint is arguably where the argument has most bite: if grammaticalization is a cline from demonstrative/numeral to article, the D-noun analysis implies that loss of independent use is loss of a use permission, not category change — a testable claim about transitional systems. I cannot verify from the supplied material what the Scandinavian or Balkan facts would show under the analysis; that is exactly why the paper should either engage them or state explicitly why synchronic English is the right laboratory and flag the typological consequence as future work. **Remedy:** add a short §6 or §8 subsection: (i) state the principled basis for the English restriction (the argument inherits CGEL's English-specific description), and (ii) sketch what the analysis predicts for bound vs. free articles under grammaticalization, without committing to a cross-linguistic defense.

**(b) The economy claim is under-argued relative to its central position in the paper (severity: major).** §7.1 presents the reduction of the determiner phrase-type inventory (Det: {DP, NP, PP} → {NP, PP}) as a gain, but §7.2 then shows that the *separate-D ordinary-Head* grammar achieves the same reduction. The paper concedes this, yet the conclusion still rests the final preference on "inheritance" extending CGEL's pronoun rationale. The inference at issue: from "CGEL includes pronouns in Noun because pronoun-headed phrases fill noun functions" to "determinatives should join them." But pronouns in CGEL *already head their own phrases in argument position without fusion complications* — whereas determinatives require the paper to concede (§7.3) that inclusion costs restating the modifier contrast at a subcategory boundary, and that the adjectival fusion construction (*the idle rich*) must be retained separately. The consistency argument ("Why should systematic nominal projection warrant inclusion for pronouns but require cross-category licensing for determinatives?") is therefore weaker than the pronoun case by exactly the amount of that additional cost. The paper states the cost honestly but never weighs it against the consistency gain in a way that a skeptic about inclusion (as opposed to a skeptic about headedness) must accept. **Remedy:** either (i) quantify the allocation-of-generalizations comparison more explicitly — e.g., enumerate which restrictions are stated once under inheritance versus repeated under the cross-category projection rule — or (ii) downgrade the taxonomic thesis to the level of confidence the fragment actually supports: the evidence decisively supports ordinary nominal projection for determinatives and only provisionally supports Noun membership over a separate-D ordinary-Head grammar. Note that the paper itself, in Table 6, shows the separate-D ordinary-Head account shares the reduced inventory and Head relations; the difference is purely where the modifier contrast is stated.

**(c) Single-judgment evidence in the matched fragment (severity: moderate).** The permissions table (Table 4) and the fragment's judgments rest on constructed intuitions for a deliberately minimal inventory (*the, a, every, some, few, my, she*), with predicative uses excluded. The corpus supplement shows Det–Head independence is attested for 65 tokens but explicitly disclaims productivity estimation, and reports zero bare *few*, *either* or *neither* tokens. The argument that independent use is "widespread across the inventory" (§5.1) is thus supported by CGEL's citation of forms plus two corpus attestations plus constructed examples. This is adequate for a taxonomy paper, but the paper's own standard — "the pattern recurs across lexemes and appropriate contexts" — would be better met by a systematic survey of the full CGEL determinative list (the Huddleston et al. 2021 footnote gives the inventory) against the permission schema, rather than a selected-form table. **Remedy (required if the breadth claim is retained):** report the permission profile for the full determinative inventory, even impressionistically; the corpus table generation machinery already exists in the supplements.

## 4. Key question for the author

If the D-noun analysis is correct, what does it predict about a language whose articles have grammaticalized further or less far than English — specifically, does loss of argument use (the article endpoint) follow from the same use-permission architecture, and would an intermediate English form (e.g., *every*, which you argue is integrated by quantificational connections despite lacking independence) be exactly the transitional case your account predicts? If yes, the English restriction looks principled and the account gains typological depth; if not, the restriction risks looking evasive.

## 5. Recommendation

**Revise and Resubmit.** The central idea — extending CGEL's pronoun-membership rationale to determinatives, with taxonomy and headedness cleanly separated — is worth pursuing and is presented with exemplary candor about what its evidence does not show. Required repairs: (i) an explicit, principled justification of the English-only scope with at least a sketch of the typological/diachronic consequences (weakness a); (ii) a more careful calibration of the taxonomic thesis to the demonstrated result, given that the fragment licenses a separate-D ordinary-Head competitor with the same economy (weakness b). Optional extensions: full-inventory permission survey (weakness c); alignment of the Reynolds 2021 matrix comparison with the aligned inventories the paper says would be needed to test nesting versus coordination. The strengths are real; the gaps are ones a focused revision can close without new theory.
