This is a simulated review from a Word Grammar perspective, not a statement of Richard Hudson’s views; assessments of the cited literature are limited to its presentation in the supplied manuscript.

The paper proposes that CGEL’s determinatives form a fourth subclass of Noun, coordinate with common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns, sharing nominal structure while retaining their distinctive lexical and constructional restrictions.

Three strengths deserve emphasis. First, “The nearest alternatives” treats the competing nominal analysis seriously. The acknowledgment that determiners “need not constitute another category node” correctly frames the comparison, as presented here, as one involving valency and inherited properties rather than merely alternative category names. Equally valuable is the explicit recognition that Hudson’s inventory and CGEL’s broader determinative inventory differ. This prevents a comparison between nominally identical but extensionally different classes.

Second, the manuscript now confronts the principal logical limitation of its quantitative evidence. “Coordinate subclasses and inheritance” states that distributional separation “would not show that the superclass is incoherent.” That is precisely the distinction needed when evaluating a proposal that nests determiners within pronouns. The matrix reproduction and sensitivity analysis are useful contributions because they distinguish evidence for differentiated profiles from evidence for a particular hierarchy. The admission that there are no common or proper nouns in the matrix further disciplines the interpretation.

Third, the worked analyses make the proposal assessable. “Two heads in one expression” distinguishes the modification of *every* from that of *teacher*, while “Count the restrictions as well as the labels” acknowledges that a phrase-type distinction “hasn’t disappeared from the grammar.” These are substantive improvements over an argument that treats fewer primary category labels as sufficient evidence of grammatical economy.

My weaknesses, ranked by impact, are these.

1. **The distinctive proposal remains a preference whose decisive comparison is deferred.** “Coordinate subclasses and inheritance” says, “Establishing either result requires a matched fragment of the competing grammars.” “Consequences for further comparison” then proposes that fragment as future work. Yet coordination is what distinguishes this paper from the nominal analysis it identifies as its nearest predecessor. Preservation of CGEL’s classification is an understandable practical advantage, but it does not establish that the retained intermediate distinctions organize English more effectively.

   A concrete alternative is to replace part of the repeated discussion of limitations with a small, explicit grammar comparison. Fix a shared inventory containing *the, every, some, few, she,* and *my*, together with common-noun controls. Analyse dependent determination, independent arguments, partitives, degree modification and adjectival modification. State each account’s inherited properties, valencies and exceptions, identifying conditions that must be repeated. A nested analysis should be allowed cross-classification and lexical restrictions on the same terms as the coordinate analysis. If the accounts prove equivalent over this fragment, make the paper’s central contribution an explicit nominal reconstruction of CGEL and present coordination as an implementation choice. That would remain worth publishing if the reconstruction delivers a demonstrable descriptive gain.

2. **The architectural comparison acknowledges mutual dependency without testing its consequences.** “The nearest alternatives” reports “mutual dependency between determiner and common noun” and construction-sensitive external heads. But the subsequent trees simply retain the common noun as ultimate head. The two modifier attachments in *almost every experienced teacher* establish distinct local relationships; they do not independently establish which element governs the expression’s external distribution.

   Work through the temporal-adjunct evidence already mentioned, alongside a construction that the cited account analyses as selecting the determiner externally. Explain how the proposed constituent structure captures both restrictions and whether doing so requires additional selection rules. The category/function distinction is a legitimate analytical commitment, but its availability does not settle this architectural comparison. Van Langendonck (1994), named in the review remit, may provide relevant context; its specific contribution requires source verification, and I do not treat its omission alone as a defect.

3. **The article treatment needs an actual licensing statement.** “The articles and other restricted members” says that an article’s “lexical entry restricts the available uses.” This is necessary, but currently schematic. Once *the* itself projects an NP, the analysis must distinguish that NP’s permissible embedding from its prohibited occurrence as an ordinary argument. The comparison with *my* shows that restricted nominal forms are possible; it does not establish that the restrictions are identical or cheaply inherited.

   Supply explicit entries or constructional conditions for *the, every,* and *some*. Show exactly what permits their determiner uses and what blocks the first two independently. This would make the claimed localization of restrictions testable rather than assured.

My key question is: **Which grammatical generalization becomes available because determinatives inherit directly from Noun that cannot be captured equally economically through a broader pronoun class with appropriate valencies?**

My principal objection would be overturned by a matched fragment showing that direct inheritance captures a recurring restriction while the nested account must duplicate it or introduce additional exceptions. A comparably explicit demonstration of economy against CGEL would also strengthen the paper’s contribution even if coordination remained undecided.

**Revise & Resubmit:** the nominal proposal is worth pursuing and the manuscript is unusually candid about its limits, but its distinctive hierarchy needs at least one completed comparative demonstration rather than a programme for obtaining one.
