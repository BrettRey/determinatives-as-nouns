**Review: "English determinatives as nouns" — recommendation: major revision**

**My account of the contribution.** The manuscript argues that the CGEL determinative category (articles, demonstratives, quantifiers) should be classified as a subclass of NOUN, coordinate with common noun, proper noun and pronoun, rather than as a separate primary category. It carefully separates three decisions — lexical classification, phrase headedness, grammatical function — and argues that independent uses ("I'll take *some*"), partitives ("*some of the wine*"), and pre-head modification ("*the lucky few*") motivate nominal projection for determinatives, while AdvP/AdjP modification asymmetries must be retained lexically. Two empirical exercises are added: (i) a reproduction of Reynolds (2021)'s determinative–pronoun feature matrix, showing that DISCO separation is robust but k-groups clustering agreement (published as 129/138) varies from 75–132 across random starts and depends on feature selection; (ii) a small CGELBank inventory (387 D tokens in 220 trees) with traceable identifiers. The paper explicitly declines to claim that coordination beats Hudson's nested pronoun analysis on the present evidence.

**Principal problem: the thesis dissolves under the paper's own hedges.** The most consequential passage is in the abstract: "The result is a case for a fourfold nominal analysis, with its empirical support distinguished from the stronger evidence needed to exclude competing nominal hierarchies" — echoed in §7: "Its strongest present claim is a coherent and descriptively motivated organization of the facts. A claim of unique empirical superiority over Hudson's hierarchy would require evidence beyond that supplied here." Since Hudson already puts determiners inside Noun, the paper's *distinctive* claim (coordination, not nesting) is precisely the one it concedes is undecided on its evidence. What remains is (a) an argument against CGEL's separate D category that is largely a restatement of Hudson's and Payne et al.'s position, and (b) an unquantified economy claim. Table 6 ("The proposed economies and their limits") itself concedes: "fewer primary categories and fewer determinative-specific structural applications don't mean fewer selectional distinctions." A reader is left asking what the paper establishes beyond CGEL that Hudson's 2004/2010 analyses do not. The section on articles is candid about this too: "The articles remain its most demanding test, rather than becoming positive evidence merely by being called defective" — but no positive case for *the*/*a* as nouns is then supplied beyond analogy with defective *my*.

**Secondary problems.**
1. *The quantitative section undercuts rather than supports the thesis.* Table 4's best-objective fits (125/138 public matrix, 97/138 with four labels removed) show the clustering result is fragile, which is a genuine finding — but a negative one about Reynolds (2021), and it does no positive work for coordination. The paper says so ("This result concerns the association between the coded profiles and the supplied partition. It doesn't say whether either group is a primary category"), which raises the question of why it occupies so much space.
2. *The corpus inventory is too sparse for its role.* The paper observes: "the sample has no D-token occurrence of bare *few*, *either* or *neither*," and that "*something reliable*" "isn't evidence for the specific premodification pattern in *the lucky few*." Honest, but it means the two constructions doing the heaviest argumentative work have no corpus support at all; the case rests on constructed judgments.
3. *Notation friction.* In Figure 1, *some* in "some apples" occupies an NP_D functioning as Det, containing a Nom headed by N_D — a three-level projection for a single function word, with "Nom" vacuous over one word. The tree is internally consistent but the cost of explicitness claimed in §3.2 ("a cost of making the analysis explicit") is never weighed against alternatives in the figures.
4. I cannot verify from the manuscript the precise page citations to Hudson (2004: 7–14), Sommerstein (1972: 197–203), Lyons (1968: 232–235, 279) or Payne, Huddleston & Pullum (2010: 13–14); these should be spot-checked. My assessment of Hudson's taxonomy and Payne et al.'s category/function discipline rests on my knowledge of that literature, not on anything in the submission.

**Concrete alternative for the principal problem.** Either (a) rescope the paper: drop the coordination claim and present it as an argument that determinatives are nouns (against CGEL), acknowledging Hudson as the closest precedent and positioning the matrix audit and CGELBank inventory as the novel contributions; or (b) do the comparison §7 says is needed: write out a matched fragment — a fixed list of ~15 constructions (independent uses, partitives, *the lucky few*, *almost every*, genitives, articles, *no/none*, floating quantifiers, numerals) — stated twice, once under coordinate inheritance and once under Hudson's nested pronoun-with-valency, and count rule statements and lexical exceptions under each. This is feasible, would give the "economy" claim content, and would genuinely discriminate the hierarchies. As submitted, option (a) is the minimal repair; option (b) is the better paper.

**Minor:** the abstract is too long and partly self-summarizing; Table 1's DP_CGEL/NP_D notation will burden readers; "(b)" in (6) contains the ungrammatical-looking "none have credibility" — if this is verbatim corpus text, flag it as such; "Draft, September 2026" date should be updated.
