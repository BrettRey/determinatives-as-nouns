**Simulated review — John Payne perspective (Journal of Linguistics).** I am not the real scholar and make no claim about his actual views; recollections of his published work are marked unverified.

**1. The claim.** The paper argues that CGEL's determinative category should be relocated as a fourth subclass of Noun, coordinate with common noun, proper noun and pronoun, so that independent *some* is an ordinary nominal head rather than a Det–Head fusion, while all determinative-specific licensing restrictions are retained at subclass and lexeme level.

**2. Strengths.**
(i) The three-way separation in §2 ("Classification, headedness and function") is executed with unusual discipline. "Headedness and category membership are separate questions" is stated in §1 and then actually honoured: the genitive tree (Figure 3) and the *almost every experienced teacher* tree (Figure 2) show that nounhood is not inferred from determiner function, and the paper explicitly disclaims the D-headed architecture.
(ii) The self-limitation is exemplary and rare. §5.1 reports that "the sample has no D-token occurrence of bare *few*, *either* or *neither*"; §5.2 reports the clustering instability against the author's own prior result; §8.1 concedes that "A phrase-type distinction has moved into the features of a shared category; it hasn't disappeared from the grammar." A reviewer does not have to extract these concessions.
(iii) §7 ("The articles and other restricted members") correctly identifies its own hardest case and refuses the circular move: "the articles can't be called nouns simply because the proposed noun subclass has been defined to include them."

**3. Weaknesses, ranked.**

**W1 (most serious). The paper removes Det–Head fusion while retaining fusion elsewhere, without engaging the developed statement of the apparatus.** Fusion is cited only from CGEL Ch. 5 (§2.2: "Fusion is a structural analysis of an occurrence, not a change in its lexical category"). My recollection — *unverified* — is that Payne, Huddleston & Pullum (2007) develops fusion of functions at article length, with a set of defining properties and coverage of Mod–Head as well as Det–Head cases; that treatment is where the reanalysis has to bite, and I do not see it engaged. The cost surfaces at the Mod–Head residue. §4.4 uses *the idle rich* only defensively — "adjectival fused heads can occur with a preceding adjective" — and Table 5 concedes "Fusion remains available elsewhere, including adjectival and genitive constructions." So the paper never states its own analysis of *the rich* / *the idle rich*, and the economy claim reduces to: fusion is dispensed with in one corner and kept in the corner that motivates it.

*Concrete alternative.* (a) Add a subsection auditing the fusion apparatus property by property against its dedicated statement, saying for each whether the fourfold analysis preserves, reassigns or loses it, and specifically why Det–Head goes while Mod–Head stays. (b) State the analysis of *the rich* explicitly. If *rich* keeps fused Mod–Head, say what principle blocks the parallel treatment of *few* in *the lucky few*; if *rich* is instead a converted noun, own that much larger commitment. A defensible answer either way is available; the current silence is what is not defensible.

**W2. The three discriminators do not discriminate, by the paper's own admission.** Paradigm productivity, structural saturation and internal modification are developed in §4.1–§4.4 (not, as a reader might expect from the roadmap, in one signposted place — they are never named together). Each is then withdrawn: "A closed class doesn't support the same inference about new lexical items as an open class" (§4.1); "the absence of an overt antecedent doesn't establish the absence of silent nominal structure" (§4.2); "the word order doesn't prove the attachment it represents" and "Internal adjectival modification is therefore not a categorical discriminator" (§4.4). The honest summary is the paper's own: "none selects coordination over nesting within Noun by itself." *Fix:* reframe them as adequacy constraints on any analysis, and add the missing positive work — a licensing profile for the full determinative inventory (AdjP premodification, AdvP degree modification, post-head PP/relative, partitive, bare argument), which would also test whether *the lucky few* is a productive Nom pattern or a lexicalised residue.

**W3. The economy comparison is deferred to the paper that would settle it.** §8.3 proposes "a useful next comparison would encode the same finite set of constructions under the coordinate and nested analyses" — that comparison is the argument, and a matched fragment over roughly a dozen determinatives and a fixed construction set, with stipulations counted, is feasible now rather than promised.

**4. Key question.** What is your analysis of *the idle rich*, and what independently motivated principle retains fusion there while removing it for independent *some*?

**5. Recommendation: Revise & Resubmit.** The thesis is worth pursuing and the argumentative hygiene is genuinely high, but the central reanalysis currently engages fusion only through a grammar's summary rather than through its defended formulation, and the claimed economy is not yet accounted.

**Reversal conditions.** My principal objection fails if the reference list already contains Payne, Huddleston & Pullum (2007) with substantive engagement I could not see in the rendered text; if the `analysis/` directory already contains the matched fragment; or if the author can show a Det–Head fusion analysis making a false prediction that ordinary nominal projection avoids — that single case would convert W1 into the paper's strongest section.
