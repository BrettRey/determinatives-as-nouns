# Review: "English determinatives as nouns" (Journal of Linguistics)

**(1) Claim.** The paper argues that the English determinative category should be reclassified as a fourth subclass of NOUN, coordinate with common noun, proper noun and pronoun, so that dependent and independent uses share ordinary nominal projection while all distributional restrictions survive at the subclass and lexeme levels.

**(2) Strengths.**

- The paper's analytical discipline is exemplary. §2 and Table 1 carefully separate *category*, *headedness* and *function*, and the author repeatedly resists overclaiming: "The fact that *some* can occupy an argument position doesn't itself prove this tree" (§4.2), and "a primary-category boundary... removed, while local licensing distinctions remain" (§9.3). The self-audit of Reynolds 2021 (§6.2, Table 4) — showing that k-groups agreement ranges from 75 to 132 of 138 depending on seeds and feature choice — is a model of reproducible scholarship, and the conclusion that "one percentage from an unseeded run can't bear the argument for a particular taxonomy" is exactly right.
- The economy accounting in §8 (Table 5) is honest in a way most categorial proposals are not: the author concedes that "A phrase-type distinction has moved into the features of a shared category; it hasn't disappeared from the grammar" (§8.1). This anticipates precisely the objection I would otherwise have to press in HPSG terms (see below), and I welcome it — but it also concedes most of what I think remains problematic.
- The corpus inventory (§6.1) with stable sentence identifiers, the explicit admission that "the sample has no D-token occurrence of bare *few*, *either* or *neither*", and the refusal to count *something reliable* as evidence for the *the lucky few* pattern, all keep the empirical record and the claims properly aligned.

**(3) Weaknesses, ranked by impact.**

*W1 (most serious): the disjunction re-emerges at the selection level, so the economy claim is thinner than advertised.* In §3.2 and Figure 1, the determiner of *some apples* is realized by an NP_D, and in §8.1 the author admits the Det function must still select "a determinative-headed NP, a suitable genitive NP, or another licensed expression." In an HPSG implementation this is a disjunction in the SELECT (or SPR, on the head-complement/specifier layout one adopts) value of the head noun — exactly the disjunction a uniform D category with typed determiner realizations was designed to collapse. The paper's uniformity claim for NP:Det is thus only as good as the typed feature system it never spells out. If determinative-headed NP, genitive NP, and PP-type determiners are separate types on a Det-realization hierarchy, the grammar has not unified determiner selection; it has renamed it. The paper knows this ("fewer primary categories and fewer determinative-specific structural applications don't mean fewer selectional distinctions," Abstract) but the economy argument in §8 never quantifies the trade-off against CGEL or against a typed-feature analysis.

*W2: the argument is avowedly English-only, and that scope undercuts the taxonomic conclusion.* "The claims concern synchronic English" (§1). But the question of whether the determiner word-class patterns with A or with N — the very question the paper's A-side alternative raises — has been answered differently cross-linguistically. My own work (Van Eynde 2003, unverified details from memory) split determinatives between adjective-like and noun-like subclasses on inflectional evidence, and the NP-internal agreement literature (Van Eynde 2007; also Italian and Dutch determiner agreement facts, which I recall but do not verify here) shows determiners inflecting like adjectives in gender/number paradigms. A paper that assigns *all* determinatives to NOUN while restricting evidence to a language with morphologically impoverished determiners needs at minimum a §10-style discussion of what the Italian or Dutch paradigms would do to the fourfold proposal. §9's treatment of *the* and *a* as "lexical gaps" is the right move for English, but defective paradigms are exactly where cross-linguistic inflectional evidence bites, and the paper is silent.

*W3: the rejection of any A-side alternative is by omission, not argument.* §2 surveys CGEL, Hudson, Postal and Sommerstein, but never states the hypothesis that determinatives form a class (or split classes) allied with adjectives, despite that being a live alternative with prior art. Since the paper's own §5.4 shows determinatives patterning with nouns on attributive modification (*the lucky few*) while patterning against them everywhere else, the A/N partition question deserves explicit engagement, not absence from Table 2.

**(4) Key question.** Can you give a concrete typed-feature specification of the Det-selection under your analysis — e.g., a Determiner-Realization type hierarchy under which determinative-headed NP and genitive NP are sisters — and show that the number of types and constraints is genuinely smaller than in a CGEL-style or D-style grammar? Without that, §8's Table 5 compares labels, not grammars.

**Concrete alternative for W1.** Rather than leaving "determinative-headed NP or a suitable genitive NP" as prose, introduce an inherited type *det-np* with constraints [HEAD.DET +], subtypes *det-d* (determinative-headed) and *det-gen* (genitive NP), and let the SPR value of common nouns be simply *det-np*. Then the paper's central claim becomes testable in HPSG terms: if the determinative/pronoun/common-noun restrictions can all be stated as constraints on subtypes of *det-np* and on the nominal projection the author already posits, the economy argument is made at the level where it can fail; if the constraints proliferate, the coordination-over-nesting question in §7 gets the "matched fragment of the competing grammars" the author himself says is required (§7, penultimate paragraph).

**(5) Recommendation: Revise & Resubmit.** The paper is careful, honest about its limits, and makes a genuinely interesting taxonomic proposal, but the central economy claim needs a formal implementation, and the English-only scope needs either cross-linguistic qualification or an explicit argument that the A/N-alternative evidence does not generalize — both achievable in revision, which is why I do not recommend rejection.
