# Review: "English determinatives as nouns"

## (1) Claim

The paper argues that the English determinative category (CGEL's D: *the, this, some, every, many*…) should be classified as a fourth subclass of Noun, coordinate with common noun, proper noun and pronoun, with nominal projection shared across subclasses and the determinative-specific restrictions retained at the subclass and lexeme levels.

## (2) Strengths

**a. Disciplined separation of category, headedness and function.** The paper's repeated insistence — "Headedness and category membership are separate questions" (§1), "a determinative can occur outside determiner function… Neither fact requires a category change on each occurrence" (§2.1) — is exactly the conceptual hygiene this literature needs, and it correctly diagnoses sloppy moves in both directions.

**b. Unusual epistemic honesty about its own evidence.** The audit of the determinative–pronoun matrix (§5.2) is exemplary: the author re-examines his own published result, shows that clustering agreement "depends on initialization and feature selection," and concedes the public file has 155 features where the article reports 232. Likewise §5.1 flags the missing D-tokens of *few, either, neither* and refuses to let *I need something reliable* stand in for *the lucky few*. This is a model of not overselling.

**c. The treatment of the articles (§7).** The paper does not pretend the hardest cases are easy: "The articles remain its most demanding test, rather than becoming positive evidence merely by being called defective." The *my/mine* parallel (§7) is a genuinely apt precedent for a determiner-use-only nominal, and the diachronic backstop — "Descent from a demonstrative or numeral doesn't guarantee that a modern article retains the ancestor's category" — is the right guard against a lazy etymological argument, and I am sympathetic to it (more below).

## (3) Weaknesses, ranked by impact

**W1 (most serious): The English-only scope is stated but not defended against the cross-linguistic picture it begs.** The paper says "The claims concern synchronic English" (§1) and nowhere else confronts the typology of articles. If "determinative" is a subclass of N**oun** because free-standing English articles pattern nominally, what does the proposal say about languages where the article is a bound morpheme? In the Scandinavian languages the definite article is a suffixal marker on the noun (*bord-et*, roughly 'table-the'); in the Balkan languages the article is postposed and cliticized (my recollection of the standard description; unverified against primary sources here). A suffixed article cannot head an NP in anyone's syntax, so either (i) the fourfold analysis is a language-particular accident of English word order and prosody — in which case the paper should say so and draw the typological consequence explicitly — or (ii) the paper is silent on a partition of its own category that any reviewer of *Definiteness*-adjacent work will raise in the first page. There is a well-known typological and diachronic split between free-standing/bound articles and between preposed/postposed ones (my recollection of the general literature; unverified). The paper gestures at diachrony only to defuse it ("Diachronic accounts… can't settle the synchronic category decision," §7), which is fair as far as it goes, but it never asks the constructive diachronic question: if English articles are grammaticalized demonstratives/numerals that have *decategorialized* (lost nominal distributions — my recollection of Hopper & Traugott-style grammaticalization; unverified), is "defective noun" the right endpoint, or has the article left the noun category altogether? The paper needs to argue that "restricted member of Noun" beats "emerged functional element" for *the/a*, not just that both are consistent.

To be clear: this is a problem with the *arguing*, not with whether the idea is worth pursuing. The claim is well worth pursuing; the scope limitation is currently evasive rather than principled.

**W2: The coordinate-vs-nested question is left genuinely open, which is honest but leaves the title overreaching.** §6 concedes that "Where the resulting grammars license the same expressions with equivalent conditions, the available examples don't decide between them" and that "A claim of unique empirical superiority over Hudson's hierarchy would require evidence beyond that supplied here." Given that, the abstract's "argues for treating the determinative category… as a subclass of Noun" promises more than §6 delivers: what is actually established is that a fourfold analysis is *coherent and economical relative to CGEL*. Either add the matched-fragment comparison the paper itself specifies (§6, §8.3) or retitle/rescope.

**W3: The economy argument is asserted more than measured.** §8's Table 6 is a good start, but "reduces the occasions on which that special relation must be invoked" (§8.1) is never quantified even for the worked fragment. The adjective-modification cost is acknowledged ("Some existing noun generalizations must be stated for common nouns… A responsible comparison counts such revisions," §8.2) but not counted. A table of rules revised under each analysis would make §8 falsifiable.

## (4) Key question

Does the paper claim that *free-standing* articles and determinatives are nominal while *bound* articles (Scandinavian suffixal, Balkan postposed) are not — and if so, what does that typological split predict about the category status of determinatives in languages that lack articles altogether?

## (5) Recommendation

**Revise & Resubmit**: the core analysis is careful and the epistemic standards are exemplary, but the paper must either defend the English-only scope against the free-standing/bound typology and the grammaticalization endpoint literature, or explicitly bound its claim as language-particular and say what the fourfold taxonomy predicts cross-linguistically.

**Concrete alternative for W1.** Add a section (after §7) that (i) distinguishes free-standing articles (English, French, German) from bound/suffixal articles (Scandinavian, Balkan) and article-less systems, citing the standard typological treatments (Lyons 1999 is an obvious one; I declare that interest and that my citing it is a recollection-based suggestion, unverified against the manuscript's bibliography); (ii) states explicitly whether the fourfold analysis is proposed as an English-particular description or as one instantiation of a typological parameter on where definiteness marking sits on the noun–affix continuum; and (iii) engages the decategorialization literature to argue why "defective member of Noun" is the correct synchronic endpoint for *the/a* rather than "emerged functional head." This would convert the scope restriction from a liability into a testable prediction.

**Reversal note.** My principal objection is the unhandled cross-linguistic picture. It would be overturned if the manuscript, or a planned companion, showed that the fourfold analysis extends naturally to bound-article languages (e.g., by treating the Scandinavian suffix as nominal morphology of the common noun, distinct from English free-standing determinative nouns) — in which case the English-only restriction would be principled, and the paper's case would be *strengthened* by the typology rather than threatened by it.
