*Simulated review from the requested CGEL-oriented perspective; this is not a statement of Geoffrey K. Pullum’s views. Source attributions are assessed only as presented in the supplied manuscript.*

**1. Claim**

The paper proposes that CGEL’s determinatives constitute a fourth subclass of Noun, sharing ordinary nominal projection with common nouns, proper nouns and pronouns while retaining their distinctive lexical and constructional restrictions.

**2. Strengths**

First, the paper understands the distinction on which a serious discussion must rest. “Headedness and category membership are separate questions” (“The question”) is exactly right. The trees make that distinction inspectable: classifying *some* as nominal does not displace *apples* as the ultimate head of *some apples*. Nor does the paper confuse CGEL’s determinative phrase with the DP hypothesis. This makes the proposal intelligible as a revision within an N-headed account.

Second, the treatment of fusion is substantially more careful than an argument that simply equates independent occurrence with nounhood. “Fusion is a structural analysis of an occurrence, not a change in its lexical category” (“The nearest alternatives”) identifies the relevant alternative fairly. The discussion of independent genitives also recognizes that nounhood and fusion are compatible. The author consequently avoids attributing to CGEL the absurd position that an independently occurring determinative must have changed lexical category.

Third, the empirical reporting is unusually candid. “Counting those labels can’t independently establish that the decisions are correct” (“A reproducible corpus inventory”) properly limits the use of CGELBank. The matrix audit distinguishes separation of supplied groups from evidence for their taxonomic rank, and exposes sensitivity to initialization and feature selection. These are useful contributions even if the grammatical proposal ultimately proves unpersuasive.

**3. Weaknesses, ranked by impact**

**1. The principal comparative argument remains conditional where it needs to become demonstrative.** In “Count the restrictions as well as the labels”, the decisive passage begins: “If those differences also specify the possible independent forms and dependents”. The following sentence then allows that fusion might predict additional restrictions. Those are the two possibilities the paper needs to compare; stating both does not establish an economy.

The problem is particularly sharp because the manuscript itself supplies the counterexample to an automatic connection between nominal classification and ordinary headedness: independent *mine* can remain a fused construction despite being nominal. Consequently, making D a subclass of Noun and removing Det–Head analyses are two decisions, neither of which entails the other. The economy table partly bundles them. A grammar could broaden Noun while retaining fusion, or permit D-headed NPs without making D a noun subclass.

A concrete repair would replace part of the repeated qualifications with a small explicit grammar comparison. Use the same lexical entries and judgments for *some apples*, independent *some*, *some of the wine*, *almost every teacher*, *the lucky few*, dependent *my* and independent *mine*. Compare at least three analyses: separate D with fusion; nominal D with fusion; nominal D with ordinary Head. State each licensing rule, each restriction on dependents, and each rule that actually disappears. Include unavailable counterparts, particularly independent *every*. This would establish whether the gain comes from taxonomy, from replacing fusion, or from both. No complete computational grammar is necessary.

**2. The article analysis lacks an independently stated licensing condition.** “Its lexical entry restricts the available uses” (“The articles and other restricted members”) describes where a solution might be written, but does not provide it. The proposal assigns *the* an NP projection in *the wine* while excluding that projection from ordinary argument positions. Precisely what does the selecting construction inspect: the head lexeme, determinative subclass, a dependent-only feature, or a restricted phrase type?

The analogy with dependent genitive *my* makes such restrictions conceivable; it does not establish that the article restriction has the same explanation. Likewise, the determination requirement of singular count common nouns must be distinguished from the distribution of a purported complete NP headed by an article. Write representative entries for *the*, *every* and *some*, and show how the same determiner construction licenses them while independent constructions distinguish them. Count those conditions in the comparison above. This is a request to finish the analysis, not a claim that articles cannot be nouns.

**3. The paper needs a positive grammatical result beyond compatibility.** Its careful conclusion repeatedly shows that the proposal can accommodate familiar facts. Yet preserving common-noun headedness is also a constraint on the alternatives, not an advantage supplied by the new taxonomy. In particular, the count, number and common/proper restrictions mentioned for articles never receive the worked treatment given to modification.

Add a compact comparison showing how determiner selection reaches the relevant properties of the head nominal once both the determining expression and the containing expression are NPs. Then identify one resulting generalization that broader Noun genuinely supports. This would sharpen the extension beyond the shared N-headed architecture.

**4. Key question and reversal condition**

What independently motivated rule becomes simpler specifically because determinatives are nouns, once fusion and phrase licensing are held apart? A matched fragment showing a genuine reduction without compensating restrictions would overturn my principal objection.

**5. Recommendation**

**Revise & Resubmit:** the proposal is worth pursuing and is presented responsibly, but publication requires the explicit comparative fragment currently deferred to future work.
